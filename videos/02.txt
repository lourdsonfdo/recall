RCP 202 — Chapter 02: Therapeutic Hypothermia

[02-001 · What this chapter covers]
Therapeutic hypothermia, now usually called targeted temperature management, is careful control of body temperature to protect the brain from secondary injury after cardiac arrest. In this chapter we cover the current targets from the instructor's TH slides and how they differ from older numbers, the evidence behind cooling, how cooling protects the brain, why it is not routine in traumatic brain injury, the three phases of cooling, shivering, cold diuresis, the potassium and glucose swings, the ABG at thirty seven degrees, and the respiratory therapist's ventilator targets.

[02-002 · TTM today: who, how cold, how long]
The instructor's slides give the current numbers, marked as twenty twenty five AHA updates. Who: adults who remain unresponsive after return of spontaneous circulation. Temperature: select and keep a constant temperature between thirty two and thirty seven point five degrees Celsius. How long: at least thirty six hours. Alongside it, avoid hypotension, using fluids and vasopressors to a mean arterial pressure of at least sixty five, individualized to the patient, and avoid hypoxemia, excess oxygen, and large PaCO2 swings. The primary goal is to protect the brain from secondary injury. Modern practice stresses controlled temperature and fever prevention, not cooling every patient to thirty two.

[02-003 · Which temperature number to use]
You will see several temperature ranges, so match the number to the question. For a current practice question, use the slide text: thirty two to thirty seven point five degrees for at least thirty six hours. If the question names the Hypothermia After Cardiac Arrest trial, answer thirty two to thirty four degrees for twenty four hours. The RCP 105 slide also prints thirty two to thirty four for TH. The older TTM range is thirty two to thirty six degrees. The RCP 105 notes give that range, and Hess adds at least twenty four hours. Hess defines hypothermia as a core temperature of thirty five degrees or less.

[02-004 · The evidence: the 2002 cooling trial]
The landmark Hypothermia After Cardiac Arrest trial in two thousand two enrolled two hundred seventy five patients resuscitated from ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia arrest. The cooled group was held at thirty two to thirty four degrees for twenty four hours. Survival was sixty four percent versus fifty percent. Favorable neurologic outcome was forty seven percent versus thirty one percent, and the notes call that sixteen point difference the most important finding. Favorable means Cerebral Performance Category one or two. Separately, in early studies, the benefit was clearest in ventricular fibrillation; the PEA and asystole groups were small and less conclusive.

[02-005 · HIE: three causes and the RT's role]
Hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy is damage to the brain and spinal cord from severe cerebral hypoxia. The deck names three causes. One, inadequate ventilation and oxygenation, such as respiratory arrest or carbon monoxide poisoning. Two, inadequate perfusion, such as cardiac arrest or shock. Three, a fall in cerebral perfusion pressure from a low mean arterial pressure or a high intracranial pressure. Treatments listed include oxygenation, ventilation and perfusion, seizure control, hyperbaric oxygen, and therapeutic hypothermia. Routine hyperbaric oxygen for HIE is still being researched. After return of circulation, the respiratory therapist maintains oxygenation and ventilation and supports temperature management while avoiding hypotension and hypoxemia.

[02-006 · The ischemic cell injury cascade]
When cerebral blood flow stops, neuronal oxygen stores are used up in about twenty seconds and the patient loses consciousness. Within five minutes of complete anoxia, brain glucose and ATP are gone. Then the sodium potassium pump fails and the neuronal membrane depolarizes. Depolarized neurons release excess glutamate, the brain's primary excitatory neurotransmitter. Glutamate opens channels that let calcium flood in, which switches on destructive enzymes and nitric oxide synthase. Chloride and sodium follow, osmotic edema forms, and the cell dies. The instructor's one line version is energy failure, calcium overload, neuronal death, and the glutamate step is the concept worth knowing.

[02-007 · How cooling protects the brain]
The TH slides list how cooling may protect the brain. It suppresses inflammatory mediators. It reduces free radical production and helps mitochondria. It may reduce caspase activation, which limits apoptosis. It stabilizes neuron membranes and may reduce glutamate release and calcium influx while lowering the brain's oxygen demand. It may protect the blood brain barrier and reduce edema. It prevents fever, which the notes call the most clinically important row, because fever raises metabolic demand and lowers the seizure threshold. Brain metabolism falls about five to seven percent for each degree of cooling. The deck stresses these are proposed mechanisms, not proof of better outcomes.

[02-008 · Necrosis, apoptosis, and injury after ROSC]
An ischemic cell can recover, die by necrosis, or enter apoptosis. Necrosis is uncontrolled death from severe injury, with membrane rupture and inflammation. Apoptosis is programmed cell death. It can happen hours or days later and adds to delayed brain damage, but the cells shrink and are cleared without the inflammation of necrosis. Brain injury does not stop when circulation returns. The slides describe post resuscitation encephalopathy: ischemia and cell injury lead to loss of autoregulation and microcirculatory dysfunction, creating an oxygen supply and demand mismatch. With autoregulation impaired, brain flow follows systemic blood pressure, so hypotension after resuscitation worsens ischemia.

[02-009 · Autoregulation and CPP]
Cerebral autoregulation keeps brain blood flow steady across a wide range of blood pressures by constricting and dilating cerebral vessels. That is why not everyone with a blood pressure of ninety over sixty becomes ischemic. Autoregulation can be lost in severe hypotension or head trauma, and then flow depends on blood pressure and intracranial pressure. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. For the HIE deck, normal CPP is seventy to eighty with a critical threshold of seventy, and normal ICP is eight to twelve with twenty as critical. Mortality rises about twenty percent for every ten millimeters of mercury drop in CPP. For trauma questions, the goal is CPP above sixty.

[02-010 · Quick check: CPP math]
A head injured patient has a mean arterial pressure of ninety. The intracranial pressure rises from ten to thirty. Compute the cerebral perfusion pressure before and after, and decide whether each value meets the trauma goal and the HIE deck's critical threshold. Pause and work it out.
[6-second practice pause]

[02-011 · Answer: CPP math]
Before, ninety minus ten is eighty, which meets both the trauma goal of above sixty and the HIE threshold of seventy. After, ninety minus thirty is sixty. The trauma goal is greater than sixty, so sixty no longer meets it, and it is well below the HIE threshold of seventy. The intracranial pressure of thirty is also above the critical value of twenty. Raise the mean arterial pressure, lower the intracranial pressure, or both. With mortality rising about twenty percent per ten millimeters of mercury drop, this fall from eighty to sixty matters.

[02-012 · Why TH is not routine in brain trauma]
Cardiac arrest is a global loss of brain blood flow. Traumatic brain injury is a direct mechanical injury, often regional, with edema, raised intracranial pressure, and inflammation. Induced hypothermia may lower intracranial pressure, but it has not consistently improved neurologic outcome in brain trauma, so routine prophylactic hypothermia is not recommended. Fever prevention and adequate perfusion still matter. A large multicenter trial of three hundred ninety two patients with severe brain injury found no significant improvement in survival or neurologic outcome, and more complications. If a brain injured patient is cooled, rewarm slowly, because rapid rewarming can bring instability and rebound intracranial hypertension. The teaching pearl: lowering a number does not mean better recovery. Hess calls TH likely beneficial in adult brain trauma, but the instructor's deck says not routine, so use the deck.

[02-013 · The three phases of TTM]
Temperature management runs in three phases. Induction: activate the protocol, document the neurologic exam before sedation if possible, start sedation early, begin continuous core temperature and cardiac monitoring, start the cooling method, and treat shivering early. Maintenance: hold a stable core temperature for at least thirty six hours, with fluctuation no more than about zero point two to zero point five degrees, a suggestion rather than a universal rule. Monitor electrolytes, glucose, and especially potassium, check the skin under cooling devices, and use EEG when seizures are suspected. Rewarming: a slow, controlled return toward normal temperature, about zero point two to zero point five degrees per hour, following your protocol.

[02-014 · Rewarming risks and shivering]
Rewarming is not just turning the device off. Vasodilation can cause hypotension and instability, potassium rises, and glucose can fall. Overshoot is temperature rising above the target, a rebound hyperthermia. Afterdrop is a further fall in core temperature as cold peripheral blood returns. Both are why rewarming is gradual, and fever is avoided for seventy two hours after return of circulation. Shivering occurs in up to forty percent of patients. It slows cooling and raises oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production. Treat it with sedation, anti shivering drugs, and skin counter warming. Monitored neuromuscular blockade is the last resort.

[02-015 · Cold diuresis]
Cold diuresis is increased urine output during cooling. The slide gives the cause as increased renal blood flow during cooling. The notes add the chain: cooling causes peripheral vasoconstriction, which pushes blood to the core. The heart senses the larger venous return as overload, so atrial natriuretic peptide is released and antidiuretic hormone falls. The kidneys then make large volumes of dilute urine. The consequences are loss of fluid, potassium, magnesium, and phosphate, and hypovolemia with less preload and cardiac output. Monitor volume status and electrolytes, and treat the losses.

[02-016 · Cooling versus rewarming: K and glucose]
The slides contrast the two phases. During cooling, potassium shifts into the cells and cold diuresis wastes it, so hypokalemia develops, along with low magnesium and phosphate. Cooling also causes insulin resistance and hyperglycemia. During rewarming the shifts reverse. Potassium moves back out of the cells, risking rebound hyperkalemia and dysrhythmias, and insulin sensitivity returns, so hypoglycemia may develop. That is why potassium is replaced carefully during cooling. Give potassium chloride by mouth or slowly IV, never undiluted, less than forty milliequivalents in one hour and less than two hundred in twenty four hours. Replacement stops several hours before rewarming, and potassium is reassessed. Most protocols keep glucose at eighty to one hundred eighty. For general potassium normals, use three point eight to five point zero milliequivalents per liter.

[02-017 · Other adverse effects of cooling]
Cooling affects nearly every system. Cardiovascular: bradycardia is expected, a heart rate of forty five to fifty five at thirty three degrees, with higher vascular resistance and possible arrhythmias, especially with low potassium or magnesium. The ECG can show Osborn waves. Hess warns that if the temperature drops below the desired range, arrhythmias and coagulopathy follow; serious ventricular arrhythmias go with deeper cooling and low potassium or magnesium. Hematologic: platelets work less well. Clotting also slows, but lab clotting tests run at thirty seven degrees and can look near normal while the patient bleeds. Immune: infection can develop without fever. Gut and liver: slow emptying and slower drug clearance, so sedatives last longer. Respiratory: less carbon dioxide production and weaker cough and secretion clearance, raising pneumonia risk.

[02-018 · Glasgow Coma Scale]
The Glasgow Coma Scale adds three parts: eye opening up to four, verbal up to five, and motor up to six. The maximum is fifteen and the minimum is three, and lower means worse. The instructor stresses that motor response best predicts neurologic outcome. Use mild thirteen to fifteen, moderate nine to twelve, and severe three to eight. A score of eight or less prompts you to consider intubation for airway protection, a guideline rather than an absolute law. In intubated patients verbal cannot be tested, so the range is two T to ten T. Heavy sedation or paralysis can lower the score without severe brain injury.

[02-019 · ABG and temperature: the physics]
The blood gas analyzer always measures at thirty seven degrees, whatever the patient's temperature. Gas solubility is inversely related to temperature, so colder blood holds more dissolved gas. In a cooled patient, the real PaO2 and PaCO2 are lower and the pH is higher than the thirty seven degree printout, so the patient looks alkalotic. Warm patients show the opposite. The slide's advice is to understand the direction, not memorize the numbers. Oxygen content does not change, so a low corrected PaO2 is not true hypoxemia. Cooling also shifts the oxyhemoglobin curve to the left.

[02-020 · Temperature correction formulas]
For every degree below thirty seven, the TH slides give pH plus zero point zero one five, PaCO2 minus two, and PaO2 minus five. Written as formulas: corrected PaO2 equals measured PaO2 minus five times thirty seven minus the patient's temperature. Corrected PaCO2 uses two, and corrected pH adds zero point zero one five times the same difference. Hess's example: a patient at thirty three degrees with a printout of pH seven point four, PaCO2 forty, and PaO2 ninety. The difference is four. PaCO2 becomes thirty two, pH becomes seven point four six, and PaO2 becomes seventy, close to Hess's sixty eight.

[02-021 · Correct the ABG or not?]
The sources disagree on whether to temperature correct. The TH slides say correction is not automatically required: follow the prescribed blood gas strategy. Alpha stat uses the uncorrected thirty seven degree values, and pH stat uses temperature corrected values. The RCP 105 Monitoring deck says correct to the core temperature, and RCP 203 says always document the patient's temperature at sampling. Hess says correction is unnecessary and that any corrected values must be labeled, with the thirty seven degree values also reported. For the instructor's exam, give the slide's answer, follow the prescribed strategy, and know the per degree corrections.

[02-022 · Ventilator targets during TH]
The deck's chain is: lower temperature, lower metabolism, lower carbon dioxide production, changed ABG interpretation, and ventilator changes. If minute ventilation stays the same while the patient cools, PaCO2 falls, and cerebral vasoconstriction cuts brain blood flow. Adjust often using ABGs and end tidal CO2 trends. During rewarming carbon dioxide production rises again, so minute ventilation may need to go back up. The slide targets are PaCO2 thirty five to forty five, PaO2 sixty to one hundred five after return of circulation, and mean arterial pressure at least sixty five. The older notes list thirty five to forty and eighty to one hundred fifty.

[02-023 · PaCO2, ICP, and hyperventilation]
A higher PaCO2 dilates cerebral vessels and raises intracranial pressure. A lower PaCO2 constricts them, lowering pressure but also blood flow. Keep PaCO2 normal, thirty five to forty five. Routine hyperventilation is no longer recommended because too much vasoconstriction can worsen brain ischemia. Temporary hyperventilation to about thirty to thirty five is only for an emergency rise in intracranial pressure or impending herniation. The RCP 105 slide allows PaCO2 as low as twenty six in the first twenty four hours, with no added benefit below that. For the brain injured patient, also sedate to prevent pressure spikes, keep PaO2 above sixty, and suction only when needed.

[02-024 · RT cautions during cooling]
Several equipment and monitoring cautions come with a cooled patient. Do not use a heat and moisture exchanger in a hypothermic patient, because it reduces the exhaled humidity available. Hess says to set the ventilator humidifier to the patient's target core temperature, while the TH slide notes say humidified oxygen, not heated circuits; the deck does not reconcile these, so match the wording of the question. Cooling and sedation blunt cough and secretion clearance, so plan airway clearance and watch for pneumonia without fever. Handle the airway gently, since suctioning and arterial punctures can bleed. Transcutaneous oxygen accuracy is affected, and weaning may be delayed.

[02-025 · Quick check: ABG direction]
A patient cooled to thirty two degrees has an ABG run at thirty seven that shows a PaCO2 of forty five. In which direction are the patient's real PaCO2 and pH compared with the printout, and roughly by how much? Pause and answer.
[6-second practice pause]

[02-026 · Answer: ABG direction]
Colder blood holds more dissolved gas, so the patient's real PaCO2 is lower than the printout and the real pH is higher. The difference from thirty seven is five degrees. PaCO2 falls by two per degree, so forty five minus ten gives about thirty five. The pH rises by zero point zero one five per degree, so about zero point zero seven five higher. The patient looks more alkalotic than the printout suggests.

[02-027 · Quick check: rewarming labs]
Your patient has been cooled for thirty six hours and was given potassium for hypokalemia during cooling. Rewarming is about to start. Which way do potassium and glucose tend to move during rewarming, and what does that mean for potassium replacement? Pause and answer.
[6-second practice pause]

[02-028 · Answer: rewarming labs]
During rewarming, potassium shifts back out of the cells, so it can rise into rebound hyperkalemia with dysrhythmias, which is why replacement during cooling is done carefully. Insulin sensitivity returns, so glucose can fall into hypoglycemia. That is why potassium replacement is stopped several hours before rewarming, potassium is reassessed before and during rewarming, and glucose is checked every one to two hours while rewarming.

[02-029 · Recap: therapeutic hypothermia]
To recap. Current practice is a constant thirty two to thirty seven point five degrees for at least thirty six hours in adults unresponsive after return of circulation, with a mean arterial pressure of at least sixty five and fever prevention. The trial used thirty two to thirty four for twenty four hours. Cooling lowers brain metabolism and may blunt the injury cascade, but it is not routine in brain trauma. Cooling brings cold diuresis, low potassium, and high glucose; rewarming brings rebound high potassium and low glucose, so rewarm slowly. The analyzer reads at thirty seven, so interpret the gases with temperature in mind. Keep PaCO2 thirty five to forty five and adjust ventilation as the patient cools.
