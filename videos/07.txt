RCP 202 — Chapter 07: GI System: Routes of Nutrition Delivery

[07-001 · Why nutrition belongs in a respiratory course]
Welcome to section seven, the GI system and routes of nutrition delivery. Nutrition is a respiratory topic because the diaphragm is a muscle, and a starved or overfed patient may struggle to wean. Hess chapter eleven names only three routes of nutrition: oral, enteral, and parenteral, in that order of preference. In this lesson we will walk through each route, the enteral tube choices, when parenteral nutrition is actually indicated, aspiration precautions during tube feeding, and two high-yield metabolic traps: overfeeding with its respiratory quotient and refeeding syndrome with hypophosphatemia.

[07-002 · The three routes and the order of preference]
Oral is the preferred method of nutrient delivery. Enteral, in Hess chapter eleven, means feeding-tube delivery through the gut when the patient cannot chew or swallow. Parenteral means any route other than the alimentary canal, essentially an IV, and it is reserved for when the GI tract is not functioning, or when stimulating it would make things worse. Oral often falls short in lung disease because of dyspnea while eating, early satiety, reflux, bloating from air swallowing, and side effects of bronchodilators, steroids, and antibiotics. The rule from Hess: if oral intake falls short despite high-calorie, high-protein supplements and snacks, enteral feeding by tube should be considered. Enteral is also the method of choice for ventilated patients.

[07-003 · Enteral tubes: short term vs long term]
Enteral access splits by duration. Nasogastric and orogastric tubes go through the nose or mouth into the stomach, and are used short term, less than three to four weeks. The Dobhoff is a small-bore flexible NG tube, about four millimeters inside diameter, placed over a stylet with a weighted end that peristalsis carries into the stomach. It is usually more comfortable than other NG tubes. NG tubes may be contraindicated in severe reflux, gastroparesis, or high aspiration risk, and Hess chapter thirty-nine warns they can impair the cough reflex. Percutaneous tubes, such as a PEG placed endoscopically in a sedated patient, go through the skin into the stomach or small intestine for long-term feeding, and can be replaced without another endoscopy. Comfort order: PEG is more comfortable than nasal tubes, and Dobhoff beats other NG tubes.

[07-004 · Post-pyloric feeding and confirming position]
A post-pyloric or small-bowel tube goes past the stomach into the duodenum or jejunum, optimally past the ligament of Treitz. It is chosen when aspiration risk is high or when gastric emptying is poor; the more distal the feeding, presumably the less likely aspiration. A larger gastric tube can decompress the stomach while the post-pyloric tube delivers feed. Confirming position is an RT concern: the tip and side hole must sit beyond the gastroesophageal junction, and an improperly positioned feeding tube can enter the airway, so radiographic confirmation is essential before use. Hess adds that a colorimetric CO2 detector or capnography can catch accidental tracheal placement; a color change means the tube is in the trachea.

[07-005 · Parenteral nutrition: indications and access]
Parenteral nutrition is reserved for when the GI tract is not functioning, or when stimulating the gut or pancreas would worsen the patient. Hess lists severe pancreatitis, GI fistulas, short bowel syndrome, prolonged ileus, and some cancers. It should not be started if support is expected to last less than seven days. Access needs a central or peripheral venous catheter, and Hess says central access is usually preferred. The RCP 203 deck explains the two lines: a peripheral line is a short catheter in a small vein for short-term fluids or non-irritating medications, while a central line sits in a large vein such as the jugular, subclavian, or femoral, or an arm PICC. It is designed for longer-term use and lists IV nutrition among its uses.

[07-006 · Prompt: why does TPN still carry risks?]
Pause and think. Even when parenteral nutrition is clearly indicated, it carries risks. What happens to the gut when we bypass it for days, and what carbon dioxide and electrolyte problems should the RT watch for when TPN starts?
[6-second practice pause]

[07-007 · Answer: gut atrophy, CO2 overload, low phosphate]
Bypassing the gut leaves it unstimulated, which can lead to mucosal atrophy, weakens the gut barrier, and may raise the risk of bacterial contamination. On the respiratory side, dextrose-heavy TPN raises oxygen use and CO2 production, and respiratory acidosis during mechanical ventilation has been reported within hours after starting TPN from overfeeding. Hess chapter five adds that parenteral nutrition with carbohydrate overload drives phosphate into cells and is a named cause of hypophosphatemia. So the RT watches ventilation, blood gas, and serum phosphate when TPN starts.

[07-008 · Enteral vs parenteral: the clean contrast]
Enteral uses a functioning gut, so nutrients travel through the portal system to the liver, which may enhance immune competence, maintain mucosal integrity, preserve normal flora, and stimulate secretory IgA. It is safer and considerably less expensive, though not risk-free. Parenteral bypasses the gut, so the gut barrier weakens and infection risk rises, and it costs more. Hess's key point is simple: enteral is preferred because it carries fewer infection risks, may protect the GI mucosa, and costs less. Hess chapter thirty-nine adds that trauma patients receiving enteral nutrition for five to seven days had lower sepsis rates than those given IV fluids.

[07-009 · When to start: the critical-care rules]
Hess clinical practice guideline eleven-dash-one says to start early enteral nutrition within twenty-four to forty-eight hours in a critically ill patient who cannot maintain volitional intake. Use enteral over parenteral. In most critically ill patients it is acceptable to start enteral in the stomach. Withhold enteral during hemodynamic instability until the patient is fully resuscitated. For high-risk or severely malnourished patients, advance toward goal over twenty-four to forty-eight hours while watching for refeeding syndrome, aiming for more than eighty percent of goal energy and protein within forty-eight to seventy-two hours. Protein target: one point two to two point zero grams per kilogram actual body weight per day, possibly higher in burn or multiple trauma. Energy target when indirect calorimetry is unavailable: twenty-five to thirty kilocalories per kilogram per day.

[07-010 · Aspiration precautions: head of bed first]
In all intubated ICU patients receiving enteral nutrition, elevate the head of the bed thirty to forty-five degrees. Hess chapter thirty-six's ventilator bundle says up to forty-five degrees, especially during enteral feeding. The RCP one-oh-five Procedures deck notes that studies disagree on the exact angle, and that elevation should be done unless contraindicated, for example after neurosurgery or in severe hypotension. Beyond the head of bed, divert feeding post-pyloric in high-risk patients, switch from bolus to continuous infusion for intolerant patients, and consider prokinetics such as metoclopramide or erythromycin when aspiration risk is high. Do not use blue food coloring or glucose oxidase strips as markers of aspiration. Gastric residual volumes should not be used routinely, and feeds should not be held for residuals under five hundred milliliters without other signs of intolerance. Before postural drainage, stop tube feeds one hour before and during the therapy.

[07-011 · Respiratory quotient and overfeeding]
The respiratory quotient, RQ, equals VCO2 divided by VO2, the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed. A measured RQ should fall in the physiologic range of zero point six seven to one point three; critically ill patients sit near zero point nine zero. Pure carbohydrate burns at an RQ of one point zero, protein near zero point eight, fat near zero point seven one, alcohol zero point six seven. Higher RQ means more CO2 produced per unit of oxygen used. Overfeeding carbohydrate drives lipogenesis and extra CO2, which raises ventilatory demand and can precipitate respiratory failure or prolong weaning in a patient with limited reserve.

[07-012 · Fat-based formulas: deck vs CPG conflict]
The decks favor fat-based nutrition for ventilated and CO2-retaining patients: fat supplies more kilocalories with less CO2, and the diet should contain at least forty percent of its kilocalories from fat. Hess's pulmonary-disease formulas give forty to fifty-five percent of calories from fat and less than forty percent from carbohydrate, at one point five kilocalories per milliliter. But Hess's clinical practice guideline eleven-dash-one suggests NOT using specialty high-fat, low-carbohydrate formulas in ICU patients with acute respiratory failure, and suggests fluid-restricted, energy-dense formulas instead, especially with volume overload. The concept the decks test is that more carbohydrate means a higher RQ and more CO2, so avoid overfeeding. If a question asks what the current ICU guideline recommends, answer with the CPG.

[07-013 · Undernutrition weakens the breathing pump]
Starvation and hypercatabolism cannibalize the diaphragm. Protein-calorie malnutrition hits fast-twitch fibers hardest and shows up as a drop in maximum inspiratory and expiratory pressures, vital capacity, and voluntary ventilation. A malnourished patient may fail a spontaneous breathing trial simply because the diaphragm lacks the strength to sustain breathing. Malnutrition also impairs cell-mediated immunity and surfactant production, and low albumin raises the risk of pulmonary edema. Nutritional risk flags are weight loss of ten percent or more over six months, or body weight less than eighty percent of ideal. The hypercatabolic phase of critical illness can last seven to ten days, and caloric needs may rise by up to one hundred percent; burns may need one and a half to two times, sometimes two to three times, normal calories.

[07-014 · Prompt: what goes wrong when feeding restarts?]
Pause and think. A severely malnourished patient has just been started on nutrition support. A cluster of problems can then appear that may precipitate respiratory failure or prolong ventilation. What is this syndrome called, and which electrolyte does this section tie most closely to it?
[6-second practice pause]

[07-015 · Answer: refeeding syndrome and phosphate]
The syndrome is refeeding syndrome, the metabolic disturbance that follows reinstituting nutrition in a starved or malnourished patient. It brings severe electrolyte imbalance, vitamin deficiency, and hyperglycemia, and can cause arrhythmias, heart failure, pulmonary edema, respiratory failure, diaphragm and intercostal weakness, and a rise in VCO2. The electrolyte this section focuses on is phosphate; hypophosphatemia decreases ATP and impairs respiratory-muscle function. The Management of MV deck defines hypophosphatemia as serum phosphate less than one milligram per deciliter, while Hess chapter five gives the adult normal range as two point seven to four point five milligrams per deciliter, so by Hess any value under two point seven is already low. Advance severely malnourished patients toward goal over twenty-four to forty-eight hours, and in acute respiratory failure monitor serum phosphate closely and replace it when needed.

[07-016 · Recap: nutrition and the ventilated patient]
Three routes, in order: oral, enteral, parenteral. Enteral tubes split by duration, NG and Dobhoff short term, PEG long term, and post-pyloric when aspiration risk is high. Parenteral is reserved for a non-functioning gut or when stimulating it would cause harm, and uses a central line when possible. Head of bed thirty to forty-five degrees during feeding. Overfeeding carbohydrate raises the RQ and CO2 and may prolong ventilation; the decks favor fat-based formulas, but the current CPG advises against specialty high-fat formulas in acute respiratory failure. Malnutrition weakens the diaphragm; restarting nutrition in a starved patient can trigger refeeding syndrome with hypophosphatemia. Nutrition affects weaning.
