RCP 203 — Chapter 04: Lung Sounds: All of Them, and What Causes Each

[04-001 · What this chapter covers]
This chapter covers every lung sound on the study guide and what causes each one. We start with the three normal breath sounds and the rule that a normal sound in the wrong place is abnormal. Then we cover diminished and absent sounds, fine and coarse crackles, wheezes, rhonchi and stridor, the pleural friction rub, grunting, and the voice sounds. We finish by putting the sounds together with the other chest findings, condition by condition, so you can tell a pneumothorax from an effusion from atelectasis.

[04-002 · Normal breath sounds and their locations]
A breath sound is normal only at its proper location, and the deck says to learn the location more than the description. Vesicular sounds belong over the peripheral lung fields. They are soft and low pitched, and inspiration lasts longer than expiration. Bronchovesicular sounds belong over the main bronchi, at the upper sternum and between the scapulae. They are moderate in pitch and intensity, with inspiration and expiration about equally long. Bronchial sounds, which Hess calls bronchial or tracheal, or tubular, belong over the trachea. They are loud and high pitched, and expiration is as long as or slightly longer than inspiration.

[04-003 · Bronchial sounds in the wrong place]
The same sound can be normal or abnormal depending on where you hear it. Bronchial breath sounds out in the lung periphery mean denser lung is carrying tracheal sound outward. They suggest consolidation or atelectasis. Consolidated lung fills with fluid, mucus, pus or cellular debris, and that dense tissue transmits the large airway sound to the chest wall, where normally you would hear only soft vesicular sounds. One conflict to know: a Hess figure description calls vesicular sounds high pitched and breezy, but Hess Table one dash two and the RCP 103 deck both say low pitched. Use low pitched, from the deck.

[04-004 · Diminished and absent breath sounds]
Breath sounds get quieter when less air moves, or when sound is blocked from reaching the chest wall. The deck's causes are shallow or slow breathing, obstructed airways or hyperinflated lungs as in asthma and COPD, air or fluid in the pleural space, and obesity. Hess sorts them by pattern. Diffusely diminished sounds come from shallow breathing, or from hyperinflation with low airflow, as in emphysema and acute asthma. Localized decreases come from airway obstruction, atelectasis or main stem intubation, and decreased sounds at the bases are common with postoperative atelectasis. Unilateral decreases point to bronchial intubation, pneumothorax, atelectasis or pleural effusion. And in asthma, a silent chest means airflow is so low the wheeze disappears. It is never improvement. It is a late, critical warning that respiratory arrest is imminent.

[04-005 · Crackles: fine versus coarse]
Adventitious sounds are extra sounds laid over the normal breath sounds: rhonchi, wheezes, crackles, which are also called rales, and stridor. RCP 103 calls rhonchi an outdated term, but RCP 203 chapter two still lists it, and crackles is the preferred modern term. Crackles are discontinuous, popping sounds. Fine crackles are high pitched, come at the end of inspiration, and are heard most often over the bases. They come from terminal airways or fluid filled or collapsed alveoli popping open. Coarse crackles come from excessive airway secretions. Hess describes them as air moving through fluid in the large airways, usually cleared by suctioning or coughing.

[04-006 · Where crackles show up]
Fine crackles are the deck's hallmark sign of pulmonary edema, and Hess adds that crackles climbing higher up the lung fields mean worsening heart failure. They also appear in ARDS, in atelectasis, and in interstitial lung disease, where small airways reopen in stiff lungs. Hess describes bibasilar, Velcro-like crackles in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. In pneumonia you hear crackles over the involved lobe, and infants with respiratory distress syndrome or pneumonia have fine crackles. Coarse crackles appear in the decks' bronchiectasis, COPD and pulmonary edema cases. And coarse crackles heard over the trachea mean retained secretions, which is an indication to suction.

[04-007 · Wheezes, rhonchi and stridor]
A wheeze is continuous, musical and whistling, mainly expiratory, though Hess says it can be heard throughout the cycle. It comes from narrowed or obstructed airways: smooth muscle constriction, wall edema or mucus. Think asthma, COPD and bronchiectasis, and Hess adds heart failure and foreign body aspiration, where a child may have a wheeze over one isolated area. Rhonchi are deep, low pitched and rumbling, more on expiration. They come from secretions in the larger airways and may clear with coughing. Hess adds airway spasm or tumor. Stridor is a crowing, high pitched, inspiratory sound from upper airway obstruction, as in croup, post-extubation laryngeal edema and tracheomalacia.

[04-008 · Think location: bronchi or upper airway]
The location of the sound points to the treatment. Wheezing comes from the bronchi, so it calls for a bronchodilator. Stridor comes from swelling in the upper airway, so it calls for racemic epinephrine, which RCP 105 mentions after bronchoscopy. In a severe asthma attack, the wheeze becomes both inspiratory and expiratory, called biphasic. RCP 203 Lesson 6b gives a clean contrast. Wheezes are high pitched and musical, usually from narrowed small airways. Rhonchi are low pitched and snoring-like, usually from larger airways with secretions, and they may clear or change after a cough.

[04-009 · Practice: a sound after extubation]
Pause and work this out. Shortly after extubation, your patient develops a high pitched, crowing sound that you hear on inspiration. What is the sound, where is it coming from, and what does the deck give for it?
[6-second practice pause]

[04-010 · Answer: stridor, upper airway, racemic epi]
That is stridor. A crowing, high pitched, inspiratory sound means upper airway obstruction, and post-extubation laryngeal edema is one of its listed causes. Because the problem is swelling in the upper airway, the deck pairs stridor with racemic epinephrine. Compare it with a wheeze, which is musical, mainly expiratory, comes from narrowed bronchi, and calls for a bronchodilator instead.

[04-011 · Friction rub, grunting and voice sounds]
A pleural friction rub comes from inflamed pleural surfaces that no longer glide smoothly. Hess describes a grating sound, like two pieces of leather rubbing together, localized to the irritated area, and it may be intermittent. It is heard occasionally with pleural effusion, with pulmonary embolism when the infarction reaches the pleura, with interstitial lung disease, pleural infection, and after thoracic surgery. Grunting is heard in newborns in respiratory distress, who close the glottis to maintain lung volume. Voice sounds get louder and clearer over consolidation, because dense tissue transmits sound better. In bronchophony, ninety nine is clearly heard. In egophony, an e sounds like an a. In whispered pectoriloquy, a whispered one, two, three is clearly heard. In atelectasis, whispered pectoriloquy is diminished or absent.

[04-012 · Heuer's table: early versus late crackles]
The study guide fills one gap from Heuer's table two dash sixteen. Early inspiratory crackles are scanty, transmitted to the mouth and not affected by cough. They come from the sudden opening of atelectatic bronchi, in bronchitis, emphysema and asthma. Late inspiratory crackles are diffuse and fine, appearing first in the dependent regions. They come from the sudden opening of collapsed peripheral airways, in atelectasis, pneumonia, pulmonary edema and fibrosis. Heuer also describes a pleural friction rub as creaking or grating, mainly on inhalation, from pleurisy. This is Heuer's version, and the instructor's slide forty seven may differ. The slide's own tables are images, and no source describes monophonic versus polyphonic wheezes, so check the textbook pages the slide cites.

[04-013 · By condition: asthma, consolidation, air and fluid]
Now put the sounds together with the other findings. Asthma and COPD give wheezing, diminished sounds and a prolonged expiration, with hyperresonance. Consolidation and pneumonia give bronchial sounds, crackles and whispered pectoriloquy, with increased fremitus and a dull percussion note. A pneumothorax gives absent breath sounds, with the trachea pushed away and hyperresonance. A pleural effusion gives absent or diminished sounds, and occasionally a rub, with the trachea pushed away, a dull note, and decreased or absent fremitus. Notice that the pneumothorax and the effusion both push the trachea away. The percussion note separates them: hyperresonant air versus dull fluid.

[04-014 · By condition: atelectasis, obstruction, edema]
Atelectasis gives absent or diminished sounds, bronchial in some cases, and crackles, with the trachea pulled toward the affected side and a dull note. Upper airway obstruction gives stridor. Pulmonary edema gives crackles, which the deck notes are called rales, and wheezes, and the RCP 203 notes list grunting, gurgling or wheezing sounds among its symptoms. Hess Table one dash three adds two more. Emphysema gives decreased or absent sounds with no adventitious sounds, and pulmonary fibrosis gives vesicular sounds with crackles. The key contrast to remember: atelectasis pulls the trachea toward the lesion, while a pneumothorax or a large effusion pushes it away.

[04-015 · Practice: name the condition]
One more. On one side of the chest, breath sounds are absent, the percussion note is hyperresonant, and the trachea is shifted away from that side. Which condition is this, and which finding rules out its closest look-alike?
[6-second practice pause]

[04-016 · Answer: pneumothorax]
This is a pneumothorax. Absent sounds and a trachea pushed away also fit a large pleural effusion, but an effusion is dull to percussion, because it is fluid. Hyperresonance means air, so the percussion note decides it. Atelectasis is ruled out too, because it pulls the trachea toward the affected side.

[04-017 · Recap]
Here is what to take into the exam. Learn the location of each normal sound, because bronchial sounds in the periphery mean consolidation or atelectasis. A silent chest in asthma is a warning, not improvement. Fine crackles mean alveoli popping open, the hallmark of pulmonary edema, and coarse crackles mean secretions. Wheezes come from the bronchi and call for a bronchodilator; stridor comes from the upper airway and calls for racemic epinephrine. Voice sounds get louder over consolidation. And by condition, use the trachea and the percussion note: a pneumothorax is hyperresonant, an effusion is dull, and atelectasis pulls the trachea toward itself.
