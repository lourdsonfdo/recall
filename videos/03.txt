RCP 202 — Chapter 03: Communication

[03-001 · What this chapter covers]
Communication sounds soft, but it is testable and it shapes every bedside outcome. This chapter walks through four pieces. First, the difference between closed and open questions, and the interview strategy of starting broad and narrowing down. Second, the five levels of communication, from cliches to peak intimacy, and where the therapeutic relationship actually lives. Third, proxemics, the four distance zones, and why respiratory therapists live in the intimate space of their patients. And fourth, the three V's, which remind us that most of the message is not in the words.

[03-002 · Closed versus open questions]
Closed questions produce short specific answers, often yes or no or a single number. They are useful when you need information quickly, especially from a patient in respiratory distress. Open questions are thought provoking and invite the patient to tell their story, surfacing things you did not know to ask. Both are appropriate, and the respiratory therapist's skill is knowing when to use each. Hess adds that open questions are the most valuable form during the initial assessment and almost always begin with what, why, or how. Avoid leading questions, which suggest the answer you want, and compound questions, where a yes leaves you asking yes to what.

[03-003 · Interview strategy: broad to narrow]
The teaching sequence is start broad, then narrow. Begin with an open question like tell me about your cough. Then move to focused closed questions: when did it start, is it productive, what color is the sputum. Clarify anything ambiguous. Avoid leading and compound questions. Use silence and facilitation to let the patient fill the space. Finally summarize to confirm. If you notice an inconsistency, the deck calls this respectfully identifying inconsistencies; Hess calls it confrontation. The spirit is the same: honestly point out a mismatch, for example you said your breathing was fine but I noticed your respiratory rate was quite high.

[03-004 · The five levels of communication]
The deck numbers levels from five most superficial down to one deepest. Level five is cliche conversation: how are you, fine. Level four is reporting facts, where most routine healthcare talk happens. Level three is personal ideas or judgments: I don't think these treatments are helping me. Level two is feelings and emotions: I am scared I will end up on the vent again. Level one is peak communication, intimate, reserved for close personal relationships. The therapeutic sweet spot is levels two and three. The chain to remember is rapport, trust, cooperation. Patients may self disclose at level two; the therapist responds therapeutically, not reciprocally.

[03-005 · Proxemics: four distance zones]
Proxemics is how distance shapes communication. The deck lists four zones, in feet. Intimate is zero to one and a half, personal is one and a half to four, social is four to twelve, and public is twelve to twenty five. The respiratory therapist works inside the intimate zone constantly: suctioning, auscultating, placing a BiPAP mask, drawing an ABG, performing trach care. That is a space patients normally reserve for people very close to them. The skill is what you do before you enter: explain and ask, I am going to listen to your lungs, is that okay? These are conceptual zones that vary with culture, relationship, and condition.

[03-006 · The three V's]
The three V's split the message. Verbal, the words, is about seven percent. Vocal, meaning tone, volume, pitch, and rate, is thirty eight percent. Visual, meaning facial expression, gestures, posture, and body language, is fifty five percent. So about ninety three percent of the message is nonverbal. When verbal and nonverbal signals conflict, people lean on vocal and visual cues, especially for feelings and attitudes. The caveat: Mehrabian's percentages describe the communication of feelings when signals disagree. The numbers do not apply to a command like change PEEP to ten. Memorize seven thirty eight fifty five, and know it is an overgeneralization.

[03-007 · Quick check: question type]
A nurse hands you a patient who is working to breathe and cannot speak in full sentences. You need information fast. Which questioning type does the deck recommend as the primary tool at this moment, and name two question types to avoid in any interview.
[6-second practice pause]

[03-008 · Answer: question type]
Use closed questions. The deck specifically says closed questions are extremely useful when you need specific information quickly, especially with a patient in respiratory distress. The two question types to avoid are leading questions, which suggest the answer you want, and compound questions, which ask several things at once so you cannot tell what the yes applies to.

[03-009 · Quick check: proxemics]
You are placing a BiPAP mask on a new admit. Name the distance zone you are operating in, and name the percent of your communication that is now nonverbal according to the three V's model. Pause and answer before the next slide.
[6-second practice pause]

[03-010 · Answer: proxemics]
You are inside the intimate zone, zero to one and a half feet, a space the patient normally saves for close relationships. By the three V's, about ninety three percent of your message is nonverbal, split as thirty eight percent vocal and fifty five percent visual. So your tone, your face, and how you handle the mask matter more than the words. Explain, ask permission, and keep your face and voice calm.

[03-011 · Recap: communication]
To recap. Open questions to start, closed questions to focus, avoid leading and compound questions, and use silence to listen. The five levels run from cliche down to peak; aim for the therapeutic sweet spot of feelings and personal ideas. Proxemics has four zones: intimate, personal, social, public, with boundaries at one and a half, four, twelve, and twenty five feet, and respiratory therapists live in the intimate zone. The three V's are seven, thirty eight, fifty five, so most of the message is tone and body language. Ask permission before you enter and keep verbal, vocal, and visual congruent.
