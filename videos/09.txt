RCP 202 — Chapter 09: Trauma

[09-001 · Trauma: airway, bleeding, and brain]
Welcome to section nine, trauma. The exam here is wide. We will build it on the primary survey, ABCDE, and then focus on the respiratory high-yields: pneumothorax recognition and treatment, chest tubes, the Glasgow Coma Scale, the MAP minus ICP relationship that drives cerebral perfusion, imaging choices, blunt versus penetrating injury, head-injury priorities, and postoperative pulmonary embolism. A running theme for the RT: avoid hypoxemia, avoid excessive swings in PaCO2, and help prevent secondary brain injury.

[09-002 · Pneumothorax: what it is]
The Trauma deck defines a pneumothorax as air in the pleural space that can cause the lung to collapse. It is one of the most common and potentially lethal injuries in chest trauma: about twenty percent of patients who reach a trauma center alive have one. There are three subtypes: simple or closed, open, and tension. The chest-tube deck lists these causes: positive-pressure ventilation, a ruptured bleb in emphysema, a bronchopleural fistula, a leaking subpleural cyst, and chest trauma. Iatrogenic causes are thoracentesis, central vein and pulmonary artery catheterization, and bronchoscopy with transbronchial biopsy.

[09-003 · Pneumothorax at the bedside]
Bedside findings: chest pain and respiratory distress with diminished or absent breath sounds on the affected side. Percussion is hyperresonant, fremitus is decreased, and expansion is decreased on one side. Subcutaneous emphysema, the crackling crepitus under the skin, means an air leak but does not by itself confirm a pneumothorax. Tracheal deviation away from the affected side is classic but late and unreliable, so do not wait for it. Hypotension with respiratory distress and unilateral diminished breath sounds should raise concern for tension. A chest x-ray confirms it in a stable patient. On the ventilator, a pneumothorax raises both peak and plateau pressures as compliance drops.

[09-004 · Simple vs open vs tension]
Simple pneumothorax: air collects without progressive pressure buildup; small ones may resolve on their own, and larger ones may need a chest tube. Open, the sucking chest wound: a chest-wall defect lets air move with each breath; place a sterile occlusive dressing, often taped on three sides, then a chest tube and surgical closure. Tension: air enters but cannot escape, pressure builds, the mediastinum may shift, venous return and cardiac output fall, and obstructive shock or cardiac arrest can follow. When tension is suspected with hemodynamic instability, decompress immediately without waiting for a chest x-ray, then place a definitive chest tube.

[09-005 · Needle decompression landmarks]
Needle decompression is lifesaving but temporary; the definitive treatment is a chest tube. A fourteen-gauge catheter is commonly taught, with size, length, and site following current protocols. The traditional site is the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line. Adult trauma protocols often prefer the fourth to fifth intercostal space anterior to the midaxillary region because catheter failure rates are lower. Hess adds that about thirty percent of trauma patients have a chest wall thicker than five centimeters. The RCP 105 deck calls untreated tension pneumothorax the only absolute physiologic contraindication to positive-pressure ventilation, so decompress before or at the same time as starting PPV.

[09-006 · Glasgow Coma Scale: E plus V plus M]
The GCS provides the most objective assessment of consciousness. It adds eye, verbal, and motor scores: eye opening up to four, verbal response up to five, motor response up to six, giving a maximum of fifteen and a minimum of three. The 203 deck calls three deep coma, and Hess says it suggests brain death. Severity bands: mild thirteen to fifteen, moderate nine to twelve, severe eight or less. One RCP 105 slide prints thirteen to fourteen and less than eight, but use thirteen to fifteen and eight or less. Motor response is the best predictor of neurologic outcome. In an intubated patient, verbal cannot be assessed, so the score runs from two-T to ten-T.

[09-007 · Prompt: score this trauma patient]
Pause and think. A trauma patient opens eyes to pain, makes incomprehensible sounds, and withdraws from pain. Add it up. What is the overall score, what severity band is it, and what single airway decision does the deck tie to that number?
[6-second practice pause]

[09-008 · Answer: GCS 8, intubate for protection]
Eye to pain is two, incomprehensible sounds is two, withdrawal is four. Two plus two plus four equals eight. Eight sits at the top of the severe band, and the deck's rule is GCS of eight or less means intubate. We intubate not because the patient cannot breathe but because the patient cannot protect the airway, cough, handle secretions, or guard against aspiration. Remember the exceptions: intoxication, post-ictal states, and heavy sedation can give a temporary low GCS without severe brain injury, so clinical judgment still rules.

[09-009 · MAP, ICP, and cerebral perfusion]
Mean arterial pressure is the systemic driving pressure. The 203 deck writes it as diastolic plus one-third of systolic minus diastolic. For a blood pressure of one-ten over seventy, that is seventy plus thirteen point three, about eighty-three. Intracranial pressure is the pressure inside the rigid skull. The Monro-Kellie doctrine says the total volume of brain, CSF, and venous blood must stay constant, so if blood is added, CSF and venous blood must leave or ICP rises; Hess adds that once compensation is used up, ICP rises exponentially. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals MAP minus ICP. If MAP falls or ICP rises, CPP falls, cerebral blood flow drops, and ischemia can follow.

[09-010 · CPP targets and what moves ICP]
The Trauma deck goal is CPP above sixty millimeters of mercury, reached by lowering ICP, raising MAP, or both, and Hess agrees. The RCP 105 decks call seventy to eighty normal and seventy their critical threshold; for a trauma question, use sixty, but recognize seventy if an answer choice uses it. Normal ICP differs between decks; RCP 105 gives eight to twelve. Every deck that gives a number agrees ICP above twenty is abnormal and needs treatment. What raises ICP: edema, hemorrhage, high intrathoracic pressure from PEEP or recruitment, hypercapnia, hypoxia, Trendelenburg, neck flexion, suctioning, and asynchrony. What lowers it: head elevation, less CSF, and hyperventilation; Hess adds mannitol.

[09-011 · CT vs MRI: when and why]
CT uses x-rays from multiple angles to build cross-sectional slices, with gray levels in Hounsfield units, and it uses ionizing radiation. The Trauma deck calls chest CT the best modality for the hemodynamically stable patient with a chest injury; it finds injuries a chest x-ray misses, such as small pneumothoraces and vascular injury. Unstable patients should not have lifesaving treatment delayed for CT. Hess adds that non-contrast head CT characterizes most early intracranial lesions. MRI uses a strong magnetic field and radio-frequency pulses on hydrogen nuclei, with no ionizing radiation. Diffuse axonal injury is hard to see on CT and may require MRI. MRI limits monitoring and needs compatible equipment and aluminum oxygen cylinders, because steel cylinders become projectiles.

[09-012 · Chest tubes: indications, size, site]
Place a chest tube any time air or fluid in the pleural space keeps the lung from fully expanding. The RCP 105 indications are a large tension pneumothorax over twenty-five percent, hemothorax, pleural effusion, empyema, and chylothorax. Match size to contents: for an adult pneumothorax, sixteen to twenty French may be adequate, while blood or thick fluid gets about thirty-six French, range twenty-eight to forty, to reduce clotting. Think of a milkshake versus a water straw. Large adults take thirty-six to forty French. The safe triangle is the fourth to fifth intercostal space at the anterior-to-midaxillary line. Air rises, so aim the tube up toward the apex; fluid falls, so aim it down and posterior. Insert over the top of the rib, because the intercostal vein, artery, and nerve run along its lower border.

[09-013 · The three-chamber drainage system]
Three chambers, three jobs. One, collection, collects and measures drainage. Two, the water seal holds about two centimeters of water as a one-way valve, so air leaves but cannot return. Three, suction control: the water level, not the wall vacuum, sets the suction; the RCP 105 deck recommends minus ten to minus twenty centimeters of water. Gentle continuous bubbling in the suction chamber is normal; vigorous bubbling only means wall suction is too high. Tidaling in the water seal, rising on spontaneous inspiration and falling on expiration, means the system still communicates with the pleural space. Intermittent bubbling with exhalation or cough is an air leak, expected while a pneumothorax is evacuating; continuous bubbling means a large leak or a leak in the system.

[09-014 · Troubleshooting and removal]
Unexpected loss of tidaling suggests a kinked or blocked tube: check for kinks and dependent loops, milk the tubing, and if that fails, check the patient and notify the physician. For continuous bubbling, clamp briefly near the chest wall. If bubbling stops, the leak is likely from the patient; get a chest x-ray as ordered and notify the physician. If it continues, move the clamp toward the collection system to isolate the leak; if it never stops, replace the system. Never leave a tube clamped with an ongoing air leak. If drainage holes show, the tube is partly out: notify the physician and never push it back in. Tube pulled out: occlusive dressing, firm pressure, call the physician. Removal criteria: drainage under one hundred milliliters in twenty-four hours, pneumothorax resolved on chest x-ray, and no air leak. For transport, keep the unit upright and below the chest, and never clamp routinely.

[09-015 · Blunt vs penetrating trauma]
Blunt trauma: an external force injures internal structures without necessarily breaking the skin. Motor vehicle crashes dominate; typical injuries are rib fractures, pulmonary contusions, pneumothorax, blunt cardiac injury, and aortic injury from rapid deceleration, commonly at the isthmus just distal to the left subclavian. Large diaphragm tears are diagnosed more often on the left. Fractures of ribs one and two, the scapula, or sternum signal great force. Penetrating trauma: an object enters the chest. Low-velocity stab wounds damage along the path; high-velocity rifle wounds, over fifteen hundred feet per second, cause extensive damage through energy transfer and cavitation. Cardiac tamponade and esophageal injury are most often penetrating. A blunt-trauma patient may look stable at first, then worsen as contusions evolve.

[09-016 · Primary survey and the other lethal injuries]
The primary survey is ABCDE, ordered by rapidity and lethality: airway with cervical-spine protection, breathing, circulation with hemorrhage control, disability using GCS and pupils, and exposure while preventing hypothermia. The secondary survey is a head-to-toe exam with an AMPLE history once the patient is stabilized. Other lethal chest injuries: massive hemothorax, more than fifteen hundred milliliters of pleural blood, treated with chest tube drainage, blood products, and bleeding control; Hess adds that more than two hundred milliliters an hour for two to four hours points to emergent thoracotomy; cardiac tamponade, with Beck's triad of hypotension, JVD, and muffled heart sounds, though not always all three, treated by emergency pericardial decompression; and flail chest, three or more ribs fractured in two or more places. Pulmonary contusion may worsen over the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours.

[09-017 · Head injury: lesions and secondary injury]
Epidural hematoma sits between dura and skull, is often arterial, and may show a lucid interval before sudden deterioration, though not in every patient; Hess adds it is biconvex on CT, often from the middle meningeal artery. Subdural hematoma sits between dura and arachnoid, usually from torn bridging veins, is more common, and Hess says the underlying injury is usually worse. Subarachnoid blood can trigger vasospasm. Diffuse axonal injury comes from shearing forces and may need MRI. To prevent secondary injury, watch for hypoxia, seen in twenty-two percent of TBI patients. Hess adds that SpO2 at or below ninety percent for a median twelve to twenty minutes independently predicted death, and a single systolic under ninety is linked to doubled mortality.

[09-018 · Ventilation and osmotic therapy in TBI]
Outside herniation, the goal is normocapnia, a PaCO2 around thirty-five to forty-five. Hyperventilation lowers PaCO2, constricts cerebral vessels, and temporarily drops ICP, but it also drops cerebral blood flow and can cause ischemia. The Trauma deck reserves it for impending herniation in severe TBI, to a PaCO2 below twenty-five, following protocol. The RCP 105 deck instead gives a twenty-six floor and a temporary thirty to thirty-five; for this exam, use the Trauma deck numbers. Mannitol pulls water out of the brain but, as a diuretic, may worsen hypotension; hypertonic saline draws water out and supports circulating volume. A new unilateral fixed, dilated pupil is a neurosurgical emergency.

[09-019 · Prompt: what drug class actually dissolves clot?]
Pause and think. A postoperative patient develops sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, and hypoxemia. CT pulmonary angiography confirms a pulmonary embolus. The physician asks for a drug that will dissolve the clot already lodged in the pulmonary artery. Which drug class is it, and what single lab value defines therapeutic anticoagulation for ongoing prevention?
[6-second practice pause]

[09-020 · Answer: thrombolytic dissolves; PTT 2–2.5x]
Only thrombolytics dissolve existing clot; the common agent is tissue plasminogen activator, or tPA, and its bleeding risk must be weighed in a postoperative patient. Anticoagulants such as heparin, warfarin, or apixaban prevent further clot formation but do not dissolve what is already there. Anticoagulation is considered therapeutic when the PTT is two to two point five times the control. The management order: prophylaxis with intermittent pneumatic compression and early ambulation; anticoagulation; thrombolytics to dissolve clot; and an inferior vena cava filter to keep leg clots from reaching the lungs. Diagnosis is a V/Q scan showing perfusion defects with normal ventilation, or CT pulmonary angiography with contrast; the revised deck calls sudden dyspnea the most important sign. Virchow's triad explains the cause: stasis, vascular injury, and increased coagulability.

[09-021 · Recap: the trauma essentials]
ABCDE first. Pneumothorax: diminished or absent breath sounds and hyperresonance; the trachea shifts away, but late. Suspected tension with instability gets decompression before a chest x-ray. GCS equals E plus V plus M; eight or less and we think airway. CPP equals MAP minus ICP; keep it above sixty in trauma and avoid secondary injury from hypoxia, hypotension, hypercapnia, or high intrathoracic pressure. Chest tubes: match size to contents, insert over the rib in the safe triangle, water depth sets suction, tidaling means still connected. Postoperative PE: anticoagulant prevents, thrombolytic dissolves, PTT two to two point five times control.
