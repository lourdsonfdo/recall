RCP 203 — Chapter 02: Pharmacology Calculations

[02-001 · What this chapter covers]
This chapter is the pharmacology calculations handout. We start with units and conversions, then the proportion method that solves almost every dose problem, including dosing by milligrams per kilogram. Next we turn percentage-strength solutions into milligrams per milliliter, and then into a dose or a volume, with the albuterol and racemic epinephrine problems the deck uses. We cover why the amount of diluent does not change the dose, and finish with ratio solutions such as one to one thousand, and with diluting a stock solution. Write every calculation out. The deck is clear that patient safety matters more than mental math.

[02-002 · Units and conversions]
Most drugs are expressed in milligrams or micrograms. The metric units are the meter or centimeter for length, the liter or milliliter for volume, and the gram or milligram for mass. A milliliter and a cc are exactly the same volume, so three milliliters is three cc. Milli means one thousandth of the base unit, so one thousand milligrams is one gram. To convert, move the decimal three places: four thousand five hundred milligrams is four point five grams. The household measures are on the handout too. One teaspoon is five milliliters, or sixty drops. One tablespoon is fifteen milliliters, three teaspoons, or one hundred eighty drops. One cup is two hundred forty milliliters, eight fluid ounces.

[02-003 · The proportion method and mg/kg dosing]
First convert everything to consistent units. Then set up the proportion: the known dose over the known amount equals the desired dose over the desired amount. Cross multiply and solve. The deck also writes it as the original dose per amount equals the desired dose per amount. A dosage schedule in milligrams per kilogram takes two steps. Step one, calculate the dose needed by multiplying the milligrams per kilogram by the patient's weight. Step two, calculate how much of the preparation to give, using the same proportion. The deck's own worked milligram per kilogram examples are images on the paper handout, so check the handout for those.

[02-004 · mg/kg in practice: caffeine and surfactant]
The study guide works one example of its own, applying the deck's rule. Caffeine citrate has a loading dose of twenty milligrams per kilogram and a maintenance dose of five milligrams per kilogram. For a one point five kilogram infant, twenty times one point five is thirty milligrams to load, and five times one point five is seven point five milligrams daily. Other weight-based doses appear in the decks. Beractant is four milligrams per kilogram of birth weight. Poractant is two point five milliliters per kilogram, with a maximum total of five milliliters per kilogram. Notice that poractant is dosed by volume, not by milligrams. The deck's rule is to write the calculation out every time.

[02-005 · Percent strength: % × 10 = mg/mL]
A percent means grams per one hundred milliliters. One percent is one gram in one hundred milliliters, which is one thousand milligrams in one hundred milliliters, or ten milligrams per milliliter. That gives the deck's shortcut: the drug percent times ten equals milligrams per milliliter. Every percentage problem then takes two steps. Step one, convert the percent into milligrams per milliliter, the concentration. Step two, multiply the concentration by the volume to get the dose, or divide the dose by the concentration to get the volume. The most common mistake is skipping step one, or mixing up milligrams, which are weight, with milliliters, which are volume.

[02-006 · Practice: albuterol by percent]
Try two problems before the answer. First, how many milligrams of albuterol are in three milliliters of a point zero eight three percent solution? Second, how many milliliters of a point five percent albuterol solution give a two point five milligram dose? Use the two steps.
[6-second practice pause]

[02-007 · Answer: 2.5 mg, and 0.5 mL]
Problem one. Point zero eight three times ten is point eight three milligrams per milliliter. Times three milliliters is two point four nine milligrams, which the deck prints as about two point five, the standard nebulizer dose. Problem two. Point five times ten is five milligrams per milliliter. Two point five milligrams divided by five is point five milliliters. The deck adds racemic epinephrine. Two point two five percent is twenty two point five milligrams per milliliter, so a dose of point two five to point five milliliters is five point six two five to eleven point two five milligrams, which the deck prints as five point six three to eleven point two five.

[02-008 · Diluent does not change the dose]
The amount of diluent changes only how long the drug takes to give. The amount of active drug stays the same. Two point five milligrams of albuterol is two point five milligrams whether it is mixed with two, five or ten milliliters of saline. So why add diluent at all? Most nebulizers will not aerosolize well below about one milliliter, because of their dead volume, and treatment time matters. The aim is a total volume of about two point five to three milliliters. The exam trap is the idea that more saline makes a weaker treatment. That is wrong. More saline only makes a longer one.

[02-009 · Ratio solutions and percent types]
No deck in the vault works these, so this comes from Hess chapter fifty five. There are three percent types: weight per weight, weight per volume, and volume per volume. Weight per volume is the one used in pharmacology. A ratio such as one to one hundred means one gram in one hundred milliliters. Hess's example is point five milliliters of one to two hundred epinephrine. One to two hundred is one thousand milligrams in two hundred milliliters, or five milligrams per milliliter, so point five milliliters holds two point five milligrams. By the same rule, the study guide works out that one to one thousand is one milligram per milliliter, so the deck's point three to point five milligrams of one to one thousand epinephrine is point three to point five milliliters.

[02-010 · Practice: dilute a stock solution]
One more. You need five milliliters of a ten percent solution, and you have a twenty percent stock. How much stock do you draw up, and how much water do you add?
[6-second practice pause]

[02-011 · Answer: 2.5 mL stock plus 2.5 mL water]
Use the dilution equation: volume one times concentration one equals volume two times concentration two. Here x times twenty equals five times ten, so x is two point five milliliters of stock, plus two point five milliliters of water to make five. Two more facts from Hess. Ten thousand parts per million equals one percent. And watch one printed example: Hess lists albuterol two point five milligrams in point five milliliters as five percent, but two point five divided by point five is five milligrams per milliliter, which is point five percent. Reversing the deck's percent times ten rule gives the same answer. Use point five percent.

[02-012 · Recap]
Here is the chapter in a few lines. Get every quantity into the same units, then use the proportion. For milligrams per kilogram, find the dose first, then the amount. For a percent, multiply by ten to get milligrams per milliliter, then multiply by the volume or divide the dose. The diluent changes the treatment time, never the dose. A ratio is grams per milliliters, so one to one thousand is one milligram per milliliter. And to dilute a stock, volume times concentration stays the same on both sides.
