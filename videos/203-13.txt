RCP 203 — Chapter 13: PFTs: Lung Volumes and Capacities, Bronchoprovocation, Lung-Volume Measurement, Obstructive vs Restrictive

[13-001 · What this chapter covers]
This chapter covers pulmonary function testing from the ground up. We start with the four lung volumes and the capacities built from them, with their normal values. Then the spirometry values, FVC, FEV1, their ratio, the mid flows and peak flow, and the quality rules for a good test. Then the bronchodilator response, the three ways to measure functional residual capacity, and the methacholine challenge. We finish by putting it together to tell obstructive from restrictive disease.

[13-002 · The four lung volumes]
There are four volumes, and every capacity is made of two or more of them. Tidal volume is the gas inhaled or exhaled in normal breathing, normally point five liters. Inspiratory reserve volume is the most gas you can breathe in from the end of a normal inspiration, about three point one liters. Expiratory reserve volume is the most gas you can breathe out from the end of a resting expiration, about one point two liters. Residual volume is the gas left after a maximal expiration, about one point two liters, and it cannot be exhaled.

[13-003 · The capacities, and why FRC is key]
Inspiratory capacity is tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve. No slide prints its value, but three point one plus point five is three point six liters. Functional residual capacity is residual volume plus expiratory reserve, the gas left at the end of a resting expiration, about two point four liters. Vital capacity is tidal volume plus both reserves, about four point eight liters, roughly fifty milliliters per kilogram. Total lung capacity is residual volume plus vital capacity, about six liters. Functional residual capacity is the key static volume. A spirometer can measure inspiratory capacity and expiratory reserve, but not residual volume, so the functional residual capacity must be measured separately, and then residual volume and total lung capacity are calculated from it.

[13-004 · FVC, FEV1 and their ratio]
The forced vital capacity is the total air exhaled rapidly from total lung capacity, for at least six seconds per Hess. The chapter eight deck notes that under six seconds in adults has traditionally meant early termination, though that is not a universal minimum now. Normal is eighty percent of predicted or more. FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second of that maneuver, also normal at eighty percent of predicted or more. The FEV1 to FVC ratio is the percent of the forced vital capacity exhaled in the first second. Normal is seventy to eighty percent or more, depending on age, and below seventy percent means obstruction. Hess gives a normal ratio of point seven five to point eight five, declining after age thirty.

[13-005 · Mid flows, peak flow and test quality]
The forced expiratory flow from twenty five to seventy five percent is the average flow over the middle half of the forced vital capacity, and it reflects the small airways. Seventy percent of predicted or less means small airway obstruction, and a low value can show early small airway disease even when the FEV1 is normal. Peak expiratory flow is the highest flow during the maneuver, and below eighty percent of predicted means expiratory flow obstruction. A good test needs at least three error-free maneuvers: no slow or false start, no cough, no breathing during the effort, and no early termination. The two largest forced vital capacities, and the two largest FEV1 values, judged separately, must be within one hundred fifty milliliters. Hess prefers the lower limit of normal to the eighty percent rule, but use the deck's eighty and seventy percent cutoffs here.

[13-006 · Bronchodilator response]
When obstruction is suspected, repeat spirometry at the drug's peak response time, usually fifteen to thirty minutes after the bronchodilator. The percent change is the post value minus the pre value, divided by the pre value, times one hundred. Reversible obstruction, as in asthma, means the FEV1 improves by at least twelve to fifteen percent and by at least two hundred milliliters in adults. The RCP 103 asthma deck says twelve percent and, or, two hundred milliliters, with twelve percent of predicted for children, and Hess says twelve percent with a minimum of two hundred milliliters in FEV1 or FVC, ten to fifteen minutes after albuterol. Use the RCP 203 slide: twelve to fifteen percent and two hundred milliliters.

[13-007 · Practice: is it reversible?]
Pause and calculate. Before a bronchodilator, the FEV1 is three point zero liters. After it, the FEV1 is three point three liters. What is the percent change, and is this reversible obstruction by the RCP 203 criteria?
[6-second practice pause]

[13-008 · Answer: 10%, not reversible]
Three point three minus three point zero is point three. Point three divided by three point zero is point one zero, times one hundred is ten percent. That is below twelve to fifteen percent, so it is not reversible, even though the change, three hundred milliliters, passes the volume rule. Both parts must be met. This is the deck's own worked example.

[13-009 · Measuring FRC: washout and dilution]
There are three ways to measure functional residual capacity. In nitrogen washout, an open circuit, the patient breathes one hundred percent oxygen, and you measure the nitrogen washed out of the lungs, starting from room air at about seventy nine percent. The test ends when exhaled nitrogen is below one and a half percent for three breaths, usually under five minutes, and you stop at seven. A rise in nitrogen means a leak. In helium dilution, a closed circuit, the patient rebreathes a known volume containing about ten percent helium, and the functional residual capacity is calculated from how much the helium is diluted. Carbon dioxide is absorbed with sodium or barium hydroxide, and a leak gives a falsely high result.

[13-010 · The body box, and the trapped-gas trap]
In body plethysmography, the patient sits in a sealed box. A shutter closes at functional residual capacity, the patient pants, and Boyle's law gives the thoracic gas volume. It is fast, and it measures all the gas in the chest, including trapped gas and bullae. Here is the trap. Helium dilution and nitrogen washout measure only gas that communicates with the airways, so they can underestimate the functional residual capacity in obstruction. Normally the two match. A thoracic gas volume larger than the functional residual capacity means trapped gas, as in bullous emphysema or air trapping. Hess's equations add one picture: a small lung, as in fibrosis, gives a steeper pressure slope, and a large lung, as in emphysema, a flatter one.

[13-011 · Bronchoprovocation: the methacholine challenge]
Bronchoprovocation deliberately provokes bronchospasm in patients with hyperresponsive airways. It is used when spirometry is nondiagnostic but asthma is suspected, because mild asthma often gives normal spirometry between episodes. Methacholine is a direct-acting cholinergic, a parasympathomimetic. The patient inhales rising doses, with spirometry after each. The deck calls the test positive when the FEV1 falls by twenty percent or more, meaning hyperresponsiveness consistent with asthma. Hess adds that you keep stepping up while the FEV1 stays above eighty percent of baseline, report the PD20, the dose that causes a twenty percent fall, and give albuterol afterward.

[13-012 · Mannitol, exercise, and when not to test]
Hess lists two other challenges. Mannitol is positive if the FEV1 falls by fifteen percent or more from baseline, or by ten percent or more between doses. An exercise challenge is about eight minutes of hard exercise, and a ten percent fall in FEV1 is positive. Bronchoprovocation is contraindicated in moderate to severe obstruction. On test day, no bronchodilators and no exercise, and Hess says to reschedule four to six weeks after a chest infection. One gap remains: no source gives a concentration cutoff, a PC20, for a positive methacholine test.

[13-013 · Obstructive versus restrictive: spirometry]
Obstructive disease reduces airflow; restrictive disease reduces lung volume. In obstruction, the FEV1 to FVC ratio is below seventy percent, the FEV1 is reduced, the forced vital capacity is normal or reduced, and the mid flows are reduced. In restriction, the ratio is normal or increased, the forced vital capacity is reduced, the FEV1 is normal or reduced, and the mid flows are normal or reduced. The deck's examples of obstruction are asthma, COPD and bronchiectasis. Its examples of restriction are interstitial lung disease, atelectasis, pleural effusion and Guillain-Barré.

[13-014 · Obstructive versus restrictive: volumes and DLCO]
Volumes confirm the pattern. In obstruction, total lung capacity rises with hyperinflation, and a high residual volume, or a high residual volume to total lung capacity ratio, means air trapping. In restriction, all volumes fall, total lung capacity is below eighty percent of predicted, and the ratio stays normal. Hess points out that spirometry only suggests restriction; a low total lung capacity confirms it. The diffusing capacity falls in emphysema and stays normal or rises in asthma and chronic bronchitis. It falls in interstitial lung disease, and a normal value with restriction points to a chest wall cause. The deck's cutoffs are below seventy percent for the ratio, and below eighty percent of predicted for vital capacity, total lung capacity and diffusing capacity. All three reduced means a combined defect.

[13-015 · Practice: name the pattern]
One more. A patient's FEV1 to FVC ratio is eighty five percent, total lung capacity is sixty five percent of predicted, and the diffusing capacity is reduced. Is this obstructive or restrictive, and which kind of disease does the low diffusing capacity point to?
[6-second practice pause]

[13-016 · Answer: restrictive, likely ILD]
This is restrictive. The ratio is normal to high, not below seventy percent, and the total lung capacity is below eighty percent of predicted, which confirms restriction. The reduced diffusing capacity points to interstitial lung disease, a problem in the lung itself. If the diffusing capacity were normal with this restriction, Hess would point you to a chest wall cause instead.

[13-017 · Recap]
Here is the chapter in brief. Four volumes, tidal, inspiratory reserve, expiratory reserve and residual, build every capacity, and the functional residual capacity must be measured separately. FVC and FEV1 are normal at eighty percent of predicted, and a ratio below seventy percent means obstruction. Reversibility needs twelve to fifteen percent and two hundred milliliters. Washout and dilution miss trapped gas; the body box does not. A methacholine challenge is positive at a twenty percent fall in FEV1. And restriction is confirmed by a low total lung capacity, with the diffusing capacity pointing to the lung or the chest wall.
