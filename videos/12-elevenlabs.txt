RCP 202 — Chapter 12: Respiratory Anatomy (Hess Ch 49)

[12-001 · Chapter 49: everything in anatomy]
Welcome to section twelve, respiratory anatomy, Hess chapter forty-nine. The study guide flags this chapter as everything. We will cover the five processes of respiration, fetal and postnatal lung development, upper and lower airway, the lungs and alveoli, pleurae and mediastinum, the bony thorax, respiratory muscles and their innervation, the mucociliary apparatus, airway and vascular smooth muscle, and the key immune cells of the lung. Names and numbers carry this chapter, so we will keep them precise.

[12-002 · The five processes of respiration]
Respiration has five distinct processes, and ventilation is only the first. One, ventilation: movement of air from the atmosphere to the lung. Two, perfusion: circulation of blood through the cardiovascular system. Three, diffusion: movement of oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar-capillary membrane from high to low pressure. Four, external respiration: gas exchange between alveoli and blood. Five, internal respiration: gas exchange between blood and tissues. Gas exchange occurs exclusively at the acinus, the alveolar level. Gas distribution is gas moving through the upper airway and the conducting airways of the lower tract.

[12-003 · The five stages of fetal lung development]
Memorize this order: embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, alveolar. Embryonic, weeks four to six, builds proximal airways from a lung bud off the foregut. Pseudoglandular, weeks six to sixteen, builds conducting airways and nearly completes the diaphragm; survival after premature delivery is highly unlikely. Canalicular, weeks seventeen to twenty-six, builds the vascular bed, and type one and type two cells appear; limited gas exchange becomes possible, so survival is possible with intensive, advanced care. Saccular, weeks twenty-seven to thirty-six, forms terminal saccules, and surfactant is present. Alveolar, thirty-six weeks and beyond, with alveoli peaking at about three hundred million by age eight. Hess's prose and glossary give different dates; the guide says use Table 49-2.

[12-004 · Birth transition and infant airway]
Before birth the lung is fluid-filled, which keeps it expanded and helps it grow. The first breath needs a transpulmonary pressure of approximately forty to eighty centimeters of water and roughly forty milliliters of volume to overcome surface tension and residual fluid. Capillaries and lymphatics may take several hours to clear the fluid. The infant has a proportionally larger head and tongue, smaller nasal passages, and a higher larynx, which make it a preferential nose breather. The cricoid ring is the narrowest point in the infant, the glottis in the adult. One millimeter of circumferential edema can cut infant airway diameter by more than fifty percent, as in croup, epiglottitis, or post-extubation edema. Newer imaging questions the cricoid rule, but for boards the deck says children, cricoid; adults, glottis.

[12-005 · Upper airway: nose, sinuses, pharynx]
The upper tract runs from nose and mouth to larynx; the lower tract begins at the trachea. The nasal cavity filters, warms, and humidifies air; nasal conchae are bony ridges projecting into the cavity, and the respiratory mucosa secretes mucus. Paranasal sinuses, maxillary, frontal, sphenoid, and ethmoid, are hollow spaces lined with ciliated epithelium that act as resonant chambers and lighten the skull. The pharynx has three regions: the nasopharynx carries air only and has ciliated epithelium with goblet cells; the oropharynx has stratified squamous epithelium and carries air and food; the laryngopharynx is the most inferior. Enlarged tonsils, adenoids, or uvula may impede breathing, especially during sleep. The epiglottis covers the tracheal opening during swallowing.

[12-006 · Larynx: cartilages and the glottis]
The larynx is a cartilaginous passage between pharynx and trachea, housing the vocal cords. The hyoid bone sits above it; the epiglottis is the uppermost cartilage. The thyroid cartilage is the largest and makes the Adam's apple. The cricoid cartilage is the only complete ring around the airway and sits below the thyroid. Between the thyroid and cricoid lies the cricothyroid membrane, where a cricothyrotomy can open the lower airway in an emergency. The vocal cords are mucosal folds; the opening between them is the glottis, a triangular slit that separates the upper and lower airway and closes during swallowing.

[12-007 · Trachea, carina, main stem bronchi]
The trachea is ten to thirteen centimeters long, about two to two-and-a-half centimeters in diameter, supported by sixteen to twenty C-shaped cartilages, and lined with pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium; smoking destroys its cilia. At the carina it bifurcates into right and left main stem bronchi. The right main stem leaves at twenty to thirty degrees from vertical, the left at forty-five to fifty-five degrees. Because the right angle is smaller, foreign bodies and suction catheters more often go right. For endotracheal tube placement, the deck target is four to six centimeters above the carina with the head neutral; too deep usually enters the right main stem. Hess chapter seventeen says three to seven centimeters, but the guide says use four to six on this exam.

[12-008 · Conducting vs respiratory zone]
Main bronchi split by dichotomous branching into lobar, then segmental, then subsegmental bronchi, then bronchioles. Up to the bronchioles the airways are supported by cartilage. Bronchioles are less than one millimeter wide and have no cartilage; elastic forces of the lung hold them open during exhalation. Terminal bronchioles are the last conducting airway. Hess's prose places the respiratory zone at the sixteenth to twenty-third generations; the chapter's figure places terminal bronchioles at generation sixteen and the respiratory zone at seventeen through twenty-three. The guide says know both. The conducting zone distributes air; the respiratory zone, the acinus, is where gas exchange happens. The Mucus deck's notes credit alveolar ducts and respiratory bronchioles with about ten percent of exchange, alveoli with the other ninety.

[12-009 · Prompt: estimate anatomic dead space]
Pause and work this one out. The RCP 105 Principles of Mechanical Ventilation deck gives a quick bedside rule for estimating anatomic dead space from body weight. Your patient weighs one hundred eighty pounds. What is the rule, and about how many milliliters of anatomic dead space does this patient have?
[6-second practice pause]

[12-010 · Answer: about 180 mL of dead space]
The deck's rule is about one milliliter of anatomic dead space per pound of body weight, so a one-hundred-fifty-pound person has about one hundred fifty milliliters. For our patient, one hundred eighty pounds times one milliliter per pound equals about one hundred eighty milliliters. The unit is pounds, not kilograms. Remember this is an estimate from the instructor's deck, not a measured value, and it sits alongside the chapter's point that the conducting airways move air without exchanging gas.

[12-011 · Lungs: lobes, fissures, segments]
Normal adult lungs weigh about eight hundred grams and are about ninety percent gas and ten percent tissue. There are five lobes and eighteen segments. The right lung has three lobes, upper, middle, and lower: a horizontal fissure separates upper and middle, and an oblique fissure separates middle and lower, with ten segments total. The left lung has two lobes, upper and lower, separated by an oblique fissure, with eight segments, including the lingular segments of the upper lobe. The hilum, on the medial surface, is where bronchi and vessels enter. The right lung is larger because the left shares its side with the heart. Knowing segments matters for bronchoscopy and postural drainage.

[12-012 · Alveoli and the AC membrane]
The adult lung has about three hundred million alveoli, each two hundred to three hundred microns in diameter. Pulmonary capillaries cover eighty-five to ninety percent of the alveolar surface. Capillary blood and alveolar gas are separated by vascular endothelium, interstitial space, and alveolar epithelium, and gas crosses this alveolar-capillary membrane by diffusion. The flat type one cells that line it are only zero point one to zero point five microns thick. Adjacent alveoli communicate through pores of Kohn, and channels of Lambert connect alveoli to adjacent terminal bronchioles. The COPD deck notes that this collateral ventilation helps compensate in emphysema.

[12-013 · Prompt: the cell that keeps alveoli open]
Pause and think. Two alveolar cell types line the air sac. One is thin and squamous and does most of the gas exchange. The other is cuboidal, sits in the corners, and makes a specific substance that keeps alveoli from collapsing at end-expiration. Which cell makes it, what is it called, and which disease name is tied to its deficiency in the newborn?
[6-second practice pause]

[12-014 · Answer: type II makes surfactant]
Type one pneumocytes are flat and squamous, cover about ninety to ninety-five percent of the alveolar surface, and are the major site of gas exchange, but they are sensitive to injury from high oxygen, bacteria, bleomycin, cyclophosphamide, and particulates. Type two pneumocytes are cuboidal, cover the remaining five to ten percent, and are the primary source of surfactant. They also differentiate into type one cells when those are damaged. Hess gives slightly different percentages in other passages; learn the ninety-to-ninety-five and five-to-ten ranges. Surfactant is ninety percent phospholipid and ten percent protein; the main surface-active molecule is dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine, DPPC. Absence of surfactant causes atelectasis, and surfactant deficiency is seen in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome.

[12-015 · Interstitium and lymphatics]
The interstitium is the space between alveolar epithelium and vascular endothelium, a continuum from the visceral pleura to the hilum. Its matrix holds elastin, which gives the lung its elastic recoil, and collagen, which limits over-distention and makes up fifteen to twenty percent of lung dry weight. Elastases or alpha-one antitrypsin deficiency can cause considerable lung damage. Interstitial lung disease means more cells and a thicker interstitium; the ILD deck adds that the thickened barrier reduces diffusion and causes hypoxemia. Lymphatics lie beneath the visceral pleura, exit at the hilum, drain to mediastinal nodes, and remove excess fluid and protein, bacteria, and foreign material.

[12-016 · Pleurae, pleural space, mediastinum]
The visceral pleura covers the lung surface and dips into the fissures, is innervated by the vagus, and has no pain sensation. The parietal pleura lines the chest wall, is innervated by intercostal and phrenic nerves, and is very pain-sensitive; pleurisy pain may be referred to the neck and shoulder. Ciliated mesothelial cells line both. The pleural space holds less than twenty milliliters of thin, low-protein fluid. Capillary leakage is balanced by pleural lymphatics, which can handle about seven hundred milliliters a day. The fluid lets lung and chest wall slide while holding them tightly together, and pleural pressure stays negative. The mediastinum is everything in the thorax except the lungs and pleurae: heart in pericardium, great vessels, trachea, esophagus, thymus, nerves.

[12-017 · Bony thorax and chest-wall mechanics]
The bony thorax is the sternum, twelve pairs of ribs, thoracic vertebrae, clavicles, and scapulae. The sternum has three parts: the manubrium connecting to ribs one and two, the body connecting to ribs three to seven, and the xiphoid at the tip. Ribs one to seven are important in ventilation: they lift outward and upward like a bucket handle while the sternum rises like a pump handle. The anteroposterior diameter is normally about half the width of the chest. In obstructive disease with air trapping, the AP diameter increases to produce a barrel chest; the RCP 103 decks tie it to COPD and the emphysema pink puffer.

[12-018 · Diaphragm and accessory muscles]
The diaphragm is the primary muscle of inspiration. It is dome-shaped, attaches to vertebrae, ribs, and xiphoid, and its fibers join at the central tendon. On inspiration it contracts and flattens, lowering intrathoracic pressure and creating a pressure difference with the atmosphere. Excursion is about one point five centimeters in tidal breathing and up to six to ten centimeters at high work of breathing. The phrenic nerve, C3 to C5, supplies it; Hess says cervical fractures between C1 and C5 are likely to impair breathing, and the neuro deck adds that a spinal cord injury at or above C3 to C5 can paralyze the diaphragm. Accessory inspiratory muscles are the scalene, sternocleidomastoid, pectoralis major, trapezius, and external intercostals. Accessory expiratory muscles are the internal intercostals and the abdominals: rectus, external and internal obliques, transverse. Exhalation is normally passive, from elastic recoil of the lungs plus relaxation of the diaphragm; abdominal contraction means active exhalation.

[12-019 · Innervation summary]
A quick nerve map. The phrenic nerve, C3 to C5, is the motor supply to the diaphragm. The vagus supplies the visceral pleura, which has no pain sensation. Intercostal and phrenic nerves supply the parietal pleura, which is very pain-sensitive. The olfactory nerve carries smell, and nasal sensory neurons start the sneeze reflex. In the pulmonary vasculature, nerve endings release acetylcholine and norepinephrine, which affect vascular tone. The Mucus deck adds that submucosal glands are under parasympathetic control and increase mucus with cholinergic stimulation, while surface goblet cells do not seem to be directly innervated.

[12-020 · Mucociliary apparatus]
We breathe twelve to twenty-four thousand liters of air per day, so the primary defense in the conducting airways is the mucociliary escalator. Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, with goblet cells, lines the airway. Cilia beat in a watery sol layer, and a mucous gel layer rides on top. Where sources differ, the guide uses the instructor's deck: a ciliated-to-goblet ratio of five to one, not Hess's four to one; cilia seven microns long in larger airways and five or less in small bronchioles, not Hess's six; and one hundred milliliters secreted per day. Hess adds that cilia beat one thousand to fifteen hundred times per minute. Dehydration limits secretion production, and smoking, cystic fibrosis, COPD, asthma, and several drugs impair transport.

[12-021 · Smooth muscle and hypoxic vasoconstriction]
Both airways and pulmonary vessels have smooth muscle under involuntary control. The pulmonary vasculature has less smooth muscle than systemic arteries, and its tone, pressure, and resistance are low. Smooth muscle directs regional blood flow by changing vessel caliber. Vasoactive chemicals come from endothelial cells, nerves, mast cells, or other organs. Hypercapnia, a fall in pH, and particularly hypoxia cause pulmonary vasoconstriction; hypoxia acts directly on smooth muscle cells. This response exists to match alveolar ventilation and perfusion and maximize gas exchange. Note that chapter forty-nine does not cover the gross anatomy of the pulmonary or bronchial circulation.

[12-022 · Mast cells, macrophages, dendritic cells]
Three cells to know. Mast cells, ten to twenty microns, sit in respiratory mucosa and alveolar septa with abundant granules; they release histamine, serotonin, leukotrienes, and cytokines. Mast cell numbers are elevated in asthma. Alveolar macrophages, sometimes called type three cells, are bone-marrow derived and perform phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and cytokine production, with interleukin-one and TNF as the most significant cytokines; they remove bacteria from the acini. Dendritic cells are also bone-marrow derived and mobile, and their main role is antigen presentation; they take up antigens and migrate to lymph nodes to activate T lymphocytes. Inhaled or systemic steroids reduce dendritic cell numbers.

[12-023 · Hess chapter 49 key points]
The Hess chapter forty-nine key points, in brief. The respiratory system's primary functions are gas distribution and gas exchange. The five stages of fetal lung development are embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, and alveolar. Postnatal development creates infant-adult anatomic differences. Upper airway: nose, nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx, larynx. Lower airway: trachea, main stem bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli. The lungs consist of airways and alveoli. The bony thorax is sternum, ribs, vertebrae, clavicles, scapulae. Respiratory muscles are diaphragm plus accessory inspiratory and expiratory muscles. Visceral pleura surrounds the lung, parietal lines the chest wall, with the pleural space between. The mucociliary apparatus clears the lower tract. Important lower-tract cells include mast, macrophage, dendritic. Alveoli have type one and type two cells. Interstitium lies between epithelium and endothelium.

[12-024 · Recap: name the parts, know the numbers]
The lung distributes gas through the conducting zone from trachea through terminal bronchiole, then exchanges gas in the acinus. Right main stem is more vertical, twenty to thirty degrees; left is forty-five to fifty-five; ETT tip four to six centimeters above the carina. The right lung has three lobes with ten segments; the left has two lobes with eight segments. Type one cells line gas exchange; type two cells make surfactant. Diaphragm is primary, phrenic C3 to C5; accessory inspiratory muscles join with increased effort. Visceral pleura has no pain; parietal has pain. Mucociliary escalator defends; smoking destroys cilia. Five stages: embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, alveolar.
