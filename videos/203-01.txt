RCP 203 — Chapter 01: Pneumonia, CXRs, Pleural Effusions, Accessory Muscles, Extubation Parameters

[01-001 · What this chapter covers]
Welcome to the first topic on the RCP 203 final study guide. It packs five exam items into one chapter. We start with pneumonia: what it is, how it shows up on exam, and how the categories and the typical versus atypical patterns differ. Then we read a chest X-ray step by step, and use those findings to recognize a pleural effusion and tell a transudate from an exudate. Next come the accessory muscles and what their use means. We finish with the weaning and extubation numbers, and the separate checks that decide whether the tube can actually come out.

[01-002 · Pneumonia: definition and signs]
The RCP 103 deck defines pneumonia as an infectious inflammatory process that mainly affects the gas exchange area of the lungs, causing capillary fluid to pour into the alveoli. Causes are bacteria, viruses, fungi or aspiration. Hess calls it inflammation and consolidation of lung tissue, most often from infection. Vital signs rise: fever, heart rate, respiratory rate and blood pressure. The decks separate fever by cause, above one hundred one degrees Fahrenheit for bacterial and below one hundred one for viral. Tachypnea comes from hypoxemia sensed by the peripheral chemoreceptors, and from poor lung compliance. The cough starts dry and hacking, then turns productive with yellow or green sputum, and it may be rust colored or show hemoptysis.

[01-003 · Pneumonia: exam and diagnostic tests]
Over consolidated lung, the exam shows increased vocal fremitus, a dull percussion note, bronchial breath sounds or crackles, and whispered pectoriloquy. In lobar pneumonia, chest expansion is reduced on the affected side. The chest X-ray shows increased density, air bronchograms, and possibly an abscess with an air fluid level, or an effusion or empyema. The blood gas shows respiratory alkalosis with hypoxemia early, and respiratory acidosis with hypoxemia when severe. The white count rises in bacterial pneumonia and falls in viral pneumonia, sputum goes for culture and sensitivity, and the PFT pattern is restrictive. Hess adds that pneumonia is a clinical diagnosis. An infiltrate on imaging is necessary, and a pathogen is isolated in only forty percent of cases. Bronchoalveolar lavage is indicated for non-resolving pneumonia and for bacterial VAP.

[01-004 · Pneumonia categories: CAP, HAP, VAP, HCAP]
The decks sort pneumonia by where it was acquired. Community acquired pneumonia comes from normal social contact, and Hess names Streptococcus pneumoniae as the most common bacterium. Hospital acquired pneumonia appears forty eight hours or more after admission and was not present at admission. Its main organisms are Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MSSA and MRSA. Ventilator associated pneumonia develops after intubation. The sources disagree on timing: RCP 103 says more than forty eight to seventy two hours, while the RCP 105 notes and RCP 203 both say forty eight hours. Use forty eight hours or more. Health care associated pneumonia meant hospitalization within ninety days, nursing home residence, or IV antibiotics, chemotherapy or wound care within thirty days. Hess says that term was retired in twenty sixteen.

[01-005 · Typical versus atypical pneumonia]
Hess contrasts two patterns. Typical pneumonia starts abruptly, with rigors, high fever and pleuritic chest pain. The sputum is rusty or purulent, the white count is usually elevated, and the chest X-ray shows lobar or segmental consolidation with air bronchograms. The main organism is Streptococcus pneumoniae. Atypical pneumonia is a flu-like illness: dry cough, moderate fever and no rigors. It is more common in young adults. The white count is usually normal, and the film shows diffuse interstitial or peribronchial opacities. Its organisms, such as Mycoplasma, Chlamydia and Legionella, are not seen on Gram stain. Mycoplasma is the most common atypical cause, and it produces walking pneumonia.

[01-006 · Practice: name the pattern]
Pause and work this one out. A young adult has a dry cough, a moderate fever and no rigors. The white count is normal, and the chest film shows diffuse interstitial opacities. Which pattern is this, what is its most common cause, and what drug class does the deck tell you to think of?
[6-second practice pause]

[01-007 · Answer: atypical, Mycoplasma, macrolide]
This is atypical pneumonia. The flu-like picture, the missing rigors, the normal white count and the diffuse interstitial film all point away from the abrupt lobar picture of typical pneumonia. The most common atypical cause is Mycoplasma, which produces walking pneumonia. Because these organisms are not seen on Gram stain, a negative stain does not rule them out. For treatment, the pharmacology deck gives a simple rule: atypical pneumonia, think macrolide.

[01-008 · Reading a chest X-ray, step by step]
RCP 203 gives a fixed order. First verify the patient identity, the orientation and the image quality. Identify the view, AP or PA. Check the hemidiaphragms and the costophrenic angles, sharp or blunted. Check the tracheal position. Estimate the cardiothoracic ratio. Note every tube and line. Then compare with previous films. For image quality, remember the word ripe: rotation, inspiration, position, meaning AP or PA, and exposure. Exposure is a common trap. An underexposed film is too white, and an overexposed film is too dark.

[01-009 · White versus black, and the diaphragm]
Think of white as more stuff and black as more air. An opacity, meaning white, points to atelectasis, consolidation or pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or a pleural effusion. A lucency, meaning black, points to emphysema or a pneumothorax. A blunted costophrenic angle means fluid, a pleural effusion. The diaphragm tells you about volume. A flattened diaphragm means hyperinflation, as in COPD, emphysema or acute asthma. An elevated diaphragm means volume loss or diaphragm weakness, as in atelectasis or phrenic nerve paralysis.

[01-010 · Heart size, Kerley B lines and the ETT tip]
Cardiomegaly means the heart is wider than fifty percent of the chest on a PA film. Do not apply that rule to an AP film, because the AP view magnifies the heart. Kerley B lines mean interstitial pulmonary edema, as in heart failure. The endotracheal tube tip should sit four to six centimeters above the carina. A tube that is too deep usually enters the right mainstem. Hess adds three film tricks. A lateral decubitus film identifies free pleural fluid. An expiratory film shows a small pneumothorax. And an air bronchogram means the opacity is in the lung itself, not in the pleura or the mediastinum.

[01-011 · Pleural effusion: transudate versus exudate]
A pleural effusion is fluid in the pleural space. The common causes are CHF, cancer and pneumonia, and it is a restrictive disorder because it limits lung expansion and lowers compliance. Effusions come in two kinds. A transudate comes from systemic pressure changes. The fluid is thin and watery, low in protein, with few cells, and clear or light straw. CHF is the most common cause, then cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome. An exudate comes from pleural inflammation that raises capillary permeability. The fluid is high in protein with many cells, cloudy or opaque. Causes are infection such as pneumonia or TB, malignancy, and autoimmune disease. The deck says a fluid pH below seven point three zero suggests an exudate. Hess, using Light's criteria, gives a fluid to serum protein ratio of point five or more, or an LDH ratio of point six or more.

[01-012 · Effusion: exam, film and thoracentesis]
A large effusion pushes the trachea away from the fluid. Tactile fremitus is decreased, the percussion note is dull, and breath sounds are diminished or absent. On the chest X-ray, blunting of the costophrenic angle is the earliest and most common sign. Larger effusions add a meniscus sign and shift the mediastinum to the unaffected side. Effusions under about three hundred milliliters may not show on a standard film. Thoracentesis can be diagnostic, to tell transudate from exudate, or therapeutic, when the effusion is large enough to cause respiratory distress. Up to about fifteen hundred milliliters may be removed. Complications are pneumothorax, bleeding, infection and re-expansion pulmonary edema.

[01-013 · Accessory muscles and what they mean]
The accessory muscles of inspiration, per RCP 203, are the sternocleidomastoid, the scalenes, the upper trapezius and the pectoralis major. Hess also lists the external intercostals. Expiration is normally passive. The expiratory accessory muscles, the internal intercostals and the abdominals, join in when resistance to expiration is significant, as in severe obstructive disease, so abdominal contraction means active exhalation. The deck's main point is that accessory muscle use at rest means increased work of breathing. During a spontaneous breathing trial, it is a sign to consider stopping, and it is an early sign of weaning failure. The NBRC pearl: a rate of thirty eight, accessory muscle use, sweating, a falling tidal volume and a rising carbon dioxide mean impending ventilatory failure from muscle fatigue. One trap: paralysis masks accessory muscle use.

[01-014 · Weaning numbers: RSBI, NIF, VC, tidal volume]
The rapid shallow breathing index is the rate divided by the tidal volume in liters. Use less than one hundred, from RCP 105. Hess uses less than one hundred five, and the deck says the NBRC commonly uses one hundred five. Negative inspiratory force should be minus twenty to minus thirty centimeters of water in twenty seconds, and RCP 203 says minus twenty is the minimum to extubate. Normal is more negative than minus sixty. Vital capacity should be greater than ten milliliters per kilogram, with fifteen, about one liter, in some notes and in Hess. Spontaneous tidal volume should be at least five milliliters per kilogram as the general weaning predictor.

[01-015 · Oxygenation, pH and passing the SBT]
Minute ventilation is normally six to ten liters per minute. Above ten is often linked to poor weaning outcomes, and Hess uses twelve. For oxygenation, RCP 203 wants a P over F ratio of one hundred fifty to two hundred or more, PEEP of five to eight or less, and inspired oxygen of forty to fifty percent or less. The pH should be at least seven point two five to screen, and compliance above thirty. After at least thirty minutes, RCP 203 calls the trial passed when saturation stays at least eighty five to ninety percent, the pH stays at least seven point three zero, carbon dioxide rises no more than ten, the rate stays at or below thirty to thirty five, heart rate below one hundred twenty to one hundred forty, systolic pressure between ninety and one hundred eighty to two hundred, and no vasopressors are needed.

[01-016 · The NHLBI ARDS protocol and the tidal volume trap]
RCP 203 also teaches the NHLBI ARDS protocol. A patient is ready for a trial on inspired oxygen of forty percent or less with PEEP of eight or less, or fifty percent or less with PEEP of five or less. The trial lasts up to one hundred twenty minutes on a T-piece, a trach collar, or CPAP of five or less. It is tolerated when saturation is at least ninety percent, the tidal volume is at least four milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight, the rate is thirty five or less, the pH at least seven point three zero, and there is no distress. Tolerated for thirty minutes, consider extubation. Here is the trap. Answer five milliliters per kilogram for a general weaning predictor, and use four only when the question names the ARDS protocol.

[01-017 · Practice: calculate the RSBI]
Try the calculation. During a spontaneous breathing trial, your patient breathes at a rate of thirty eight with a tidal volume of three hundred milliliters. Work out the rapid shallow breathing index. Is this patient ready, and what should happen next?
[6-second practice pause]

[01-018 · Answer: RSBI 127, not ready]
Convert the tidal volume to liters first, three hundred milliliters is point three. Thirty eight divided by point three is about one hundred twenty seven. That is above one hundred, so the patient is not ready. When a trial fails, find the cause and repeat the trial every twenty four hours. Notice too that a rate of thirty eight with a falling tidal volume is part of the NBRC picture of impending ventilatory failure from muscle fatigue.

[01-019 · After the SBT: ready to pull the tube?]
Passing the trial and pulling the tube are two separate decisions. The chapter thirteen deck says the extubation decision should be made separately from the weaning assessment. Before the trial comes a daily sedation interruption, the sedation vacation, called a spontaneous awakening trial in some hospitals, for every ventilated patient stable enough to be off sedation. It allows a more accurate readiness and neurologic assessment. If the trial is not tolerated, return to the previous settings. Then four things must hold. The upper airway is patent. The airway can be protected, with a gag reflex and the ability to lift the head off the bed. There is an adequate cuff leak, with no sign of significant airway swelling. And secretions can be cleared. The most serious extubation complication is laryngospasm.

[01-020 · Peak cough flow and the SIMV caution]
Clearing secretions means a deep cough when suctioned and, if the patient is alert, a maximum expiratory pressure above sixty centimeters of water, a peak cough flow above sixty liters per minute, or both. Peak cough flow has three bands. Above one hundred sixty is a normal, strong cough. Sixty to one hundred sixty is weak but possibly adequate, a borderline cough that may need help. Below sixty is inadequate, and the patient is not ready because secretions are likely to be retained. Normal expiratory strength is plus eighty to plus one hundred fifty, so sixty is a floor, not a target. One caution: the same slide says SIMV is no longer recommended for weaning, then adds that you may be tested on it. Know it for the exam.

[01-021 · Recap]
Here is what to carry into the exam. Pneumonia is a clinical diagnosis that needs an infiltrate, and atypical pneumonia means Mycoplasma and a macrolide. Read every film in the same order and check its quality with ripe. White is more stuff, black is more air, and a blunted angle is fluid. A transudate is a pressure problem, an exudate an inflammation problem. Accessory muscle use at rest means increased work of breathing. For weaning, the RSBI must be under one hundred, the general tidal volume at least five milliliters per kilogram, and passing the trial is not the same as being ready to extubate.
