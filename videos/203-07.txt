RCP 203 — Chapter 07: Tracheal Shift, Paradoxical Breathing, Flail Chest

[07-001 · What this chapter covers]
This chapter is about chest findings that point to a mechanical problem. We start with tracheal shift, which way it goes and why. Then paradoxical breathing, where the chest or abdomen moves the wrong way, and flail chest, where a segment of the chest wall moves on its own. We finish with the emergency that ties them together, the tension pneumothorax: how to recognize it, what to recommend and in what order, what the film shows, and needle thoracostomy step by step.

[07-002 · Toward loss, away from pressure]
The deck's memory trick is toward loss, away from pressure. The trachea shifts toward the affected side when lung volume is lost, because the volume loss pulls it, as in atelectasis, fibrosis or a surgical resection. It shifts away from the affected side when something occupies space, because the pressure pushes it, as in a large or tension pneumothorax, a large pleural effusion or a large mass. To check it, RCP 103 says to place an index finger at the sternal notch and move it gently side to side. Normally the trachea is midline above the notch.

[07-003 · Bedside patterns, and a late sign]
The RCP 203 notes give three bedside patterns. A pneumothorax gives a trachea pushed away, hyperresonance and absent breath sounds. A pleural effusion gives a trachea pushed away, decreased or absent fremitus, dullness and absent breath sounds. Atelectasis gives a trachea pulled toward, dullness and absent breath sounds. The nuance: a shift with a pneumothorax generally implies a large or tension pneumothorax, not every pneumothorax, and an effusion must be substantial to shift the trachea. The Trauma deck agrees that deviation away in a pneumothorax is a late and unreliable finding, so do not wait for it, or for a film, before treating a suspected tension pneumothorax in an unstable patient. Hess adds that on a chest film, rotation alone can make the trachea look deviated.

[07-004 · Paradoxical breathing]
The RCP 203 notes define paradoxical breathing as the chest wall or abdominal wall moving in on inspiration and out on exhalation, the opposite of normal. It is a sign of respiratory distress from damage to the structures involved in breathing. Together with dyspnea, tachypnea and accessory muscle use, thoraco-abdominal paradox, the see-saw, signals an abnormally high work of breathing. Hess tells you what each one means. If the abdomen moves in on inspiration, think diaphragm weakness or paralysis. If the chest wall moves in, think paralysis of the chest wall muscles, as in a high thoracic or low cervical spinal cord injury, or a flail segment.

[07-005 · Paradox during weaning]
Paradoxical breathing matters most during weaning. Thoraco-abdominal paradox means you should stop a spontaneous breathing trial, and paradoxical abdominal movement is an early sign of weaning failure. In spinal cord injury and severe neuromuscular disease, the chest moves inward during inhalation. And Hess chapter forty four adds that paradoxical motion of the rib cage and abdomen may indicate impending respiratory failure or diaphragm weakness.

[07-006 · Flail chest: definition and motion]
The RCP 202 Trauma deck defines a flail chest as three or more ribs fractured in two or more places, a sternal fracture, or a costochondral separation, creating an unstable segment of chest wall. Flail refers to that segment's paradoxical motion. On inspiration, the negative intrapleural pressure draws the segment in while the rest of the thorax expands. On exhalation, the opposite happens. One conflict to know: the Trauma deck says three or more ribs, but Hess chapter forty one says two or more, and Hess chapter one just says multiple. Use three or more, from the deck.

[07-007 · Why flail chest causes respiratory failure]
The chain to respiratory failure starts with pain. Pain causes shallow breaths and a poor cough, and those lead to atelectasis and retained secretions. Pulmonary contusions frequently accompany a flail chest and may worsen over the first twenty four to forty eight hours, impairing gas exchange with ventilation perfusion mismatch. Treatment is pain management, pulmonary hygiene, oxygenation, and ventilatory support when necessary, but not every flail patient needs intubation. Hess adds that a contusion is almost certain once a flail segment is found, and often blossoms at forty eight hours, so elective intubation before respiratory failure should be considered early. Other decks list a crushed or flail chest as an indication for controlled ventilation, and as a contraindication to prone positioning.

[07-008 · Practice: a segment that moves the wrong way]
Pause here. After a car crash, part of a patient's right chest wall is drawn in on every inspiration while the rest of the chest expands. What is this called, how does the Trauma deck define it, and why might the patient get worse over the next two days?
[6-second practice pause]

[07-009 · Answer: flail chest, and the contusion]
This is a flail chest. The Trauma deck defines it as three or more ribs fractured in two or more places, a sternal fracture, or a costochondral separation, which leaves an unstable segment that moves paradoxically. The patient may worsen because pulmonary contusions frequently come with it and may get worse over the first twenty four to forty eight hours. Hess says the contusion often blossoms at forty eight hours, so consider elective intubation early, before respiratory failure.

[07-010 · Simple versus tension pneumothorax]
The deck puts it in one line. A simple pneumothorax mainly compromises breathing; a tension pneumothorax compromises both breathing and circulation. The difference is not how much lung is down. It is the one-way valve effect. Air enters the pleural space but cannot adequately escape, so the pressure rises progressively. The mediastinum shifts, venous return falls, cardiac output drops, and the end point is obstructive shock and cardiac arrest.

[07-011 · Signs of a tension pneumothorax]
The deck's clinical signs are sudden respiratory distress or increased work of breathing, decreased chest expansion on the affected side, decreased or absent breath sounds on that side, tracheal deviation away from it, and a hyperresonant percussion note. The chest film shows absent lung markings and radiolucency. On the ventilator, watch for a sudden rise in airway pressure in volume control, or a fall in tidal volume in pressure control. The NBRC pearl, printed verbatim in the deck: tracheal deviation is not required to diagnose tension pneumothorax, and it is a late and unreliable finding. Recognize the combination of respiratory distress, one-sided diminished breath sounds and hemodynamic instability instead of waiting for deviation, or for the film.

[07-012 · What to recommend, in order]
First, a stat chest radiograph, and place the patient on one hundred percent oxygen. If the patient is on positive pressure ventilation, change the settings to minimize peak inspiratory pressure, by lowering the peak pressure or the tidal volume. If tension is suspected and the situation appears life threatening, recommend immediate needle decompression, or a chest tube, on the affected side. Why does one hundred percent oxygen help? It is nitrogen washout applied to the pleural space. Pleural gas is mostly nitrogen. Pure oxygen strips nitrogen out of the alveoli, lowering its partial pressure on the blood side, so the pleural air is reabsorbed faster than on room air. It works best for small, uncomplicated pneumothoraces, and it is no substitute for decompression under tension.

[07-013 · Practice: a sudden change on the vent]
Pause and think. A patient on volume control suddenly becomes distressed. The peak airway pressure jumps, breath sounds are absent on the right, the right side is hyperresonant, and the blood pressure is falling. The trachea is still midline. What do you suspect, does the midline trachea rule it out, and what do you recommend?
[6-second practice pause]

[07-014 · Answer: tension pneumothorax, act now]
Suspect a right tension pneumothorax. A midline trachea does not rule it out, because tracheal deviation is a late and unreliable finding and is not required for the diagnosis. The combination of distress, one-sided absent breath sounds and hyperresonance, a sudden rise in airway pressure on volume control, and hemodynamic instability is enough. Because the situation is life threatening, recommend immediate needle decompression or a chest tube on the affected side, put the patient on one hundred percent oxygen, and lower the peak pressure or tidal volume.

[07-015 · Who gets tension, and what the film shows]
The chapter fifteen deck lists the predisposing factors: mechanical ventilation with high airway pressures, above forty to forty five centimeters of water, chest trauma, and conditions with excessively high lung compliance such as advanced emphysema. Positive pressure makes it worse, because pleural pressure builds fast and compresses the heart, lungs and great vessels. Suspect it when one of those factors comes with a rapid decline: hypotension, hypoxemia or more work of breathing, and on volume control a rapid rise in peak and plateau pressure. Untreated, it leads to shock or pulseless electrical activity. On the affected side, the film shows hyperlucency in the pleural space with a collapsed lung, a flattened diaphragm and widened rib spaces, and the mediastinum shifts to the opposite side.

[07-016 · Needle thoracostomy, step by step]
The deck gives seven steps. One, sit the patient upright if tolerated. Two, the puncture site is the second intercostal space, midclavicular line. Three, prep with Betadine, alcohol, or both. Four, insert the angiocath over the top of the third rib until the hub is against the chest wall. Five, listen for a rush of air and watch the cardiorespiratory status. Six, remove the needle, secure the angiocath, and attach a flutter, or Heimlich, valve. Seven, immediately prepare for a chest tube. Going over the top of the rib is the deck's step. The reason, that the intercostal vessels and nerve run under each rib, is general anatomy, not a deck statement.

[07-017 · The pneumothorax ladder]
The notes give a one-line ladder for every pneumothorax. Small and stable: observe and give oxygen. Moderate: needle aspiration. Large or symptomatic: a chest tube. Tension: emergency needle decompression, then a chest tube. Recurrent: surgery, either video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery or pleurodesis. A Heimlich valve is a one-way valve used on its own, without water seal or suction, and its arrow points away from the patient. The idea that the needle turns a tension pneumothorax into a simple one is general teaching, not a deck statement.

[07-018 · Recap]
Here is what to remember. The trachea shifts toward loss and away from pressure, and in a pneumothorax the shift is late and unreliable. Paradoxical breathing means the wall moves in on inspiration; abdominal paradox points to the diaphragm, and it means stop the breathing trial. A flail chest is three or more ribs broken in two or more places, and pain and contusion drive the respiratory failure. A tension pneumothorax compromises breathing and circulation. Do not wait for deviation or a film in an unstable patient. Recommend a stat film and one hundred percent oxygen, lower the pressures, and decompress at the second intercostal space, midclavicular line, then place a chest tube.
