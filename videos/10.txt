RCP 202 — Chapter 10: Interventional Pulmonary Procedures

[10-001 · Interventional pulmonology: look and treat]
Welcome to section ten, interventional pulmonary procedures. The lesson seven A deck and Hess call bronchoscopy the most commonly used invasive procedure in pulmonary medicine, and it is both diagnostic and therapeutic; Hess adds that about five hundred thousand are done each year in the United States. In this lesson we will cover the diagnostic-versus-therapeutic split, indications and contraindications, flexible versus rigid scopes, sedation and the reversal agents, monitoring, the sampling techniques, the chapter nine key points, complications, and preparation and reprocessing.

[10-002 · Diagnostic vs therapeutic bronchoscopy]
The RCP 105 deck puts it simply: diagnostic means find out what is wrong, or look; therapeutic means fix a problem, or treat. Diagnostic examples are airway inspection, bronchoalveolar lavage, washing, and biopsy, used to identify tumors or infections. Therapeutic examples are removing mucus plugs or foreign bodies, controlling bleeding, and relieving airway obstruction by dilation, stenting, or debulking an endobronchial tumor. The RCP 105 deck says that if a question asks which procedure is both diagnostic and therapeutic, fiberoptic bronchoscopy is an excellent answer. Hess adds a safety rule: during any airway heat therapy, such as laser, electrocautery, or argon plasma coagulation, reduce FiO2 to forty percent or less so flammable components do not ignite.

[10-003 · Indications: nine reasons to scope]
The lesson seven A deck lists nine indications: atelectasis, hemoptysis, cough, suspected malignancy, infection, foreign body aspiration, interstitial lung disease, lung transplant, and trauma. For atelectasis, bronchoscopy is most valuable when a bronchus is obstructed, but try noninvasive airway clearance first, such as bronchodilators when indicated, chest physiotherapy, coughing, and suctioning. For hemoptysis, it identifies the cause, localizes the bleeding, and looks for endobronchial cancer. Foreign body aspiration is common in children; the classic triad is sudden paroxysmal coughing, wheezing, and diminished breath sounds on one side. Rigid bronchoscopy is often preferred for large objects and remains the instrument of choice in children. Hess adds that transbronchial biopsy is the gold standard for acute transplant rejection, and bronchoscopy remains the standard for diagnosing tracheobronchial injury after blunt trauma.

[10-004 · Contraindications and bleeding risk]
Three concerns dominate: oxygenation, hemodynamic stability, and bleeding risk. The AARC criteria in the RCP 203 deck say to recommend against bronchoscopy if the patient cannot be adequately oxygenated, has a bleeding disorder that cannot be corrected, has severe obstructive airway disease, has severe refractory hypoxemia, or is hemodynamically unstable. Hess adds numbers: a PaO2 under seventy millimeters of mercury despite supplemental oxygen is generally considered a contraindication; the British Thoracic Society, quoted by Hess, says to defer at least six weeks after a myocardial infarction; avoid bronchoscopy in status asthmaticus; and platelets should be above fifty thousand for transbronchial biopsy, with a minimum of twenty to fifty thousand for fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Normal PT is eleven to thirteen point five seconds, and normal PTT is twenty-five to thirty-five seconds.

[10-005 · Flexible vs rigid bronchoscope]
The flexible fiberoptic or video scope has a soft shaft three point five to six millimeters wide, and its tip rotates up to two hundred ten degrees; the RCP 105 deck instead says it bends about one hundred eighty degrees up and one hundred thirty down, but use two hundred ten for this topic. It usually goes in through the nose under local anesthesia with sedation and reaches the smaller, distal airways for biopsies and lavage. The rigid scope is a straight stainless-steel tube, nine to thirteen millimeters wide in adults, placed through the mouth with the neck hyperextended under general anesthesia, and it gives definitive airway control. Rigid is chosen for severe central obstruction, large foreign bodies, significant bleeding, silicone stents, and laser resection. It is also the instrument of choice for a foreign body in a child.

[10-006 · Sedation drugs and reversal agents]
Three drug names, and two antidotes, because one of them has none. Midazolam, trade name Versed, is a benzodiazepine giving sedation, anxiolysis, and amnesia, but no pain relief; it is reversed by flumazenil, which can precipitate seizures, so it is not given routinely to speed recovery. Fentanyl, trade name Sublimaze, is an opioid for analgesia and is reversed by naloxone, trade name Narcan. Fentanyl is often combined with midazolam, which raises the risk of respiratory depression. Propofol, trade name Diprivan, is a sedative-hypnotic with rapid onset; it causes respiratory depression and hypotension, has no analgesia, and has no reversal agent.

[10-007 · Topical lidocaine and atropine]
Hess says lidocaine is the most commonly used topical agent because of its favorable safety profile and short half-life. The RCP 104 deck adds that it blocks sodium channels, and it suppresses cough and numbs the mucosa. The doses differ by source. The RCP 105 deck gives five to ten milliliters of a one to four percent solution, by aerosol or instilled down the working channel starting at the vocal cords; Hess gives two to three milliliter aliquots of one to two percent. Use the RCP 105 deck's dose on this exam. Hess caps the total dose at five to seven milligrams per kilogram. Atropine, given intramuscularly, reduces vagal response, oral secretions, and bronchospasm. Hess notes many centers have dropped it, but for what atropine does, use the deck answer.

[10-008 · Prompt: match the reversal agent]
Pause and think. A patient has received midazolam and fentanyl for bronchoscopy, and now the breathing slows and the patient is hard to arouse. Which reversal agent matches each of those two drugs? What caution comes with the benzodiazepine reversal agent? And if the sedative had been propofol instead, what reversal agent would you reach for?
[6-second practice pause]

[10-009 · Answer: flumazenil, naloxone, none]
Fentanyl is the opioid, so it is reversed by naloxone, trade name Narcan. Midazolam is the benzodiazepine, so it is reversed by flumazenil, but flumazenil can precipitate seizures, so it is not given routinely just to speed recovery. Propofol is neither an opioid nor a benzodiazepine, and it has no reversal agent, so you support the airway and breathing instead. Hess warns that benzodiazepines and opioids together potentiate respiratory depression, so keep naloxone and flumazenil ready. The deck's memory trick: fentanyl, pain, opioid, naloxone; Versed, sedation and amnesia, benzo, flumazenil; propofol, neither, no reversal.

[10-010 · Monitoring during and after the scope]
During the procedure, the RT sets up continuous SpO2, ECG and heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate monitoring, plus capnography when indicated by the sedation protocol. Transient hypoxemia occurs in up to thirty-five percent of patients and is often relieved with supplemental oxygen. The RCP 105 deck allows up to six liters per minute for spontaneously breathing patients without an artificial airway; Hess gives two to three liters per minute to everyone, but use the RCP 105 number on this exam. On a ventilator, the scope in the tube raises airway resistance, so peak pressure may rise, delivered tidal volume may fall, FiO2 may be raised, often to one hundred percent, and alarms may need temporary adjustment. Afterward, monitor vital signs at least two hours or until stable, with continuous pulse oximetry.

[10-011 · COPD, emphysema, GERD, and lung cancer]
COPD is the umbrella diagnosis: progressive, irreversible airflow limitation, confirmed by a post-bronchodilator FEV1 over FVC below zero point seven. Emphysema is its alveolar-destruction half; the other half, chronic bronchitis, is a productive cough for at least three months in each of two consecutive years. A scabbard trachea can be seen in emphysema, and bronchoscopically placed one-way valves let trapped air out in selected advanced cases, with pneumothorax the major complication. GERD is reflux of stomach contents into the esophagus and one of the most common causes of chronic cough. Hess calls bronchoscopy the procedure most commonly used to diagnose lung cancer, with biopsy, and EBUS-TBNA for staging. The study guide notes the deck never explains why these four are grouped here.

[10-012 · BAL, washing, brushing, biopsy]
Four sampling techniques, plus needle aspiration. Bronchoalveolar lavage wedges the scope in a segmental or subsegmental bronchus, then instills and suctions back saline to sample the distal airways and alveoli, for pneumonia, opportunistic infections, and some interstitial diseases. Hess keeps the total at two hundred milliliters or less and suction under one hundred twenty centimeters of water. Washing instills saline without wedging and samples the larger airways. The standard brush collects surface cells for cytology; the protected brush collects organisms with less upper-airway contamination. Endobronchial biopsy uses forceps on a visible airway lesion; transbronchial lung biopsy samples parenchyma beyond the airway wall. Transbronchial needle aspiration passes a needle through the wall to sample beyond the airway, including lymph nodes, guided in real time by endobronchial ultrasound.

[10-013 · Hess chapter 9 key points and thoracentesis]
Hess chapter nine key points: bronchoscopy is safe and effective, and both diagnostic and therapeutic. The ideal fiberoptic patient is awake and cooperative. The deck's version of key point nine says rigid bronchoscopy is mainly for complex therapeutic work and typically needs general anesthesia. Stents are self-expandable metal, silicone, or hybrid, and one-way valves treat advanced emphysema. Hess calls pleural fluid exudative if the pleural-to-serum protein ratio is at least zero point five, or the LDH ratio is at least zero point six; the deck instead teaches transudate as a pressure problem and exudate as inflammation, infection, or malignancy, so know both. The deck recommends ultrasound guidance for thoracentesis, and Hess considers removing fifteen hundred milliliters at a time safe. A pH under seven point two zero with glucose under sixty milligrams per deciliter may indicate a complicated parapneumonic effusion.

[10-014 · Prompt: which complication is most likely?]
Pause and think. During a routine flexible bronchoscopy, which complication does the RCP 105 deck call the one you are most likely to see? And for a spontaneously breathing patient without an artificial airway, how many liters per minute of oxygen does that deck allow?
[6-second practice pause]

[10-015 · Answer: hypoxemia, up to 6 L/min]
Hypoxemia is the complication you are most likely to see, in up to thirty-five percent of patients. If the saturation falls, the RT raises the oxygen, tells the physician, and the procedure may be paused. The RCP 105 deck allows oxygen up to six liters per minute for a spontaneously breathing patient without an artificial airway; Hess says two to three, but use six on this exam. Pneumothorax occurs in one to six percent, mainly after transbronchial biopsy; suspect it with sudden dyspnea, chest pain, and decreased breath sounds. Significant hemorrhage, more than fifty milliliters, occurs in two to nine percent; airway and oxygenation come first, then iced saline, epinephrine, or thrombin through the working channel. Epinephrine strengths differ: the RCP 105 slide lists one to one thousand, its notes dilute to one to ten thousand, and Hess uses one to twenty thousand. If asked what the slide lists, answer one to one thousand.

[10-016 · Prep, recovery, and reprocessing]
Before the procedure, the physician obtains consent before sedatives are given, and the RT reviews vital signs, oxygenation, allergies, and bleeding risks. The deck says NPO, typically six to eight hours; Hess describes fasting after midnight, but use the deck's six to eight hours. Afterward, monitor vital signs at least two hours or until stable, maintain SpO2 with oxygen, and withhold food or drink until the gag reflex returns, because lidocaine numbs the throat. Hess adds no driving for twenty-four hours. Stridor gets racemic epinephrine; wheezing gets a bronchodilator. The RCP 203 deck calls the bronchoscope semicritical. The RCP 105 deck says sterilize it, but for an NBRC-style question, high-level disinfection is acceptable. Glutaraldehyde gives high-level disinfection in twenty minutes at room temperature; true sterilization takes ten hours. Biopsy forceps are critical and must be sterilized.

[10-017 · Recap: scope, sample, protect]
Bronchoscopy is diagnostic and therapeutic. Flexible for routine diagnostic work, rigid for central airway control and large foreign bodies. Sedate with midazolam, fentanyl, or propofol; reverse midazolam with flumazenil and fentanyl with naloxone, and remember propofol has no reversal. Lidocaine is the topical anesthetic, and Hess caps the total at five to seven milligrams per kilogram. BAL samples the alveolar space, washing samples airways, brushing samples surface cells, biopsy samples tissue, and TBNA samples lymph nodes. The complication to anticipate is hypoxemia; the complications to watch for are pneumothorax and bleeding. Keep FiO2 at forty percent or less during any airway heat therapy.
