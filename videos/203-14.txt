RCP 203 — Chapter 14: Artificial Airways and the Transtracheal O₂ Catheter

[14-001 · What this chapter covers]
This chapter covers the artificial airways, from the simplest to the definitive, and what to do when one fails. We start with the transtracheal oxygen catheter. Then the basic adjuncts, the oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways. Then the supraglottic rescue airways: the laryngeal mask airway, the King laryngeal tube and the Combitube. Then tracheostomy tubes and the tracheostomy button. We finish with emergencies: a blocked tube, an accidental extubation or decannulation, and a failed intubation.

[14-002 · The transtracheal oxygen catheter]
No deck in the vault covers this, so it comes from Hess chapter twenty five. A transtracheal oxygen catheter is placed surgically to bypass the upper airway. It goes through a small hole in the front of the neck into the trachea. It is used to conserve oxygen: because the upper airway dead space is bypassed, the patient gets the same effect as continuous-flow oxygen by nasal cannula at a lower flow setting. Hess lists it with the oxygen conserving devices and classes it as a low-flow, variable FiO2 device. Another benefit is better adherence, because it can be hidden from view. One trap: Hess's emergency needle cricothyrotomy, a twelve to sixteen gauge catheter through the cricothyroid membrane, is a different device, and Hess does not call it transtracheal.

[14-003 · The oropharyngeal airway: who gets one]
An oropharyngeal airway keeps the tongue off the back of the pharynx. Use it only in an unconscious patient without an intact gag reflex. In a conscious or semi-conscious patient with a gag reflex, it causes gagging, vomiting and aspiration, so consider a nasopharyngeal airway instead. It also serves as a bite block, it may make bag-valve-mask ventilation more effective, and it does not enter the larynx or the esophagus. There are two types: the Berman, rigid throughout, and the Guedel, reinforced in the bite region.

[14-004 · Sizing and inserting an OPA]
RCP 203 sizes it from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw, by anatomy, not by age alone. Hess sizes it on the cheek, with the tip no farther than the ear, but use the deck. Too large may press the epiglottis against the larynx or traumatize the airway, and too small may push the tongue back. In an adult, insert it curved upward, advance to the base of the tongue, then rotate it one hundred eighty degrees, or insert it from the side and rotate ninety. In infants and small children, do not rotate. RCP 203 says to depress the tongue with a tongue depressor and insert it directly. And do not tape over the center opening, because that is the path for a suction catheter.

[14-005 · The nasopharyngeal airway]
The nasopharyngeal airway is the alternative. It can be used in conscious or semi-conscious patients with an intact gag reflex, and it is better tolerated because it does not stimulate the back of the pharynx. Its tip rests behind the tongue, just above the epiglottis. It bypasses an upper airway obstruction, helps pass a bronchoscope, and serves as a path for nasotracheal suctioning, with less trauma from repeated suctioning. RCP 203 sizes it from the nares to the earlobe or tragus, choosing the largest diameter that passes without force. Lubricate it with water-soluble jelly and advance it along the floor of the nose, bevel toward the septum, and never force it. The major contraindication is a suspected basilar skull fracture or significant midface trauma. Complications are laryngospasm, coughing, nosebleeds, and sinus infection with prolonged use.

[14-006 · Practice: which adjunct?]
Pause and decide. A patient does not arouse but does cough, and no intubation equipment is at hand. Which basic airway do you choose, and what is the one injury that would rule it out?
[6-second practice pause]

[14-007 · Answer: a nasopharyngeal airway]
Choose a nasopharyngeal airway. The cough tells you a gag reflex is present, so an oropharyngeal airway would cause gagging, vomiting and aspiration. The nasal airway is tolerated with a gag reflex. This is the deck's own stop and think example. The injury that rules it out is a suspected basilar skull fracture, or significant midface trauma.

[14-008 · The laryngeal mask airway]
The laryngeal mask airway is a supraglottic airway: a tube and mask with an inflatable cuff, inserted blindly into the pharynx. The mask seals around the laryngeal inlet above the glottis, and it does not enter the trachea. It is used for routine airway management during general anesthesia and as a rescue device in a difficult or emergency airway, and it ventilates better than a bag-valve-mask with less gastric insufflation. Its main limit is less protection from aspiration than a cuffed endotracheal tube.

[14-009 · LMA: size, cuff and problems]
The RCP 203 deck sizes the laryngeal mask airway by weight: size four for fifty to seventy kilograms, size five for seventy to one hundred. Hess sizes it by build, but use the deck's weights. The cuff can go up to sixty centimeters of water, compared with twenty to thirty for an endotracheal tube. With positive pressure, squeeze the bag slowly and keep pressures below twenty to thirty. If it leaks, check the position; don't just add cuff air. Secure it with a bite block, not an oral airway, which causes malposition. If the patient regurgitates, do not remove it. Tilt the patient head-down or onto the side, disconnect the ventilation, suction, and prepare to intubate.

[14-010 · The airway ladder and the King LT]
The chapter fifteen deck gives a ladder. Oral and nasal airways are basic adjuncts. The King laryngeal tube, the Combitube and the laryngeal mask airway are supraglottic rescue airways. The endotracheal tube is the definitive tracheal airway. All three rescue airways are placed blindly, not through the vocal cords. The King LT has two cuffs, one inflation line that fills both, and one connector. Its tip normally ends up in the esophagus. The distal cuff blocks the esophagus, the proximal cuff seals the pharynx, and gas leaves through openings between the cuffs, in front of the larynx. The standard model dead-ends at the esophageal cuff, and the LT-S adds a gastric access lumen. Both pass an airway exchange catheter or a fiberoptic scope.

[14-011 · The Combitube: blue first]
The esophageal tracheal Combitube has two lumens, two cuffs and two connectors. The blue pharyngeal lumen has a large cuff, side holes and a dead end. The clear lumen has a regular cuff and an open tip. The Combitube usually lands in the esophagus, so ventilate through the blue lumen first, and gas exits the side holes toward the larynx. If the tip went into the trachea instead, switch to the clear lumen, which then works like an endotracheal tube. The exam anchor is blue first: esophagus, blue lumen; trachea, clear lumen. Then confirm with chest rise, breath sounds on both sides and end-tidal carbon dioxide. The King LT has no blue-first decision, because it has one connector and is designed to sit in the esophagus. The laryngeal mask, by contrast, sits in the pyriform sinus over the laryngeal opening, above the cords.

[14-012 · Practice: the Combitube]
Pause here. You place a Combitube and ventilate through the blue lumen, but there is no chest rise, no breath sounds, and no exhaled carbon dioxide. Where is the tip most likely to be, and what do you do?
[6-second practice pause]

[14-013 · Answer: the tip is in the trachea]
Blue first assumes the tip went into the esophagus, where gas from the blue lumen's side holes flows toward the larynx. If ventilating the blue lumen gives no chest rise, no breath sounds and no carbon dioxide, the tip has most likely entered the trachea. Switch to the clear lumen, which then works like an endotracheal tube, and confirm again with chest rise, breath sounds on both sides and end-tidal carbon dioxide.

[14-014 · Tracheostomy tubes]
A tracheostomy is the surgical introduction of a tube into the trachea through the neck. Tracheotomy is the procedure; tracheostomy is the opening. There are three main reasons: to bypass an upper airway obstruction, to help remove secretions, and for long-term mechanical ventilation, probably the most common. Because it bypasses the upper airway, warming, humidification and filtration are lost. Tubes are sized by internal diameter, but fit depends on the outer diameter, which should be no more than two thirds to three quarters of the trachea's internal diameter. Remove the obturator right after insertion and keep it at the bedside, with a spare inner cannula. A speaking valve or a capped fenestrated tube needs the cuff fully deflated. Never cap with the cuff up.

[14-015 · The tracheostomy button]
A tracheostomy button keeps the stoma open without a full tube. It is a short cannula with a flared outer end, a flanged inner end, and spacers to set the depth. When it is plugged, the patient breathes and coughs through the upper airway, and with a one-way valve the patient can talk, eat and cough. But positive pressure through a button leaks. It is not a secure cuffed airway, and Hess notes that it has no cuff.

[14-016 · A blocked tube: the first step]
An endotracheal or tracheostomy tube can be blocked by kinking, by the patient biting the tube, by the tip resting against the tracheal wall, mainly with trach tubes, or by thick secretions, mucus or blood clots in the lumen. Rarely, the cuff herniates over the tip, or an overinflated cuff compresses the tube. The first step, if the patient is on the ventilator, is to remove the patient from the ventilator, ventilate manually with one hundred percent oxygen, and reassess. If one is immediately available, try passing an airway exchange catheter through the blocked tube before removing it. For a trach patient, you may need to cover the stoma with a petroleum gauze pad and ventilate through the upper airway.

[14-017 · Accidental extubation or decannulation]
A partial extubation can mimic a blown cuff, so first rule out a large cuff leak by checking the cuff, the pilot tube and the valve. For a partial extubation, deflate the cuff, loosen the holder and try to reposition the tube into the trachea. Confirm by listening, by capnography or a carbon dioxide colorimeter, an esophageal detector or a scope, then a chest film. If that fails, remove the tube, ventilate by bag-valve-mask with oxygen, and consider a supraglottic airway or reintubation. If a tracheostomy tube comes out of a fresh stoma, call for help, cover the stoma with a sterile petroleum gauze pad, and bag the patient with oxygen as needed. With a well-established stoma in a stable patient, get a sterile tube of the same size or one size smaller and follow the trach change procedure.

[14-018 · Failed intubation: the pathway]
A failed intubation means the tube cannot be placed after several attempts, some sources say three. The signs are absent breath sounds, abdominal distention with sounds over the stomach during bagging, no exhaled carbon dioxide, breaths that are hard to deliver, a rapid fall in saturation, clinical deterioration, and stomach contents in the tube. Oxygen by nasal cannula during the attempts helps hold the saturation up. After any failed attempt, bag with one hundred percent oxygen. If bagging keeps the saturation above ninety percent, allow up to two more attempts by the most experienced clinician, preferably with rapid sequence intubation. If the saturation is below ninety or falling, you cannot intubate and cannot oxygenate. Insert a supraglottic airway immediately and restart bagging. If either pathway fails, the only alternative is an emergency surgical airway.

[14-019 · Recap]
Here is what to remember. An oral airway is only for a patient with no gag reflex, sized from the mouth corner to the jaw angle; a nasal airway is tolerated with a gag reflex but never with a suspected basilar skull fracture or significant midface trauma. The laryngeal mask seals above the glottis, is sized by weight, and protects less against aspiration. With a Combitube, ventilate blue first, and switch to clear if the tip is in the trachea. A tracheostomy must have its cuff down before it is capped, and a button has no cuff. For a blocked tube, take the patient off the ventilator and bag with one hundred percent oxygen. And when you cannot intubate and cannot oxygenate, place a supraglottic airway now.
