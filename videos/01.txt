RCP 202 — Chapter 01: Weaning Parameters and Extubation Assessment

[01-001 · What this chapter covers]
Welcome. In this chapter we build the full weaning and extubation picture. We wean the patient, not the ventilator. That means the best predictor of success is improvement of the underlying disease. We will walk through the readiness screen, the four groups of weaning criteria, and then every classic bedside parameter: sensorium, chest film, blood counts, MIP, vital capacity, RSBI, minute ventilation, blood gas and oxygenation thresholds, hemodynamics, and pulmonary mechanics. Then we tackle the spontaneous breathing trial, secretions and cough, and the cuff leak test. Keep this principle front and center: no single value decides weaning readiness.

[01-002 · The readiness screen]
Before any weaning parameter is measured, the patient must pass a readiness screen. The deck lists five items. There must be some reversal of the underlying cause of respiratory failure. Oxygenation must be adequate, meaning P over F at least one hundred fifty to two hundred, PEEP five to eight or less, and FiO2 at or below point four to point five. The pH must be at or above seven point two five. The patient must be hemodynamically stable, with no active ischemia or significant hypotension. And finally the patient must be able to initiate an inspiratory effort. Hess warns that no single parameter beats an actual spontaneous breathing trial, and over reliance on numbers prolongs ventilation.

[01-003 · Weaning success versus failure]
Memorize these three definitions. Weaning success means no ventilatory support for at least forty eight hours after extubation. Oxygen, bronchodilators, pressure support, and CPAP during that period do not disqualify success. Weaning in progress means the patient is extubated but still on noninvasive ventilation. Weaning failure is a failed spontaneous breathing trial, or reintubation within forty eight hours. Hess adds that extubation failure, meaning the need to reintubate, happens in five to fifteen percent of patients overall. Keep that number in mind, because reintubation is itself associated with worse outcomes.

[01-004 · Sensorium and GCS]
Sensorium is the deck's term for level of consciousness. You check it by asking time, place, and person. Oriented times three means fully alert. The Glasgow Coma Scale is called the most objective measure, with scores from three to fifteen. Use mild thirteen to fifteen, moderate nine to twelve, severe eight or less. A GCS of eight or less is a trigger to intubate for airway protection. Why does this matter for weaning? Vital capacity and MIP are effort dependent. A heavily sedated patient looks weak when they are only snowed. Diminished mental status is also a stopping sign during an SBT, so cooperation really is a bedside parameter.

[01-005 · Sedation and cooperation at extubation]
Hess adds practical hooks. Daily spontaneous awakening trials paired with SBTs shorten time on the ventilator. Patients who cannot complete four simple tasks, which are opening the eyes, following with the eyes, grasping your hand, and sticking out the tongue, are more than four times as likely to fail extubation. For sedation, dexmedetomidine, trade name Precedex, provides sedation without respiratory depression, so it can be continued through extubation. After neuromuscular blockade, confirm recovery with three bedside tests: a five second head lift, a five second tongue protrusion, and a hand grip. There is no numeric GCS threshold stated for extubation itself; the deck gives only the airway-protection trigger.

[01-006 · CXR in weaning]
The chest film evaluates thoracic structure: lungs, pleural space, catheters, and tubes. No source gives a CXR cutoff that gates weaning, but the film tells you which problems to look for. Sudden drops in compliance point to tension pneumothorax, pleural effusion, or the endotracheal tube slipping into a mainstem bronchus. Gradual drops suggest pneumonia, ARDS, or atelectasis. The memory trick is: sudden, search for the problem; gradual, treat the disease. For tube position, chest film or fiberoptic laryngoscopy are the only confirmations of placement above the carina. The deck sets the tip four to six centimeters above the carina, usually between T2 and T4.

[01-007 · CBC: Hb, Hct, WBC]
No source states a CBC cutoff that gates weaning, but the complete blood count matters because anemia lowers oxygen carrying capacity even when oxygenation looks normal. Normals from the clinical data deck: hemoglobin twelve to sixteen grams per deciliter, hematocrit forty to fifty percent, red cells four to six million per cubic millimeter, white cells five to ten thousand per cubic millimeter. The deck says septic, anemic, or malnourished patients fail weaning even on perfect settings. The one deck number is: in ventilated patients, correct anemia when hemoglobin falls below ten. For infection, white count rises with bacterial pneumonia and may fall with viral; VAP shows leukocytosis or leukopenia plus fever and infiltrate.

[01-008 · MIP or NIF: muscle strength]
MIP, also called NIF, measures inspiratory muscle strength, mostly the diaphragm. The patient inhales against an occluded manometer for about twenty to twenty five seconds using a one way valve, so each breath is more negative than the last. Remember the sign: more negative is stronger. Normal is minus eighty to minus one hundred. The weaning threshold on this course is minus twenty to minus thirty centimeters of water or more negative. Minus twenty is the floor every source agrees on. Hess cautions that even minus twenty may not be enough when resistance is high or compliance low. During neuromuscular blockade, the diaphragm is the last muscle paralyzed and the first to recover.

[01-009 · Vital capacity]
Vital capacity is the maximum exhalation after a maximum inspiration. It combines inspiratory reserve, tidal volume, and expiratory reserve, so it reflects both lung volume and muscle strength. Normal is roughly fifty milliliters per kilogram, around four to five liters in adults. The weaning threshold on this exam is greater than ten milliliters per kilogram. Hess and some RCP 203 notes offer fifteen milliliters per kilogram, but use ten. VC is effort dependent, so sedated or paralyzed patients cannot give you a real number. At the bedside, let the patient breathe spontaneously for three minutes, attach a respirometer, have them inhale deeply and exhale fully, then reconnect. Record the best of three slow vital capacity attempts.

[01-010 · RSBI formula and cutoff]
The rapid shallow breathing index is rate divided by size. You take the respiratory frequency in breaths per minute and divide by the average spontaneous tidal volume in liters. The deck cutoff is less than one hundred breaths per minute per liter. Hess and many NBRC protocols say less than one hundred five. Why the units matter: if you divide by milliliters instead of liters, the answer is nonsense. Rapid shallow breathing hurts because anatomic dead space is unchanged while tidal volume falls, which raises the dead space fraction and the ventilatory demand. Slow deep breaths are good; fast small breaths are bad.

[01-011 · RSBI worked examples]
Let us run two examples. Example one: the patient breathes thirty eight times per minute with an average tidal volume of three hundred milliliters. Convert to liters, zero point three. Divide: thirty eight over zero point three equals one hundred twenty seven. That is above one hundred, so the index predicts weaning failure. Example two: minute ventilation ten liters at a rate of twenty five. Average tidal volume is ten divided by twenty five, which is zero point four liters. RSBI is twenty five divided by zero point four, which equals sixty two point five. That is under one hundred, a good weaning candidate. Trap: if you divide by four hundred milliliters you get zero point zero six, which is meaningless.

[01-012 · Minute ventilation, VT, and rate]
The deck normals are: minute ventilation six to ten liters per minute, spontaneous tidal volume five to ten milliliters per kilogram, spontaneous rate twelve to twenty, PaCO2 thirty five to forty five. For weaning, a minute ventilation above ten liters is a sign of excessive work and poor outcomes, because high demand means a loaded respiratory pump. The minimum spontaneous tidal volume consistent with success is five milliliters per kilogram. Hess collects the classic predictors as spontaneous VT five, rate thirty, minute ventilation twelve, VC fifteen, and PImax minus twenty. Use the deck's ten liter ceiling on this exam, and recognize Hess's twelve. The NHLBI ARDS protocol accepts VT at or above four milliliters per kilogram during its SBT.

[01-013 · Oxygenation thresholds]
The oxygenation question is: does this patient still need a lot of oxygen and PEEP? If yes, they are not ready. The weaning deck lists PaO2 above sixty or SaO2 above ninety on FiO2 of point four or less, P over F at or above one hundred fifty, shunt under twenty percent, and A minus a gradient under three hundred fifty on one hundred percent oxygen. P over F categories: above three hundred eighty normal, under three hundred mild ARDS, under two hundred ARDS, under one hundred fifty prone. Shunt over twenty percent must be corrected before any weaning attempt. During an SBT, keep SpO2 at or above eighty five to ninety, pH at or above seven point three, and PaCO2 rise ten torr or less.

[01-014 · Do not chase a low PaCO2]
A trap worth repeating. If the patient hyperventilates and PaCO2 drops during weaning, do not reflexively drop the support. Low PaCO2 may be driven by hypoxia, pain, anxiety, or wrong settings. If you reduce support to chase the gas, work of breathing climbs and the patient fatigues. Find the cause instead. After neuromuscular blockade, the pre-extubation targets in the deck are PaO2 above eighty and PaCO2 under forty five, with COPD patients compared to their own baseline. On this exam, follow the deck's P over F of one fifty as the oxygenation screen, and recognize Hess's one hundred fifty to two hundred range.

[01-015 · Hemodynamic stability]
Hemodynamic stability means no active myocardial ischemia and no significant hypotension. The weaning deck requires acceptable vital signs before any trial. During the spontaneous breathing trial, keep heart rate below one twenty to one forty with a change under twenty percent, systolic between ninety and one eighty to two hundred with the same twenty percent limit, and no vasopressors or only low dose pressors. Clinical signs of failure include tachycardia, hypertension, hypotension, and arrhythmias. Hess adds a quiet trap. A patient with marginal left ventricular function may tolerate CPAP because positive pressure unloads the failing heart, and then flash into pulmonary edema after extubation when the pressure comes off.

[01-016 · Pulmonary mechanics: Cst, VD/VT, Raw]
These three are effort independent. Static compliance equals tidal volume divided by plateau pressure minus PEEP. The weaning value is above thirty milliliters per centimeter of water. Normal intubated adult compliance runs forty to eighty. Dead space fraction uses the Enghoff modification of the Bohr equation: PaCO2 minus the mean mixed exhaled CO2, divided by PaCO2. Weaning target is less than sixty percent. A worked example: PaCO2 forty, PECO2 twenty seven, so thirteen over forty equals zero point three two five, about normal. Airway resistance equals PIP minus plateau divided by flow in liters per second. Normal is zero point six to two point four, higher on the vent because of the tube.

[01-017 · The spontaneous breathing trial]
The SBT is the single best bedside test. The deck says up to thirty minutes, because patients who fail do so in the first twenty to thirty. RCP 203 and Hess allow thirty to one hundred twenty. Methods have similar success: T piece, pressure support five to eight, CPAP about five, or automatic tube compensation. SIMV is not a stand alone weaning mode. Hess's twenty seventeen guideline suggests starting with pressure support of five to eight for patients ventilated more than twenty four hours. Pass criteria are a normal pattern, adequate gas exchange, and hemodynamic stability. Failure signs are agitation, diminished mental status, diaphoresis, cyanosis, rising work, tachypnea, accessory muscle use, and abdominal paradox.

[01-018 · Quick check: RSBI]
A spontaneous respirometer reading shows a minute ventilation of twelve liters at a rate of forty. Pause and compute the rapid shallow breathing index. Does the number predict a pass or a fail by the deck cutoff?
[6-second practice pause]

[01-019 · Answer: RSBI calculation]
Tidal volume equals twelve divided by forty, which is zero point three liters. RSBI is forty divided by zero point three, which equals one hundred thirty three. That is above the deck's cutoff of one hundred and above the NBRC one hundred five, so the patient fails. Rapid shallow breathing predicts weaning failure.

[01-020 · Secretions, cough, and airway protection]
Passing the SBT answers the breathing question. Extubation also requires that the patient protect the airway. RCP 203 says remove the tube only if the patient maintains oxygenation and ventilation without support, is at minimal risk for upper airway obstruction, protects against aspiration, and can clear secretions. Cough strength is tracked with maximum expiratory pressure. Normal is sixty to one hundred, and under forty is an ineffective cough. Hess adds hard numbers: cough peak flow at or below sixty liters per minute multiplies failure risk by five, secretions above two point five milliliters per hour triples risk, and suctioning more than every two hours should preclude extubation.

[01-021 · Cuff leak test and stridor]
Before extubating a patient at risk for upper airway swelling, deflate the cuff on positive pressure and listen for a leak around the tube, or measure a leak volume. Risk factors include traumatic intubation, intubation longer than six days, a large tube, female sex, and reintubation after unplanned extubation. The test has false positives and negatives, and deflating the cuff raises aspiration risk. If the test fails, the twenty seventeen ATS and ACCP guideline suggests systemic steroids at least four hours before extubation. After extubation, if stridor develops give cool humidified oxygen and aerosolized racemic epinephrine. The most serious complication of extubation is laryngospasm.

[01-022 · Quick check: deck thresholds]
Name the deck's weaning threshold for each of the following parameters: static compliance, dead space fraction, vital capacity, maximum inspiratory pressure, and minute ventilation ceiling. Try to call out each number before the next slide.
[6-second practice pause]

[01-023 · Answer: deck thresholds]
Here are the deck values. Static compliance greater than thirty milliliters per centimeter of water. Dead space fraction less than sixty percent. Vital capacity greater than ten milliliters per kilogram. Maximum inspiratory pressure minus twenty to minus thirty centimeters of water or more negative. Minute ventilation ceiling ten liters per minute above which weaning tends to fail. These are the Chang deck numbers; recognize Hess variants of fifteen for VC and twelve for minute ventilation, but default to the deck on this exam.

[01-024 · Why patients fail weaning]
Weaning failure comes from three mechanisms: rising airway resistance, falling compliance, and respiratory muscle fatigue. Resistance climbs with a small tube, secretions, kinking, biting, circuit condensation, or bronchospasm; everything except bronchospasm is mechanical. A standard endotracheal tube raises work of breathing by fifty four to two hundred forty percent. Compliance drops suddenly from tension pneumothorax, effusion, or mainstem migration, and gradually from pneumonia, ARDS, or atelectasis. Fatigue is driven by poor oxygen delivery, electrolyte derangements such as low potassium, phosphate, calcium, and magnesium, undernutrition that depletes diaphragm substrate, and overfeeding that raises CO2 production. After fatigue, Hess suggests a twenty four hour rest.

[01-025 · Disuse and auto-PEEP]
Two extra traps. Diaphragm disuse atrophy can appear in as little as twenty four hours of fully controlled ventilation. Retrain the diaphragm with short T-tube trials or pressure support, not with prolonged full rest. Auto-PEEP in COPD raises the pleural pressure the patient must generate to trigger a breath, so it directly delays liberation. The deck also reminds us that fever and infection raise both oxygen consumption and CO2 production, which increase ventilatory demand independent of lung mechanics. Fix the drivers, do not just chase the numbers.

[01-026 · Recap: weaning and extubation]
To recap. First screen for reversal of cause, oxygenation, pH, hemodynamics, and effort. Then check the four groups of parameters: clinical readiness, ventilatory reserve, oxygenation, and pulmonary reserve. Memorize the deck numbers: RSBI under one hundred, VT at least five, VE under ten, VC over ten, MIP more negative than minus twenty, P over F at least one fifty, pH at least seven two five, Cst over thirty, VD over VT under sixty percent. Confirm readiness with an SBT up to thirty minutes. Then ask the separate airway protection question: cough, secretions, upper airway swelling. Weaning success is forty eight hours off, and extubation failure is five to fifteen percent.
