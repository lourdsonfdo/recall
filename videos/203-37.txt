RCP 203 — Chapter 37: Mechanical Ventilation, part 4: Compliance, Humidity, Cuffs, I:E and ARDSNet

[37-001 · What this chapter covers]
This is the last part of mechanical ventilation. We start with static and dynamic compliance: how to calculate each, and which normal range to use. Then minute versus alveolar ventilation, relative humidity and rain-out, and cuff pressures. Then the equation of motion, and the I to E ratio: how to compute it, how to set the flow to reach an ordered ratio, and why you usually lower it. We finish with the ARDSNet protocol, step by step.

[37-002 · Static compliance]
Start with the pressures. Peak minus plateau is the pressure spent overcoming airway resistance. Plateau minus PEEP is the pressure that distends the lung and chest wall. Which segment changes tells you which problem you have. Compliance is the change in volume divided by the change in pressure, and the deck's rule is simple: use the peak for dynamic compliance and the plateau for static. Static compliance is measured with no airflow: tidal volume divided by plateau minus PEEP. Chapter eleven treats C stat and C R S as interchangeable at the bedside, and says that in a static compliance table you disregard the peak; it is there to confuse you. For example, six hundred divided by twenty seven minus ten is thirty five point three.

[37-003 · Dynamic compliance, and a worked example]
Dynamic compliance uses the peak instead: tidal volume divided by peak minus PEEP. Heuer prints the same formula and adds that dynamic compliance is always less than static, the difference being airway resistance. The peak includes the resistive pressure, so whenever flow and resistance are present, dynamic is lower. Hess's worked example: a tidal volume of three hundred twenty, a plateau of forty, and a PEEP of twenty six. Three hundred twenty divided by fourteen is about twenty three milliliters per centimeter of water, which is low. With a peak of forty three, dynamic compliance is three hundred twenty divided by seventeen, about nineteen.

[37-004 · Which normal range?]
The sources disagree on normal static compliance. RCP 203 Lesson 3a gives forty to eighty for intubated adults, and says below twenty five to thirty means more work of breathing and difficulty weaning. RCP 105 gives sixty to one hundred for adults, about eighty. The RCP 202 burn deck gives fifty to one hundred for lung compliance and seventy to one hundred for static, and Hess gives fifty to one hundred as acceptable on the ventilator. For a ventilated adult, use the RCP 203 range, forty to eighty. No source gives a normal range for dynamic compliance.

[37-005 · Practice: calculate compliance]
Pause and calculate. A ventilated adult has a tidal volume of five hundred, a peak pressure of thirty five, a plateau of twenty five, and a PEEP of five. What are the static and dynamic compliance, and is the static value normal?
[6-second practice pause]

[37-006 · Answer: 25 static, about 17 dynamic]
Static: twenty five minus five is twenty, and five hundred divided by twenty is twenty five milliliters per centimeter of water. Dynamic: thirty five minus five is thirty, and five hundred divided by thirty is about sixteen point seven. The static value is well below the RCP 203 range of forty to eighty, at the level where work of breathing rises and weaning becomes difficult.

[37-007 · Minute versus alveolar ventilation]
Minute ventilation is tidal volume times rate, the total gas moved per minute; Hess's example is five hundred milliliters times twelve, or six liters per minute. Alveolar ventilation is rate times tidal volume minus dead space: the fresh gas that reaches the alveoli each minute. Unlike minute ventilation, it accounts for dead space. Assume about one milliliter of dead space per pound of predicted body weight, or about thirty percent. Hess splits a five hundred milliliter breath into three hundred fifty alveolar and one hundred fifty dead space, a normal dead space to tidal volume ratio of about point three. The deck's example: a one hundred twenty five pound patient, rate fifteen, tidal volume four hundred. Alveolar ventilation is fifteen times two hundred seventy five, or four thousand one hundred twenty five milliliters per minute, while minute ventilation is six thousand.

[37-008 · Why alveolar ventilation matters]
Why it matters: PaCO2 varies inversely with alveolar ventilation. A low tidal volume raises the dead space fraction. With one hundred fifty milliliters of dead space, a five hundred milliliter breath is thirty percent dead space, but a three hundred milliliter breath is fifty percent, so alveolar ventilation falls. On the ventilator, raising the rate raises minute ventilation, which raises alveolar ventilation, and the PaCO2 falls.

[37-009 · Relative humidity]
Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapor per volume, in milligrams per liter. Relative humidity is the content divided by the capacity at that temperature, times one hundred percent. At thirty seven degrees, fully saturated gas holds forty four milligrams per liter, and fifty percent relative humidity means half the maximum vapor pressure. Ventilated patients need humidity because an endotracheal tube bypasses the upper airway, and medical gases are anhydrous. Dry gas thickens secretions and causes mucous obstruction. A quick check: condensate near the patient's airway means the gas is fully saturated, at one hundred percent; with none visible, the relative humidity could be anywhere from zero to ninety nine percent.

[37-010 · Humidity targets, and rain-out]
The deck's targets: a heated humidifier as close to one hundred percent relative humidity at body temperature as possible, with the probe near the wye and a target of thirty seven degrees. An HME gives seventy to ninety percent at thirty to thirty one degrees. Hess's guideline is thirty three to forty four milligrams per liter at the wye. Rain-out is condensation as heated, humidified gas cools in the tubing: gas at fifty degrees holds more than eighty milligrams per liter, but at thirty five only forty. The deck calls condensation the most common threat to circuit patency. Manage it with a heated-wire circuit, an in-line water trap, or temporarily an HME. Treat condensate as contaminated: drain it with gloves, away from the patient, discard it as hazardous liquid waste, and never let it reach the airway.

[37-011 · Cuff pressures]
Keep endotracheal and tracheostomy cuff pressure at twenty to thirty centimeters of water; most hospitals, and the NBRC hospital, use twenty five as the high limit. Too high, above twenty five to thirty, obstructs tracheal mucosal blood flow, causing ischemia, ulceration and necrosis. Too low, below twenty, allows leakage-type aspiration, which may lead to ventilator-associated pneumonia. Measure and adjust at least once per shift, and more often after tube or cuff changes or a leak, using a calibrated manometer, a three-way stopcock and a ten or twenty milliliter syringe, or a bulb device. Attaching a manometer loses volume, so always adjust, never just measure. Readjust after settings change the peak pressure, and document.

[37-012 · Cuff traps]
Now the traps. If the cuff needs more than thirty to seal, the tube may be too small; evaluate it and recommend an exchange. Pressures that rise progressively over time suggest tracheal dilation, tracheomalacia, or a cuff or pilot balloon malfunction; consider a subglottic suction tube. The minimal leak technique is no longer recommended, and chapter nine's prevention strategies for ventilator-associated pneumonia say to avoid it. Device trap: an endotracheal tube is twenty to thirty, and a standard cuffed trach has the same general target, but an LMA is inflated to sixty or less, and a King LT is about sixty. Units trap: twenty five to thirty five millimeters of mercury is the tracheal capillary perfusion pressure, not the cuff target. Use twenty to thirty centimeters of water.

[37-013 · Practice: a cuff that won't seal]
Pause and think. To stop a leak, a patient's endotracheal tube cuff needs thirty eight centimeters of water. What is the target range, what does this suggest, and what do you recommend?
[6-second practice pause]

[37-014 · Answer: the tube may be too small]
The target is twenty to thirty centimeters of water, with twenty five as the usual high limit. Needing more than thirty to seal suggests the tube may be too small, so evaluate it and recommend a tube exchange. Leaving the cuff at thirty eight risks tracheal ischemia, ulceration and necrosis.

[37-015 · The equation of motion]
The pressure needed to deliver a breath must overcome two things: airway resistance, and the stiffness of the lungs and chest wall. The deck says you do not need to memorize the equation, just understand what it means. Hess writes ventilator pressure as elastance times volume plus resistance times flow, where elastance is the inverse of compliance. Elastance times volume is the elastic load; resistance times flow is the resistive load. Volume control fixes flow and volume, so pressure varies; pressure control fixes pressure, so flow and volume vary. Asthma raises the resistance term, and ARDS raises the volume over compliance term; both raise the peak pressure, for different reasons. Hess's example: three hundred twenty over twenty three, plus a PEEP of twenty six, gives a plateau of about forty, and adding about three for resistance gives a peak of forty three.

[37-016 · Computing the I:E ratio]
In assist control, the I to E ratio can be controlled and altered. Precise control is possible only in pure control mode with no patient triggering, but adjustments can still be made when some breaths are patient triggered. Anything that changes the inspiratory or expiratory time changes the ratio. Inspiratory time equals volume in liters divided by flow in liters per second, and is usually set by the clinician. Expiratory time is simply the time left in each breath cycle. The percent inspiratory time, or duty cycle, is inspiratory time divided by total cycle time. One to four is twenty percent; one to three is twenty five; one to two is thirty three; one to one is fifty; and two to one is sixty seven.

[37-017 · Setting the flow for an ordered I:E]
The deck's worked example: an apneic patient on volume control assist control at a rate of fifteen, with a tidal volume of six hundred. The physician orders an I to E of one to four. What flow do you set? Step one, the minute volume: fifteen times six hundred is nine thousand milliliters, or nine liters per minute. Step two, convert the ratio: one divided by one plus four is twenty percent. Step three, the needed flow is the minute volume divided by the percent inspiratory time: nine divided by point two is forty five liters per minute. Use the decimal, or the answer comes out a hundred times wrong. And this works only when you control flow in volume control assist control; in pressure control or PRVC, you set the inspiratory time directly.

[37-018 · Lowering the ratio]
More often, you will need to decrease the I to E ratio. This comes up in severe expiratory airflow obstruction, where patients spontaneously show long expiratory times and low ratios, such as one to four or one to five. If the ventilator does not provide similar ratios, air trapping or auto-PEEP will occur. If auto-PEEP is present and the ratio is high, one to one or one to two, the first step is to allow enough time for complete exhalation: lower the ratio toward one to four or one to five by lengthening expiratory time, shortening inspiratory time, or both. Lowering the rate lengthens expiratory time. The deck's pair: a rate of twenty gives a three second cycle and one to two; a rate of twelve gives a five second cycle and one to four. The memory line: shorter to get the volume in, longer to get it out; the reverse is inverse ratio ventilation.

[37-019 · Lung protection, permissive hypercapnia, ATC]
The same deck lists the lung-protective menu: volume control with a low tidal volume of four to six milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight; volume control with a plateau below thirty; pressure control with a peak below thirty; dual modes such as PRVC; APRV; and HFOV. These may be paired with permissive hypercapnia: accepting a higher PaCO2 in exchange for the lower lung-injury risk of smaller volumes or pressures. Accept hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis until the pH falls below seven point two five to seven point three zero; then recommend a higher rate or sodium bicarbonate. The pH is the stop sign, not the PaCO2. The deck also defines automatic tube compensation, which adjusts positive pressure in a closed loop to offset the resistance of the artificial airway during spontaneous breaths. To use it, program the patient's airway type and size.

[37-020 · ARDSNet: who qualifies]
The chapter fourteen deck calls the N H L B I ARDS Network protocol, or ARDSNet, the main disease-specific, evidence-based protocol the NBRC expects you to know. Built on several randomized trials, it has improved survival in ventilated ARDS patients. Consider it for an acute onset of respiratory distress not caused by heart failure, with three findings: a P to F ratio below three hundred; bilateral diffuse infiltrates on the chest film, consistent with pulmonary edema; and no clinical evidence of left atrial hypertension or left ventricular failure. The deck's P to F scale: above three hundred is normal, two hundred to three hundred mild, one hundred to two hundred moderate, and below one hundred severe.

[37-021 · ARDSNet: the starting settings]
Calculate predicted body weight. Any mode will do, but aim for an I to E of one to one or less. Start the tidal volume at eight milliliters per kilogram, and reduce it by one milliliter per kilogram at intervals of two hours or less, until it reaches six. Start the rate near the baseline, generally twelve to twenty, and no more than thirty five; adjust the volume and rate to reach the pH and plateau goals. The oxygenation goal is a PaO2 of fifty five to eighty torr, or a saturation of eighty eight to ninety five percent. Set PEEP at least five to eight, then step through the FiO2 and PEEP table. Keep the plateau at thirty or less, and measure it every four hours and after each PEEP or volume change.

[37-022 · ARDSNet: plateau rules]
The plateau rules adjust the tidal volume. Plateau above thirty: lower the tidal volume in one milliliter per kilogram steps, to a minimum of four. Plateau below twenty five with a tidal volume below six: raise it by one milliliter per kilogram until the plateau is above twenty five or the volume reaches six. Plateau below thirty but with breath stacking or dyssynchrony: you may raise it in one milliliter per kilogram steps to seven or eight, as long as the plateau stays below thirty.

[37-023 · ARDSNet: pH rules]
The pH rules adjust the rate first. The goal is seven point three zero to seven point four five; above seven point four five, which is rare, lower the rate. Between seven point one five and seven point three zero, raise the rate until the pH is above seven point three zero or the PaCO2 is below twenty five, to a maximum of thirty five. Below seven point one five, raise the rate to thirty five. If it is already thirty five and the pH is still below seven point one five, raise the tidal volume in one milliliter per kilogram steps until the pH is above seven point one five, even if the plateau target is exceeded, and consider sodium bicarbonate.

[37-024 · Practice: ARDSNet and a low pH]
Last one. An ARDS patient on the ARDSNet protocol has a tidal volume of six milliliters per kilogram, a rate of twenty four, and a pH of seven point two two. What do you change first?
[6-second practice pause]

[37-025 · Answer: raise the rate]
A pH of seven point two two is between seven point one five and seven point three zero, so raise the rate, not the tidal volume, until the pH is above seven point three zero or the PaCO2 is below twenty five, to a maximum of thirty five. The tidal volume goes up only for a pH below seven point one five, once the rate is already thirty five.

[37-026 · Recap]
Here is part four in brief. Static compliance is tidal volume over plateau minus PEEP, normally forty to eighty in a ventilated adult; dynamic uses the peak and is always lower. Alveolar ventilation subtracts dead space, and the PaCO2 falls as it rises. Aim for about one hundred percent humidity at thirty seven degrees, and treat condensate as contaminated. Keep cuffs at twenty to thirty. To hit an ordered I to E, divide the minute volume by the percent inspiratory time, and lower the ratio when there is air trapping. And for ARDSNet: six milliliters per kilogram, a plateau of thirty or less, a pH of seven point three zero to seven point four five, and raise the rate first.
