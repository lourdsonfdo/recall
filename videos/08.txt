RCP 202 — Chapter 08: Pulmonary Rehabilitation

[08-001 · Pulmonary rehab: what the RT owns]
Welcome to section eight, pulmonary rehabilitation. Rehab does not significantly improve the PFTs; it changes what the patient can do. The RT's three tasks on the exam are: keep exercise safe by holding oxygenation above a specific threshold, teach breathing retraining including diaphragmatic and pursed-lip, and know the difference between a shallow chest pattern, true hyperventilation, and true hypoventilation. We will finish with the exercise prescription itself.

[08-002 · Adequate arterial oxygenation during exercise]
Hess chapter twenty-four says all exercise training should be performed under conditions of adequate arterial oxygenation, defined as PaO2 greater than fifty-five millimeters of mercury and SpO2 greater than eighty-eight percent. Numbers to memorize: eighty-eight and fifty-five. Hess writes the limit with a greater-than sign; the RCP 203 deck accepts exactly eighty-eight and fifty-five as adequate. For this rehab exam, use Hess's strict greater-than limits. If the patient has low resting oxygenation or desaturates significantly with exertion, give supplemental oxygen to maintain saturation; severe exercise hypoxemia may need an oxygen reservoir. If oxygenation still cannot be maintained, reduce intensity or stop exercise until it returns to adequate.

[08-003 · Titrating O2 for exertional desaturation]
The RCP 203 deck gives the step-by-step titration. First caution: avoid testing a patient with resting SpO2 under eighty-five percent on room air. During the test, if SpO2 stays at or above eighty-eight percent, the patient does not need additional oxygen and the test ends. If SpO2 drops by two percent or more, or falls below eighty-eight percent, increase oxygen by one liter per minute until SpO2 is back at or above eighty-eight. The same pair also qualifies COPD patients for long-term home oxygen, when SaO2 is at or below eighty-eight percent, or PaO2 at or below fifty-five torr, on room air.

[08-004 · Quick check: exercise desaturation]
During an oxygen titration walk, your COPD patient's SpO2 falls from ninety three to eighty six percent. The patient's resting SpO2 on room air was ninety one. Should the test continue, and what do you adjust? Pause and answer.
[6-second practice pause]

[08-005 · Answer: exercise desaturation]
Testing was appropriate, because the resting SpO2 of ninety one is above the eighty five percent caution. During exercise the SpO2 fell below eighty eight and dropped by more than two percent, so the patient needs oxygen. So the test does not end here; you continue and titrate. Increase oxygen by one liter per minute and repeat until SpO2 is at least eighty eight. Hess adds that if adequate oxygenation still cannot be maintained, reduce the exercise intensity or stop until oxygenation recovers.

[08-006 · Diaphragmatic breathing: belly breathing]
Diaphragmatic breathing, or belly breathing, trains the patient to let the diaphragm do the work and rely less on the accessory muscles. The diaphragm contracts and moves downward, lungs expand more fully, and the abdomen pushes outward on inhalation and recoils on exhalation. The breaths are deeper and reach the lower lung, where gas exchange is better. It improves the efficiency of ventilation by increasing tidal volume and decreasing respiratory rate. It engages the parasympathetic nervous system, lowering heart rate and blood pressure. To teach it, place the patient in semi-Fowler's, forearms relaxed, knees bent, and put your hand on the upper abdomen below the xiphoid. The patient inhales slowly through the nose and pushes out against your hand, while you apply progressive resistance to end inspiration.

[08-007 · Pursed-lip breathing: fight dynamic collapse]
Pursed-lip breathing is especially useful in COPD and emphysema. The patient inhales slowly through the nose for about two seconds, then exhales gently through lips held as if blowing out a candle for about four seconds, so exhalation is roughly twice inhalation. The gentle back-pressure splints small airways open, helping reduce dynamic airway collapse and air trapping, can reduce dyspnea, lets more CO2 leave, slows the respiratory rate, and gives the patient a sense of control. A deck conflict to know: the RCP 203 chapter ten deck says expiration should be at least two to three times inspiration, while the rehab deck uses about two seconds in and four seconds out. For this exam, follow the rehab deck.

[08-008 · Diaphragmatic vs thoracic breathing]
The deck's contrast to belly breathing is thoracic, or chest, breathing. In chest breathing the intercostals do most of the work, air enters the upper lung, the chest and shoulders rise, and the belly stays relatively still. It is shallow, less efficient, sympathetic, and linked with stress. Belly breathing is deep, efficient, parasympathetic, and engages the diaphragm. Do not confuse the deck's thoracic breathing with Hess's thoracic expansion exercises, deep breaths limited to three or four at a time to avoid fatigue and hyperventilation, used in the active cycle of breathing to mobilize secretions. Those are therapeutic deep breaths, not shallow chest breathing.

[08-009 · Related patterns: lateral costal and accessory]
Two cousins to know. Lateral costal breathing is an inspiratory exercise like diaphragmatic breathing, but you cup both hands over the lower rib edges and the patient breathes outward against your hands around the waist. It is a good alternative to the diaphragmatic method, especially after abdominal surgery. Accessory-muscle breathing uses the scalene, sternocleidomastoid, pectoralis major, trapezius, and external intercostals; they play a minor role in quiet breathing and become visible during exercise or distress. Prominent accessory use implies increased work of breathing or diaphragm weakness. After spinal cord injury, loss of intercostals and accessory muscles leaves isolated diaphragmatic breathing: rapid, shallow, with the chest wall moving inward during inspiration.

[08-010 · Prompt: fast breathing but which?]
Pause and think. A patient is breathing twenty-eight times a minute. Can you tell from the rate alone whether the patient is hyperventilating or hypoventilating? What single lab value defines the difference, and what are the two numeric cutoffs?
[6-second practice pause]

[08-011 · Answer: PaCO2 defines ventilation]
Rate alone cannot tell you. Hyperventilation and hypoventilation are defined by the PaCO2, not by how the breathing looks. PaCO2 varies inversely with alveolar ventilation. Normal PaCO2 is thirty-five to forty-five. Acute hyperventilation means PaCO2 below thirty-five with a pH above seven point four-five, acute respiratory alkalosis, most often caused by hypoxemia when PaO2 drops near sixty. Acute hypoventilation means PaCO2 above forty-five with pH below seven point three-five, acute respiratory acidosis, caused by narcotics, sedation, severe asthma or COPD exacerbations, airway obstruction, neuromuscular weakness, or fatigue. The trap: rapid, shallow breathing can still be hypoventilation.

[08-012 · The five side by side]
The study guide puts these five side by side. Diaphragmatic is the taught belly-breathing technique: deep, efficient, parasympathetic. Pursed-lip is the taught exhalation technique: back-pressure splints airways in COPD. Thoracic breathing is chest breathing, the deck's shallow sympathetic contrast. Hyperventilation and hypoventilation are not defined by how breathing looks; they are defined by the PaCO2. PaCO2 below thirty-five raises the pH, respiratory alkalosis; above forty-five lowers it, respiratory acidosis. Keep the trained techniques separate from the gas-exchange definitions. Diaphragmatic and pursed-lip are things we teach. Hyperventilation and hypoventilation are things we measure.

[08-013 · Exercise prescription: load, intensity, duration]
Pulmonary rehab does not significantly improve PFTs; the FEV1 may stay essentially the same, but the patient walks farther. Training blends stretching and flexibility, strengthening, and endurance. Start the initial load at an intensity the patient can tolerate without undue discomfort and build progressively. Hess adds the numbers. Push toward a training effect, as much as seventy to eighty percent of predicted maximal heart rate if possible. Minimum endurance exercise is no less than twenty minutes, three times per week. After a bicycle graded exercise test, initial load is fifty to eighty percent of maximum watts for the bicycle and thirty to forty percent for the arm ergometer. The six-minute walk is the method of choice to assess exercise capacity before and after rehab.

[08-014 · Modified Borg and when to advance]
The Modified Borg Scale rates perceived exertion or dyspnea from zero, nothing at all, to ten, maximal. A Borg effort between four and six means the prescription is adequate. Advance the load when the patient finishes the session with Borg under four, heart rate under eighty percent of predicted maximum, and acceptable oxygen saturation. Increase the load for three to five minutes, then return to the previous workload. Training benefits are specific to the muscles and tasks trained, so walking is emphasized.

[08-015 · Recap: safe exercise and smarter breathing]
Keep oxygenation above eighty-eight percent SpO2 and fifty-five millimeters PaO2 during exercise; add oxygen, reduce intensity, or stop if you cannot. Teach diaphragmatic breathing to engage the diaphragm and relax accessory muscles, and pursed-lip breathing about two seconds in and four seconds out to splint small airways open. Thoracic chest breathing is the shallow contrast pattern, and is not the same as thoracic expansion exercises. Hyperventilation and hypoventilation are defined by PaCO2, not by rate. Push toward seventy to eighty percent of predicted max heart rate if possible, at least twenty minutes three times a week, and use the Modified Borg to advance load.
