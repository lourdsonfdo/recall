RCP 202 — Chapter 11: Perioperative Management of Lung Transplants

[11-001 · Lung transplant: four diseases, two operations]
Welcome to section eleven, perioperative management of lung transplants. Most recipients fall into four disease groups: COPD or emphysema, cystic fibrosis and bronchiectasis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and pulmonary arterial hypertension. We will cover when to refer, the history dates Hess asks for, how lungs are allocated, who can and cannot be a candidate, how donors are evaluated, the single-lung and bilateral operations, and how to ventilate and monitor the new graft.

[11-002 · When to refer by disease]
Hess Table forty-six one sets the referral triggers. Interstitial lung disease: UIP or fibrosing NSIP on histology or imaging regardless of lung function, FVC under eighty percent predicted or DLCO under forty percent, dyspnea, or any supplemental oxygen need. Cystic fibrosis: FEV1 decline to thirty percent or rapid decline, PaCO2 above fifty or PaO2 below fifty-five, six-minute walk under four hundred meters, or life-threatening hemoptysis or pneumothoraces. COPD: progression despite maximal therapy, FEV1 under twenty-five percent, PaCO2 above fifty or PaO2 below sixty, BODE index five to six. Pulmonary vascular disease: NYHA class three or four, six-minute walk under three hundred fifty meters, cardiac index under two liters per minute per square meter. Hess's chapter text gives other COPD cutoffs, FEV1 under twenty percent and PaCO2 above forty-five; the guide says give the table's numbers.

[11-003 · Three dates: 1963, 1983, 1986]
Hess gives three dates that are easy to mix up. Nineteen sixty-three: Dr. James Hardy performed the first lung transplant ever. The patient lived only eighteen days, but his oxygenation improved and the anastomosis was confirmed successful. Nineteen eighty-three: the Toronto Lung Transplant Group performed the first successful lung transplant, in a fifty-eight-year-old man with pulmonary fibrosis, who survived seven years on cyclosporine and azathioprine. Nineteen eighty-six: the first successful double-lung transplant, for emphysema. Two traps. Hess never literally calls the nineteen eighty-three operation single-lung; it says first successful lung transplant. And a heart-lung transplant, first done by Bruce Reitz in nineteen eighty-one, is not a double-lung transplant. Average survival has risen from about four years to five to six years now.

[11-004 · Lung Allocation Score: what it does]
Before May two thousand five, listing time drove allocation, which favored slowly progressive diseases like COPD over rapidly declining ones like IPF. The Organ Procurement and Transplant Network introduced the LAS in May two thousand five to balance waitlist mortality against post-transplant survival. It assigns each patient a score from zero to one hundred, applies to recipients twelve and older, and draws on variables such as diagnosis, age, body mass index, NYHA class, forced vital capacity, oxygen need at rest, continuous mechanical ventilation, pulmonary artery pressure, and six-minute walk. Most data must be current within six months or get a zero value. A high score does not guarantee an organ: blood type, HLA profile, chest size, donor age, and distance also matter. After the LAS, COPD transplants fell nearly ten percent and IPF transplants rose by nearly the same amount.

[11-005 · LAS to Composite Allocation Score]
The revised deck adds current practice that is not in Hess. It states that on March ninth, two thousand twenty-three, the composite allocation score, CAS, replaced the LAS, as continuous distribution replaced the binary geographic zones once used to allocate organs. The CAS is a zero-to-one-hundred score that considers medical urgency, expected post-transplant survival, biological matching challenges, patient access, and organ placement efficiency, and it adds factors such as proximity to the donor hospital. The deck reports that transplant rates rose sixteen percent and waiting-list mortality fell twenty-nine percent after the change. The guide's rule: if a question says Lung Allocation Score, answer with LAS details, and treat the CAS as current-practice context from your instructor.

[11-006 · Candidacy and exclusion criteria]
The lung transplant window is the time between activation on the waiting list and the operation, ideally when disease progression is clear but rehabilitation is still possible. Work-up includes labs, imaging, heart catheterization, and esophageal studies. Table forty-six three excludes malignancy in the last two years, with high-recurrence cancers needing five years cancer-free; localized cancers with expected five-year survival above eighty percent are the exception, and the chapter text says two to three years. It also excludes untreatable advanced dysfunction of another major organ, uncorrectable bleeding, chronic infection with virulent or resistant organisms such as Burkholderia cenocepacia, active tuberculosis, weight under seventy or over one hundred thirty percent of ideal, active substance addiction, and repeated nonadherence. Unlike kidney transplant, lung transplant does not routinely require a close HLA match; preexisting donor-specific antibodies are what raise rejection risk. The revised deck stresses that some criteria are relative, and age alone is not an absolute contraindication.

[11-007 · Brain-death and DCDD donors]
Most lung transplants come from donors after brain death. Before testing, the donor must meet preconditions: core temperature at least thirty-six degrees Celsius, systolic blood pressure at least one hundred, eucapnia with PaCO2 thirty-five to forty-five, no hypoxia, and euvolemia. The clinical exam must then show no brain-originating motor response, absent corneal and oculovestibular reflexes, and no gag or cough with tracheal suctioning, followed by a positive apnea test. Donation after circulatory determination of death rose from one to four percent of U.S. lung transplants between twenty eleven and twenty sixteen. Declaring death requires at least two minutes of observation, and sixty to one hundred twenty minutes after withdrawal of support is the usual upper limit for organ recovery, though the revised slide says limits depend on donor physiology and center protocol.

[11-008 · Prompt: when is the apnea test positive?]
Pause and think. A potential donor meets every precondition for brain-death testing and has no brain-originating reflexes. During the apnea test, how long can the donor be off the ventilator, and which two PaCO2 findings, with no breathing effort, make the test positive?
[6-second practice pause]

[11-009 · Answer: PaCO2 > 60 or > 20 above baseline]
The donor is disconnected from the ventilator for up to eight minutes. The test is positive, consistent with brain death, when PaCO2 rises above sixty millimeters of mercury, or more than twenty above the baseline, with no breathing activity. It only counts once the preconditions are met: core temperature at least thirty-six degrees Celsius, systolic pressure at least one hundred, PaCO2 thirty-five to forty-five, no hypoxia, and euvolemia. So a baseline PaCO2 of forty that climbs to sixty-two with no breaths is positive on both counts: sixty-two is above sixty, and the rise of twenty-two is more than twenty.

[11-010 · Donor criteria, procurement, preservation]
Typical donor criteria: age under fifty-five, ABO compatibility, PaO2 above three hundred on FiO2 one point zero with PEEP five, and no significant chest trauma, aspiration, or prior cardiopulmonary surgery. Extended donors older than fifty-five or with more than twenty pack-years may be accepted. In donor management, brain death can cause central diabetes insipidus, which is often treated with desmopressin. Size is matched mainly by height, and oversized lungs may lower the risk of grade three primary graft dysfunction compared with undersized lungs. At procurement, the order is sternotomy, heparin, prostaglandin, aortic cross-clamp, then a chilled low-potassium dextran flush called Perfadex, which cools the tissue and clears blood from the pulmonary vascular bed. Lungs are stored at four to eight degrees Celsius. Hess says normothermic ex vivo perfusion can keep lungs at least twelve hours; the revised slide says duration varies by system.

[11-011 · The two operations, step by step]
Single-lung transplant uses a posterolateral or anterolateral thoracotomy. The contralateral lung receives single-lung ventilation while the pulmonary artery to the explanted lung is clamped to prevent shunt. Anastomoses run posterior to anterior: bronchus first, then pulmonary artery, then pulmonary veins to the left atrium. Air is purged and three chest tubes are placed. In single-lung transplant for COPD, the more compliant native lung may take more of the tidal volume and overdistend. Bilateral transplant often uses a clamshell incision, a transverse thoracosternotomy. Cystic fibrosis generally needs sequential bilateral transplant, pulmonary arterial hypertension generally requires bilateral, and bilateral is increasingly preferred for IPF. The native lung with the worst function on ventilation-perfusion scan comes out first while the other side is ventilated; ventilation then moves to the new allograft and the second lung is replaced.

[11-012 · CPB vs ECMO in transplant]
In two thousand fifteen, an estimated thirty to forty percent of lung transplants needed extracorporeal support. Cardiopulmonary bypass drains venous blood from the right atrium into a reservoir, pumps it through a heat exchanger and oxygenator, and returns it through an arterial cannula in the ascending aorta. Veno-venous ECMO takes blood from the vena cava or right atrium and returns it to the right atrium, giving respiratory support only. Veno-arterial ECMO returns blood from the right atrium to the arterial system, giving respiratory and circulatory support; the revised deck says it is often preferred with severe pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular dysfunction. Compared with CPB, ECMO had fewer transfusions, less PGD, fewer complications, and shorter intubation, with no difference in thirty-day or one-year survival. ECMO can also bridge a patient to transplant: the ECMO deck describes a cystic fibrosis patient awake, eating, and doing physical therapy, and Hess adds that awake, ambulatory ECMO improves outcomes.

[11-013 · Lung-protective ventilation after transplant]
Hess's key point: after transplant, lung-protective ventilation with minimal PEEP and FiO2 is the cornerstone. Hess lists tidal volume at or below six milliliters per kilogram, PEEP at a maximum of five, and FiO2 at or below zero point five. The revised deck corrects this: tidal volume about six milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight, PEEP starting around five and individualized because a fixed cap is too restrictive, FiO2 at or below zero point five as a goal when tolerated, using the lowest FiO2 that keeps oxygenation adequate, and plateau pressure at or below thirty. With severe primary graft dysfunction or pneumonia needing more oxygen, ECMO may be needed. For brain-dead potential donors, Hess also recommends lung-protective ventilation, with plateau pressure under thirty and diuretics to prevent interstitial fluid overload.

[11-014 · Prompt: grading early graft injury]
Pause and think. A new lung transplant recipient arrives in the ICU. Over the first seventy-two hours the team grades a specific form of early graft injury. Which syndrome is being graded, which ratio defines the grades, and what value of that ratio marks the worst grade?
[6-second practice pause]

[11-015 · Answer: PGD graded 0–3 by P/F]
The syndrome is primary graft dysfunction, a severe form of ischemia-reperfusion acute lung injury and a major cause of early morbidity and mortality after lung transplantation. It is graded zero to three by the ratio of PaO2 to FiO2 plus the presence of pulmonary edema infiltrates. Grade zero: ratio above three hundred, no infiltrates. Grade one: above three hundred with infiltrates. Grade two: two hundred to three hundred with infiltrates. Grade three, the worst: under two hundred with infiltrates. It is measured on arrival to the ICU and then daily for seventy-two hours. Grade three at seventy-two hours correlates with higher ninety-day mortality. Treatment is mainly supportive; an inhaled vasodilator is often given, and the revised deck adds ECMO in severe cases.

[11-016 · Postoperative chest tubes and prophylaxis]
Chest tubes: single-lung gets three; bilateral gets three on each side plus two mediastinal. Order of removal: mediastinal and anterior first, posterior once the patient is ambulatory, basilar after several days of output of one hundred to one hundred fifty milliliters or less. Hess says most patients have all tubes out within seven to ten days; the revised deck says timing depends on drainage, air leaks, and lung expansion, and the volume threshold is institution-dependent. Infection prophylaxis is wide: intraoperative cefepime and vancomycin, antifungals including inhaled amphotericin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole within one week for Pneumocystis, and ganciclovir followed by oral valganciclovir for cytomegalovirus. Hess Chapter nine adds that transbronchial biopsy is the gold standard for acute rejection.

[11-017 · Recap: candidate, score, operate, protect]
Four disease groups: COPD, cystic fibrosis, IPF, and pulmonary arterial hypertension. First successful lung transplant, nineteen eighty-three; first successful double-lung, nineteen eighty-six. Allocation used the LAS from May two thousand five; the deck says the composite allocation score replaced it in March two thousand twenty-three. Candidacy excludes recent malignancy, resistant infection or active tuberculosis, uncorrectable bleeding, weight extremes, and nonadherence. A brain-dead donor needs a positive apnea test, with PaCO2 above sixty or more than twenty above baseline. Single-lung order: bronchus, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins. Bilateral often uses a clamshell. After surgery, ventilate lung-protectively: tidal volume about six milliliters per kilogram of predicted body weight, individualized PEEP from about five, and FiO2 at or below zero point five as a goal. Primary graft dysfunction is graded zero to three by the P over F ratio plus infiltrates over seventy-two hours.
