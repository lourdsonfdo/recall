RCP 202 — Chapter 05: ECMO

[05-001 · What this chapter covers]
Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation drains blood from a central vein, pumps it through an artificial lung, and returns it to the patient. Where it returns decides what kind of support you get. This chapter walks through the access points and cannulation strategies, the inclusion criteria and the thresholds that make the team consider ECMO, how VV differs from VA, and the two knobs that run the circuit day to day: blood flow and sweep gas. We also map the places where the deck and Hess disagree on survival numbers so a trap question cannot catch you.

[05-002 · Access points and cannulation]
The deck shows three cannulation strategies. VV dual site: drain from a femoral vein, return to the right internal jugular into the right atrium, or to the contralateral femoral vein. VV single site: a dual lumen catheter in the right internal jugular that drains from both venae cavae and returns to the right atrium. VA in adults: drain from a femoral vein and return into the femoral artery, the most common arterial access. VA in neonates typically uses the carotid artery. The instructor's memory trick is simple: follow the return cannula. Returns to a vein is VV, which supports the lungs. Returns to an artery is VA, which supports heart and lungs.

[05-003 · Recirculation]
A common access related problem is recirculation. That is oxygenated blood going straight back through the circuit instead of to the patient. Causes include improper cannula position, hypovolemia, excessive flow, or decreased cardiac output. Signs are a drainage line that looks bright red, almost like the return line, and a rising continuous mixed venous oxygen saturation. It becomes clinically significant when SpO2 falls. Fixes are to decrease ECMO blood flow and confirm cannula position, often with imaging. Adult VV blood flow commonly runs two to six liters per minute, and achievable flow is limited by cannula size and location.

[05-004 · Inclusion criteria for VV ECMO]
Inclusion criteria means indication. The gate is a reversible process or a credible bridge to lung transplant. VV ECMO is considered for severe respiratory failure with adequate cardiac function. The deck's indication list is severe ARDS, which is the most common, status asthmaticus, pulmonary hemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, meconium aspiration, air leak syndromes, and bridge to transplant. The instructor adds pneumonia or severe PNA progressing to ARDS, and smoke inhalation. Hess omits those two from its recap but includes airway compression from mediastinal masses and congenital airway anomalies.

[05-005 · Thresholds to consider ECMO]
There is no single trigger. The deck says consider ECMO when predicted mortality is at or above fifty percent, and consider it more strongly as predicted mortality approaches eighty percent despite optimal conventional therapy. ELSO calls this fifty to eighty window arbitrary. Other triggers: oxygenation index twenty to forty in neonates and comparable values in adults; a Murray lung injury score above three; EOLIA criteria of PaO2 over FiO2 under eighty for six hours or under fifty for three hours; and refractory respiratory acidosis with pH under seven point two to seven point two five, especially with hemodynamic compromise. Think early, before injurious ventilation causes multiorgan failure.

[05-006 · Contraindications]
Absolute contraindications are rare: irreversible coagulopathy, inability to cannulate for anatomic reasons, and futility. Severe neurologic injury and terminal disease with short life expectancy are also contraindications. Current guidelines call mechanical ventilation at high settings, meaning FiO2 above zero point nine and plateau over thirty, for seven or more days a contraindication, although newer data suggest mortality does not climb much until ten to fourteen days. The RESP score, from the ELSO registry, predicts survival. Known mortality predictors are advancing age, multiorgan dysfunction, and prolonged pre-ECMO ventilation.

[05-007 · VV versus VA ECMO]
The entire structural difference is where the return cannula sits. VV returns to a vein and supports only respiratory failure; the patient's own heart still has to pump. VA returns to an artery, bypasses heart and lungs, and supports both cardiac and respiratory failure. VV is preferred when the heart works, because it is simpler and safer. VA is reserved as a salvage technique in adult respiratory failure because it brings coronary, brain, and upper body ischemia risks, more strokes, and more vascular complications. Reported historical survival on the deck: fifty to seventy percent for VV in severe respiratory failure, thirty to fifty percent for VA in severe cardiac failure.

[05-008 · VA complications]
VA carries meaningful limb and brain risk. Limb ischemia occurs in roughly seventeen percent of VA patients and five percent require amputation, which is why many teams place a distal perfusion catheter at insertion. Ischemic stroke is more common with VA and contributes to about nine percent of all deaths. VV has far less limb ischemia but can still throw clots: catheter associated DVT shows up on post decannulation ultrasound in as many as eighty five percent of patients. Bleeding is the single most common complication across the board, followed by neurologic injury, infection, and vascular injury or thrombosis.

[05-009 · The two knobs]
Day to day you turn two knobs. Blood flow governs oxygenation: the more cardiac output you route through the oxygenator, the more oxygenated blood returns. Increasing blood flow typically fixes hypoxemia. Sweep gas governs CO2 removal, because CO2 diffuses out passively down the gradient from blood into the sweep gas. Blood leaving the oxygenator is usually fully saturated even at sweep flows of one to two liters, so raising sweep flow does little for oxygen but clears CO2. Hess allows sweep flows from zero point one to ten liters. The slide's rule: oxygenation, think blood flow and FiO2; CO2, think sweep.

[05-010 · Resting ventilator settings on ECMO]
Once ECMO is doing the gas exchange, the ventilator is rested. The deck's resting pattern on slide twenty: tidal volume under six milliliters per kilogram, FiO2 under sixty percent, rate under ten, PIP under thirty five, mean airway pressure under twenty five, plateau under thirty, PEEP between ten and fifteen. Do not memorize this as a single recipe. The pattern is lung protective: low tidal volume, low driving pressure, modest FiO2, lower rate because sweep gas handles the CO2, and enough PEEP to maintain recruitment. Hess agrees: tidal volume at or below six per kilogram, driving pressure at or below fifteen, plateau at or below thirty, PEEP ten to fifteen.

[05-011 · Quick check: knob to turn]
A patient on VV ECMO has a stable SpO2 of ninety four percent, but the morning gas shows a PaCO2 of seventy and a pH of seven point two three. Which knob do you turn, and in which direction? Pause and answer before the next slide.
[6-second practice pause]

[05-012 · Answer: knob to turn]
Increase sweep gas flow. CO2 crosses the membrane by passive diffusion down a gradient, so raising sweep flow clears more CO2 and brings the PaCO2 and the pH back toward normal. Leave blood flow alone because oxygenation is already adequate. Remember the one line rule: oxygen is blood flow, CO2 is sweep.

[05-013 · Deck vs Hess outcome numbers]
Two slide numbers show up in trap questions. First, the deck says severe ARDS survival managed with ECMO is greater than fifty four percent. Hess clarifies that fifty four is the ICU survival of severe ARDS with conventional care, and the ECMO trial arms did a bit better at fifty six for CESAR and sixty for EOLIA. Cumulative survival for all respiratory ECMO patients is sixty one percent in both sources. Second, the deck says ECMO survival rose from fifty seven percent in nineteen ninety three to seventy two percent in two thousand seven; Hess says the same, but notes those numbers describe children without comorbidities.

[05-014 · Quick check: VV or VA?]
A thirty year old in refractory ARDS from pneumonia has a cardiac output of five point five liters per minute and a mean arterial pressure of seventy five on no vasopressors. The team is considering ECMO. Which configuration is preferred, and why?
[6-second practice pause]

[05-015 · Answer: VV or VA?]
Choose VV ECMO. The heart is working, so the patient only needs respiratory support, which VV provides. VV is simpler and safer, with less limb and stroke risk than VA. VA would be the choice only if cardiac failure or cardiogenic shock were also present. The slide says it plainly: VV ECMO equals respiratory failure plus adequate cardiac function.

[05-016 · Recap: ECMO]
To recap. ECMO drains venous blood, pumps it through an oxygenator, and returns it. VV returns to a vein for respiratory support only. VA returns to an artery and supports both heart and lungs. Follow the return cannula to tell them apart. Consider ECMO when predicted mortality sits in the fifty to eighty percent window, or when lung protective strategies, proning, and neuromuscular blockade fail. Two knobs: blood flow for oxygen, sweep for CO2. Rest the ventilator with low tidal volumes and plateau at or under thirty. Bleeding is the top complication, and timing early beats timing late.
