RCP 203 — Chapter 22: Respiratory Drugs, part 1: Airway Drugs

[22-001 · What this chapter covers]
Respiratory drugs are split into three parts, and this first part covers the airway drugs. For each drug the study guide wants its class, its site of action, and its hazards. We cover acetylcysteine, beclomethasone, albuterol and levalbuterol, and ipratropium. Then the difference between short-acting and long-acting beta agonists, the leukotriene inhibitors, and the immunoglobulins and monoclonal antibodies. We finish with why albuterol shows up in the treatment of high potassium.

[22-002 · Acetylcysteine]
Acetylcysteine, Mucomyst, is a mucolytic: it reduces the thickness of mucus. Its site of action is the mucus itself. Mucus contains disulfide bonds, and acetylcysteine breaks them. It comes as ten and twenty percent, and the twenty percent is often diluted with saline, essentially to ten percent. Its hazards are bronchospasm, especially in asthmatics, so give a bronchodilator first; nausea or vomiting; a rotten egg smell; and mechanical obstruction of the airway. Do not mix it with some antibiotics. Does it work? The deck says no data show it is effective for any lung disease, and it should not be used as a mucoactive drug, and Hess calls its benefit unproven. Its most important use today, which the deck flags as very testable, is as the antidote for acetaminophen overdose.

[22-003 · Beclomethasone]
Beclomethasone dipropionate, QVAR, is an inhaled corticosteroid. It is a controller, not a rescue drug, and not for acute severe asthma. It is used for maintenance control of chronic asthma, and for COPD with other agents. Hess notes it is a prodrug activated in the lung. Glucocorticoids are lipid soluble and act on receptors inside the cell, inducing anti-inflammatory proteins and inhibiting the enzyme phospholipase A2. Steroids also restore responsiveness to beta stimulation, by increasing the number and availability of beta receptors. The most common side effects are thrush and hoarseness, and less often cough and bronchoconstriction. Prevent them with the minimal dose, a spacer, and rinsing the mouth. Hess says inhaled steroids work once or twice a day, and Heuer lists QVAR at two puffs twice daily.

[22-004 · Albuterol and levalbuterol]
Albuterol and levalbuterol are short-acting beta two agonists, rescue drugs, or relievers, for acute reversible airflow obstruction. Binding the beta receptor raises cyclic AMP, and beta two stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. The deck's class slide gives an onset of fifteen minutes and a duration of four to six hours for both. Albuterol is a fifty fifty mix of two mirror-image forms. The R form causes bronchodilation, and the S form is much less potent and may add side effects. Its nebulizer dose is two point five milligrams in three milliliters of saline. Levalbuterol, Xopenex, is the pure R form, and Hess says it is likely twice as potent, allowing half the dose. The beta agonist side effects are tremor, a faster heart rate and palpitations, tolerance, a fall in PaO2, higher glucose, lower potassium and paradoxical bronchospasm. Levalbuterol causes less tremor and heart rate change, but Hess says its superiority is unproven.

[22-005 · Ipratropium]
Ipratropium bromide, Atrovent, is an anticholinergic bronchodilator, a muscarinic receptor antagonist. Parasympathetic nerves set the airway's resting tone, and anticholinergics block that tone. The M3 receptor on airway smooth muscle and glands causes bronchoconstriction, and Hess says ipratropium blocks muscarinic receptors without preferring a subtype. Because it is a quaternary relative of atropine, it does not cross membranes well, so it stays mostly in the lung. It is used for maintenance treatment of COPD, and sometimes in asthma, and with albuterol, as in Combivent and DuoNeb, the effect is synergistic. Its onset is several minutes, with a peak at one to two hours. The most common side effect is dry mouth, then cough and dilated pupils, so protect the eyes. The deck's doses are seventeen micrograms per puff, two puffs four times a day, or point five milligrams by nebulizer four times a day.

[22-006 · SABA versus LABA]
Short-acting beta agonists are rescue drugs for acute obstruction: albuterol and levalbuterol, with an onset of fifteen minutes and a duration of four to six hours. Long-acting beta agonists are controllers, for maintenance bronchodilation and nighttime symptoms: salmeterol, formoterol and arformoterol, and the ultra-long indacaterol, olodaterol and vilanterol, with an onset of twenty minutes and a duration of twelve to twenty four hours. Short-acting agents are water soluble and long-acting ones fat soluble. Hess gives albuterol as needed, every four to six hours, salmeterol twice a day, and the ultra-long agents once a day. The deck gives formoterol three minutes to effect, lasting up to twelve hours. And the rule: long-acting beta agonists are never used alone in asthma. They need an inhaled steroid controller, and they do not replace short-acting agents for rescue.

[22-007 · Practice: rescue or controller?]
Pause and sort these. During an acute asthma attack, which of these can be used for rescue: albuterol, salmeterol, beclomethasone or montelukast? And what must a long-acting beta agonist always be paired with in asthma?
[6-second practice pause]

[22-008 · Answer: only albuterol]
Only albuterol, a short-acting beta agonist, is a rescue drug. Salmeterol is a long-acting controller with a slower onset. Beclomethasone is an inhaled steroid, a controller, not for acute severe asthma. Montelukast is a prophylactic controller, not for acute attacks. And a long-acting beta agonist must always be paired with an inhaled steroid controller in asthma, never used alone.

[22-009 · Leukotriene inhibitors]
Leukotrienes cause bronchoconstriction, mucus production and airway inflammation, and antileukotrienes block them in two ways. Montelukast, Singulair, the most tested, blocks the receptor, binding the leukotriene receptor with high affinity, and its side effects are mostly mild. Zafirlukast, Accolate, is also a receptor antagonist. It causes headache and stomach upset, Hess says to take it on an empty stomach, because food lowers its absorption by about forty percent, and it interacts with warfarin and theophylline. Zileuton, Zyflo, blocks production instead, by inhibiting the five-lipoxygenase enzyme. Monitor liver function, and it also interacts with theophylline and warfarin. All three are prophylactic controllers, not for acute attacks. They protect against exercise, aspirin and allergen triggered asthma, are not ideal alone, and work in only about fifty to seventy percent of patients.

[22-010 · Immunoglobulins and biologics]
Immunoglobulins, or antibodies, are made by B lymphocytes, in five classes. IgE is the asthma antibody: it attaches to mast cells and triggers allergic reactions. IgA protects mucosal surfaces, IgG is the most common and fights infection, IgM is the first responder, and IgD is the fifth class. Monoclonal antibodies, the biologics, treat severe asthma by injection or infusion. They are add-ons, not for acute relief and not a replacement for inhaled steroids, and the most severe side effect is anaphylaxis. Omalizumab, Xolair, binds IgE and blocks it from attaching to mast cells, for allergic asthma. Benralizumab, mepolizumab and reslizumab block interleukin five, lowering eosinophils, for eosinophilic asthma. Palivizumab, Synagis, prevents RSV in at-risk infants. The decks call these antibodies both humanized IgE and IgG, and Hess calls omalizumab IgG, so answer IgG.

[22-011 · Palivizumab, and a gap]
Palivizumab works differently from the asthma biologics. It inhibits the F protein and prevents RSV from replicating. It is given to prevent RSV in infants with bronchopulmonary dysplasia, those born before thirty five weeks, and those with congenital heart disease. One gap remains: no source covers RSV immune globulin, and intravenous immune globulin appears only for neuromuscular disease, such as Guillain-Barré and myasthenia gravis.

[22-012 · Albuterol for high potassium]
Both low and high potassium are reversible causes of cardiac arrest, among the five Hs. About ninety eight percent of body potassium is inside cells, where the sodium potassium pump keeps it. Albuterol stimulates beta two receptors on muscle and liver cells, which raises cyclic AMP and speeds the pump, so potassium moves into the cells and the serum level falls. It works within thirty minutes and lasts two to four hours. The dose for hyperkalemia is ten to twenty milligrams nebulized, far above the bronchodilator dose, and it can lower serum potassium by about half to one milliequivalent per liter. It is used with calcium gluconate, insulin with dextrose, bicarbonate if acidotic, and definitive removal. The exam anchor: albuterol does not remove potassium, it hides it inside the cells, so it is a bridge, not a cure, and potassium can rebound.

[22-013 · Practice: albuterol and potassium]
One more. A patient with dangerous hyperkalemia gets nebulized albuterol, and the serum potassium falls. Has potassium been removed from the body? Why might it rise again, and roughly what dose was given?
[6-second practice pause]

[22-014 · Answer: hidden, not removed]
No. Albuterol moves potassium into the cells, so total body potassium is unchanged. The effect lasts two to four hours, and then potassium may leak back out, so it can rebound. That is why albuterol is a bridge, used with calcium gluconate, insulin and dextrose, and definitive removal by diuretics, dialysis or resins. The dose is ten to twenty milligrams nebulized, far above the bronchodilator dose.

[22-015 · Recap]
Here is part one in brief. Acetylcysteine breaks disulfide bonds but is not recommended for lung disease; its testable use is acetaminophen overdose, and give a bronchodilator first. Beclomethasone is an inhaled steroid controller; rinse to prevent thrush. Albuterol and levalbuterol are rescue drugs with a fifteen minute onset; ipratropium blocks muscarinic tone and causes dry mouth. Long-acting beta agonists are controllers, never alone in asthma. Antileukotrienes are controllers too, and the biologics are add-ons, with anaphylaxis the worst risk. And albuterol lowers potassium by moving it into cells, a bridge, not a cure.
