1The readiness screen: what must be true before any weaning parameter is measured

  • Weaning: gradually transfer breathing work back to the patient; improvement of the underlying disease is the best success predictor.
  • Screen: some reversal of the cause, spontaneous inspiratory effort, pH ≥ 7.25, and stable hemodynamics without ischemia or significant hypotension.
  • Oxygenation: P/F ≥ 150–200, PEEP ≤ 5–8 cm H₂O, FiO₂ ≤ 0.4–0.5 before formal assessment.
  • Assess together: clinical recovery, ventilatory pattern, oxygenation, and pulmonary reserve; an SBT predicts readiness better than any single parameter.
  • Definitions: success means no ventilatory support for at least 48 hours after extubation; failed SBT or reintubation within 48 hours means failure.
  • NIV: extubated patients still receiving NIV have weaning in progress; RR/RSBI are useful indicators, while VC/NIF mainly monitor neuromuscular patients.

2Sensorium (level of consciousness)

  • Sensorium: level of consciousness; oriented × 3 means knowing time, place, and person. Sedatives, neurologic injury, hypoxemia, or hypercapnia can impair it.
  • GCS: 3–15 total; use mild 13–15, moderate 9–12, severe ≤ 8. GCS ≤ 8 prompts airway-protection assessment/intubation.
  • Weaning: poor cooperation can falsely lower effort-dependent VC/MIP; somnolence, agitation, anxiety, or other mental-status changes warrant stopping the SBT.
  • Recovery: after blockade, check head lift for 5 seconds, tongue protrusion for 5 seconds, and hand grip.
  • Extubation: adequate airway protection matters; following commands is desirable but not essential when protection is otherwise adequate. Pair daily SATs with SBTs.
Watch outRCP 105 slide text gives mild 13–14 and intubation < 8; use the notes/RCP 203 bands 13–15 and ≤ 8.
Watch outNot in your sources: a numeric GCS/LOC cutoff for extubation; ≤ 8 is an intubation/airway-protection trigger.

3CXR (chest radiograph)

  • CXR: evaluates lungs, pleural space, catheters, and radiopaque lines/tubes; look for disease or mechanical causes of reduced compliance.
  • Sudden problems: tension pneumothorax, pleural effusion, or main-stem ETT migration; gradual problems include pneumonia, ARDS, and atelectasis.
  • Findings: pneumonia can show consolidation and air bronchograms; VAP has new/progressive infiltrates. Initial inhalation-injury films may be normal.
  • ETT: exam answer is tip about 4–6 cm above the carina, usually T2–T4; verify position with CXR.
Watch outHess Ch 17 gives adult ETT tip 3 to 7 cm above the carina with neutral neck; use deck/Hess Ch 7 4–6 cm.
Watch outNot in your sources: a CXR finding or cutoff required for weaning/extubation readiness.
SourceRCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S20 · RCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S29 · RCP 103 Infectious Pulmonary Disease, S27 · RCP 105 Procedures Related to MV, S44 · RCP 202 Lssn 2 S28 · RCP 203 Lssn 6a S34 · Hess Ch 7 · Hess Ch 17 · Hess Ch 44

4CBC (Hb/Hct, WBC)

  • Normals: Hb 12–16 g/dL; Hct 40–50%; RBC 4–6 million/mm³; WBC 5,000–10,000 /mm³.
  • Anemia: lowers oxygen-carrying capacity despite apparently normal oxygenation; inadequate oxygen delivery contributes to respiratory-muscle weakness and weaning failure.
  • Infection: bacterial pneumonia raises WBC; viral pneumonia lowers it. VAP may cause leukocytosis or leukopenia with fever, purulence, and new infiltrates.
  • Ventilated patients: report and correct Hb < 10 g/100 mL; this is an oxygenation-management threshold. Fever/infection raise O₂ consumption and CO₂ production.
Watch outNot in your sources: Hb, Hct, or WBC thresholds stated as weaning/extubation criteria.
SourceRCP 103 Clinical Data, S49, 50 · RCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S9, 30 · RCP 105 Management of MV, S14 · RCP 103 Infectious Pulmonary Disease, S27 · RCP 105 Procedures Related to MV, S44 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 23

5MIP / NIF (maximum inspiratory pressure / negative inspiratory force)

  • MIP/NIF: effort-dependent inspiratory-muscle strength; more negative means stronger. Exam readiness: −20 to −30 cm H₂O in 20 sec or more negative.
  • Measurement: inspire against an occluded negative-pressure manometer; maximal effort may require 20 to 30 sec off support.
  • Hess method: ETT/trach aneroid manometer with one-way valve, occluded for 20 to 25 s; its figure measures at FRC.
  • Normal: weaning deck −80 to −100; high resistance or low compliance can make even −20 cm H₂O inadequate.
  • Neuromuscular disease: weaker than −30 signals impending ventilatory failure; diaphragm is last paralyzed and first to recover after blockade.
Watch outUse exam range −20 to −30 cm H₂O; Chang notes say at least −30, while RCP 203/Hess use −20.
Watch outNormal differs: Neuro slide 43 −60 to −100; RCP 203 > −60 and Burn < −60 both describe at least 60 cm H₂O of force.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S15, 16 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S19 · RCP 202 Lssn 2 S37 · RCP 103 Neuro-Respiratory Disorders, S21, 43 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 42 · Hess Ch 39

6VC (vital capacity)

  • VC: maximum exhalation after maximum inspiration; VC = IRV + VT + ERV, reflecting lung volume and respiratory-muscle reserve.
  • Exam values: readiness > 10 mL/kg; normal ~50 mL/kg using IBW. Example: 3.1 L + 0.5 L + 1.2 L = 4.8 L.
  • Procedure: after full support, breathe spontaneously for 3 min; suction if needed, disconnect, attach Wright spirometer, inhale deeply, exhale fully, reconnect promptly.
  • Effort: requires alert cooperation; deep sedation/paralysis prevents reliable testing. Slow VC is repeated at least three times; record the best.
  • Neuromuscular warning: FVC < 20 mL/kg signals impending failure; adequate postextubation VC must support breathing and an effective cough.
Watch outExam threshold is > 10 mL/kg; Hess/RCP 203 also list 15 mL/kg. Neuro normal FVC is 60–70 or 65–75 mL/kg.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S11, 15, 16, 50 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S18 · RCP 203 Lssn 1 S40 · RCP 103 Neuro-Respiratory Disorders, S21, 43 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 44

7RSBI (rapid shallow breathing index): formula, cutoff, worked example

  • Formula: RSBI = f ÷ VT in liters, expressed as breaths/min/L. Low means slow/deep breathing; high means rapid/shallow breathing.
  • Exam cutoff: < 100 breaths/min/L predicts readiness; rapid shallow breaths increase dead-space ventilation as VT falls.
  • Measure: off the ventilator, breathe spontaneously for 3 min or until stable; measure V̇E and f, then VT = V̇E ÷ f.
  • Example: V̇E 10 L/min and f 25/min → VT = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 L; RSBI = 25 ÷ 0.4 = 62.5.
  • Interpretation: 62.5 is < 100, supporting weaning; use liters and a stable pattern with minimum PS.
Watch outChang uses < 100, rounded from 105; Hess and many/NBRC protocols use < 105. Know both.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S11, 12 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S15, 16 · RCP 203 Lssn 1 S42 · Hess Ch 20

8V̇E (minute ventilation), spontaneous VT and RR

  • Normals: V̇E 6–10 L/min; spontaneous VT 5–10 mL/kg; spontaneous f 12–20; PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg.
  • Exam thresholds: spontaneous VT at least 5 mL/kg; V̇E > 10 L/min often predicts poor weaning outcomes from high ventilatory demand.
  • Measure: respirometer with one-way valve over 1 minute; VT = V̇E ÷ f. Higher VD/VT raises required minute ventilation.
  • Special contexts: NHLBI ARDS SBT accepts VT ≥ 4 mL/kg PBW; burn-patient unassisted VT is 6 to 10 mL/kg.
Watch outHess lists minute ventilation 12 L/min as a predictor without a direction sign; use deck 10 L/min ceiling for this exam.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S11, 16, 17 · RCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S30 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S15 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S94 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 42

9ABG and oxygenation criteria (PaO₂/FiO₂, PEEP, FiO₂, pH, shunt)

  • Weaning oxygenation: PaO₂ > 60 mm Hg or SaO₂ > 90% on FiO₂ ≤ 0.40; P/F ≥ 150 mm Hg.
  • Additional limits: shunt QS/QT < 20%; P(A−a)O₂ < 350 mm Hg on FiO₂ 100%. Correct shunt > 20% before attempting weaning.
  • RCP 203 screen: P/F ≥ 150–200, PEEP ≤ 5–8 cm H₂O, FiO₂ ≤ 0.4–0.5, pH ≥ 7.25.
  • SBT tolerance: SpO₂ ≥ 85–90% or PaO₂ ≥ 50–60 torr; pH ≥ 7.30; PaCO₂ rise ≤ 10 torr.
  • After blockade: before extubation, PaO₂ > 80 mm Hg and PaCO₂ < 45 mm Hg; compare COPD patients with baseline.
  • Low PaCO₂: investigate hypoxia, pain, anxiety, or settings; reducing support during hyperventilation can further raise breathing work.
Watch outGeneral exam P/F is ≥ 150; burn-specific Hess criterion is > 200 mm Hg. Hess prints PEEP = 8 rather than deck ≤ 5–8.

10Hemodynamic stability

  • Readiness: correct cardiac/circulatory problems; no myocardial ischemia or significant hypotension before weaning.
  • SBT limits: HR < 120–140/min, change < 20%; SBP < 180–200 and > 90 mm Hg, change < 20%; no vasopressors required.
  • Failure: tachycardia, hypertension, hypotension, or arrhythmias suggest intolerance; manage cardiac dysfunction before liberation.
  • CPAP trap: positive pressure can support marginal left-ventricular function; a tolerated CPAP trial may be followed by heart failure after extubation.
Watch outHess allows no or low-dose vasopressors; stop criteria include HR > 140/min or sustained change > 20%, SBP > 180 or < 90 mm Hg.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S6, 9, 14 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S45 · RCP 203 Lssn 3a S59 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S90, 92 · Hess Ch 20

11Pulmonary mechanics: static compliance, VD/VT, airway resistance

  • Static compliance: CST = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP); exam readiness > 30 mL/cm H₂O. Crs < 25–30 predicts difficulty.
  • Dead space: VD/VT = (PaCO₂ − PĒCO₂) ÷ PaCO₂; readiness < 60% while intubated; normal approximately 0.3.
  • Example: PaCO₂ 40 mm Hg, PĒCO₂ 27 mm Hg → (40 − 27) ÷ 40 = 13 ÷ 40 = 0.325 (32.5%).
  • Resistance: Raw = (PIP − Pplat) ÷ flow (L/sec); normal 0.6–2.4 cm H₂O/L/sec, higher through ETT/circuit.
  • Interpretation: low compliance, high resistance, and high VD/VT raise breathing work; these measurements do not require cooperation.
Watch outUse general weaning compliance > 30 mL/cm H₂O; Hess burn extubation requires at least 50 mL/cm H₂O.
Watch outNot in your sources: an established critical airway-resistance value for weaning.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S17 · RCP 203 Lssn 3a S52 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 42 · Hess Ch 50

12Spontaneous breathing trial (SBT)

  • SBT: best bedside test of sustainable breathing; exam duration up to 30 min, with failures usually within 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Methods: T-piece, PS 5–8 cm H₂O, CPAP ≈ 5 cm H₂O, or ATC have similar success; avoid stand-alone SIMV weaning.
  • Pass: stable breathing pattern, gas exchange, and hemodynamics; RCP 203 RR ≤ 30–35/min with change < 50%.
  • Fail: distress, rapid shallow breaths, paradox, accessory-muscle use, cyanosis, diaphoresis, anxiety, or reduced mental status → stop and restore full support.
  • Retry: rest, identify/correct the cause, and repeat every 24 hours. Passing an SBT still requires separate airway-protection/patency assessment.
  • Daily approach: pair SAT/SBT when appropriate; avoid SAT with active seizures, alcohol withdrawal, escalating sedative doses, neuromuscular blockade, myocardial ischemia in the previous 24 h, or increased ICP.
Watch outExam answer: up to 30 min; RCP 203/Hess use 30–120 min. RCP 203 specifies an initial 2–5 min screening phase.
Watch outDeck methods have similar success; Hess CPG favors initial PS 5–8 cm H₂O in patients ventilated more than 24 hours.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S19, 20, 21, 26 · RCP 203 Lssn 2b S44 · RCP 203 Lssn 3a S59, 60 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S90, 91, 92, 93, 94 · Hess Ch 20

13Secretions, cough and airway protection

  • Extubation: passing an SBT establishes breathing ability; also require airway protection, minimal aspiration/obstruction risk, and independent secretion clearance.
  • Cough: MEP normal 60–100 cm H₂O; < 40 cm H₂O indicates ineffective cough and poor clearance.
  • Failure risk: cough peak flow ≤ 60 L/min raises risk nearly fivefold; secretions > 2.5 mL/h raise risk threefold.
  • Secretion burden: suctioning more often than every 2 hours should preclude extubation; assess secretion quantity and character.
  • Neuromuscular patients: bulbar weakness and inability to handle upper-airway secretions escape standard weaning parameters; good ABGs do not establish airway protection.
SourceRCP 105 Airway Management, S64 · RCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S7 · RCP 203 Lssn 6a S62 · RCP 103 Neuro-Respiratory Disorders, S43 · Hess Ch 17 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 44

14Cuff-leak test and post-extubation stridor

  • Cuff leak: deflate the ETT cuff during positive-pressure ventilation; listen for airflow or measure leaked volume/percentage of VT.
  • High-risk patients: traumatic intubation, intubation > 6 days, large ETT, female sex, or reintubation after unplanned extubation.
  • Limitations: false positives/negatives are common; cuff deflation can let pooled secretions enter the lower airway.
  • Burns: edema should resolve enough for audible leak at moderate inflating pressure 20 to 30 cm H₂O.
  • Failed test: give systemic steroids at least 4 hours before extubation; repeat testing is unnecessary per the cited guideline.
  • Stridor: assess breathing/vitals; give cool humidified O₂ and recommend aerosolized racemic epinephrine. Laryngospasm is the most serious complication.
Watch outNot in your sources: a numeric quantitative cuff-leak pass/fail cutoff; the test itself is covered by Hess, not the decks.
SourceHess Ch 17 · Hess Ch 20 · Hess Ch 42 · RCP 105 Airway Management, S64 · RCP 203 Lssn 6a S63

15All weaning/extubation thresholds on one page

  • Strength/reserve: MIP −20 to −30 cm H₂O or more negative in 20 sec; VC > 10 mL/kg; spontaneous VT ≥ 5 mL/kg.
  • Breathing pattern: RSBI < 100 using VT in L; V̇E normal 6–10 L/min; SBT RR ≤ 30–35.
  • Oxygenation: P/F ≥ 150; PaO₂ > 60, SaO₂ > 90%, FiO₂ ≤ 0.40; PEEP ≤ 5–8 cm H₂O.
  • Acid–base/mechanics: pH ≥ 7.25 screening, ≥ 7.30 SBT; shunt < 20%; static compliance > 30 mL/cm H₂O; VD/VT < 60%.
  • Airway: alert enough to protect/clear; MEP < 40 means ineffective cough; suction > every 2 h precludes extubation.
  • Trial/outcome: up to 30 min with PS 5–8, CPAP ≈ 5, T-piece, or ATC; success means no support for ≥ 48 h.
Watch outDeck RSBI < 100, VC > 10 mL/kg, SBT up to 30 min; Hess/RCP 203 also give < 105, 15 mL/kg, 30–120 min.
SourceSources cited in items 1–14

16Why patients fail weaning, and what to fix first

  • Failure causes: increased airflow resistance, decreased compliance, and respiratory-muscle fatigue; correct the cause before trying again.
  • Resistance: small/blocked/kinked/bitten ETT, circuit condensation, or bronchospasm; use size 8 or larger ETT when appropriate.
  • Compliance: sudden decline suggests tension pneumothorax, effusion, or main-stem migration; gradual decline suggests pneumonia, ARDS, or atelectasis.
  • Fatigue: poor oxygen delivery, undernutrition, electrolyte deficits, and disuse weaken muscles; overfeeding increases CO₂ demand.
  • Signs/rest: tachypnea, respiratory alternans, abdominal paradox, and rising PaCO₂ suggest fatigue; rest may require 24 hours.
  • Prevention: short T-tube trials and PS retrain the diaphragm; total controlled-ventilation rest can cause atrophy within 24 hours.
SourceRCP 105 Weaning & Extubation / Pharmacotherapy, S27, 28, 29, 30 · RCP 105 Management of MV, S58, 59 · Hess Ch 20

1What Therapeutic Hypothermia is: target temperature, who gets it, how and for how long

  • Current exam target: TTM 32–37.5 °C for at least 36 h in adults remaining unresponsive after ROSC.
  • Purpose: controlled temperature reduces brain metabolic demand and secondary neurologic injury; maintain oxygenation, ventilation, and perfusion while avoiding hypotension/hypoxemia.
  • HIE: severe cerebral hypoxia damages brain/spinal neurons; causes include respiratory failure/CO, cardiac arrest/shock, or reduced CPP from low MAP/high ICP.
  • Methods: surface pads/blankets or internal endovascular cooling; contemporary temperature control also includes fever prevention at normothermic targets.
Watch outUse updated TH deck 32–37.5 °C for at least 36 h; older TH/HACA 32–34 °C for 24 h and older TTM 32–36 °C remain source-specific.
Watch outHess calls adult TBI TH likely beneficial; updated instructor deck says routine TH is not recommended for TBI.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S3, 9, 11 · RCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S11 · Hess Ch 18 · Hess Ch 1 · Hess Glossary

2Hyperglycemia, Hypoglycemia and Hypokalemia in the cooled, brain-injured patient

  • Glucose: normal 70–110 mg/dL; cooling causes insulin resistance/hyperglycemia, while rewarming may cause hypoglycemia as insulin sensitivity returns.
  • TH monitoring: updated notes give usual glucose 80–180 mg/dL; Hess general ICU nutrition target is 140–180 mg/dL, hypoglycemia < 70 mg/dL.
  • Potassium: exam normal 3.8–5.0 mEq/L; cooling shifts K⁺ into cells and cold diuresis wastes it; rewarming risks rebound hyperkalemia.
  • Hypokalemia: weakness, poor respiratory-muscle function, PVCs, flattened T waves, and severe arrhythmias; insulin and β₂ agonists can shift K⁺ intracellularly.
  • KCl hazards: oral or slow diluted IV replacement; never undiluted. Deck: UOP > 40 to 50 mL/hour, concentration < 40 mEq/L.
  • Deck limits: KCl < 40 mEq in one hour or < 200 mEq in 24 hours.
Watch outK⁺ normals differ: use RCP 103 3.8–5.0 mEq/L; RCP 105 says 3 to 5 mEq/L, Hess 3.5–5.5 mmol/L.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S4 · RCP 103 Clinical Data, S51, 53 · RCP 105 Management of MV, S53–55 · RCP 203 Lssn 1 S11 · Hess Ch 18 · Hess Ch 11 · Hess Ch 5

3Cold Diuresis

  • Cold diuresis: increased urine output during cooling; the TH deck attributes it to increased renal blood flow.
  • Mechanism: peripheral vasoconstriction shifts blood centrally → ANP release and falling ADH → large volumes of dilute urine.
  • Consequences: loss of fluid, potassium, magnesium, and phosphate; hypovolemia lowers preload and cardiac output.
SourceHess Ch 4, 5 (other diuresis) · updated TH coverage: item 14

4Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

  • GCS: eyes up to 4 + verbal up to 5 + motor up to 6; minimum 3, maximum 15. Motor best predicts neurologic outcome.
  • Eyes: spontaneous 4, voice 3, pain 2, none 1. Verbal: oriented 5, confused 4, inappropriate words 3, incomprehensible 2, none 1.
  • Motor: commands 6, localizes 5, withdraws 4, flexion/decorticate 3, extension/decerebrate 2, none 1.
  • Severity: mild 13–15, moderate 9–12, severe 3–8; GCS ≤ 8 prompts intubation/airway-protection consideration, with clinical judgment.
  • Example: eyes to pain E2 + incomprehensible sounds V2 + withdrawal M4 = GCS 8.
  • Confounders: sedation/paralysis lower scores without proving severe injury; intubated verbal response is untestable, giving 2T to 10T.
Watch outRCP 105 slide text mild 13–14 and < 8 intubation conflict with its notes; use 13–15 and ≤ 8.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S20, 21 · RCP 203 Lssn 2a S8 · Hess Ch 1 · Hess Ch 41 · Hess Ch 18

5Cell Injury, Excitatory Neurotransmitters and Apoptosis

  • Timeline: cerebral circulation stops → oxygen stores depleted within about 20 seconds; complete anoxia exhausts glucose/ATP within 5 minutes.
  • Injury chain: energy failure → Na⁺/K⁺ pump failure/depolarization → excess glutamate → calcium overload → destructive enzymes and neuronal death.
  • Glutamate: principal excitatory brain neurotransmitter; excessive release or reduced uptake promotes excitotoxicity and cell-membrane damage.
  • Apoptosis: programmed cell death; normally physiologic but harmful when needed cells die, including surfactant-producing type II cells in ARDS.
  • Cooling: lowers metabolism/O₂ use; may reduce glutamate release, calcium influx, and caspase activation while stabilizing membranes—proposed protective mechanisms.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S4, 5, 9, 11 · RCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S11 · RCP 103 Diffuse Alveolar Disease — ARDS, S5 · Hess Ch 44 · Hess Glossary

6Autoregulation (cerebral) and CPP

  • Autoregulation: cerebral vessels change resistance to keep brain blood flow stable despite pressure changes; severe hypotension/TBI can disrupt it.
  • Formula: CPP = MAP − ICP. TH/HIE exam: CPP 70–80 mm Hg, critical ≥ 70; ICP 8–12 mm Hg, critical 20.
  • Example: MAP 90, ICP rising 10 to 30 → CPP 90 − 10 = 80 to 90 − 30 = 60.
  • Injury: lost autoregulation makes brain flow dependent on BP/ICP; improve CPP by raising MAP, lowering ICP, or both.
Watch outUse TH/HIE CPP 70–80 (≥ 70), ICP 8–12; Trauma/Hess goal CPP > 60 mm Hg. Hess normal ICP < 10 and CPP > 80.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S8, 10, 14 · RCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S52, 53 · RCP 202 Lssn 7b S23 · Hess Ch 41 · Hess Ch 53

7ABG changes based on temperature (T°)

  • Temperature: analyzer measures at 37 °C; cooling raises gas solubility, lowering actual PaO₂/PaCO₂ and raising pH compared with the printout.
  • Deck formulas: PO₂ = measured PO₂ − 5 × (37 − T); PCO₂ = measured PCO₂ − 2 × (37 − T).
  • pH: measured pH + 0.015 × (37 − T); warming produces the opposite changes.
  • Example: at 33 °C, measured pH 7.40, PCO₂ 40, PO₂ 90 → 7.46, 32, 70 by deck formulas; Hess gives PO₂ 68.
  • Strategies: alpha-stat uses uncorrected 37 °C values; pH-stat targets actual pH at 7.4. Updated TH deck says follow prescribed strategy.
  • Other effects: cooling shifts oxyhemoglobin curve left, increases affinity, lowers P50, and reduces PETCO₂ as CO₂ production falls.
Watch outRCP 203 notes say reported gases are lower than actual in hypothermia; this reverses their own table. Actual gases are lower.
Watch outRCP 105 says correct ABGs; Hess says routine correction unnecessary. Updated TH says follow prescribed strategy; Hess changes PO₂ 7%/°C, PCO₂ 4%/°C, pH 0.0146/°C.
SourceRCP 203 Lssn 5b S8, 9 · RCP 105 Monitoring in MV, S10, 11 · Hess Ch 4 · Hess Ch 50 · Hess Ch 2

8Advantages and Disadvantages of Therapeutic Hypothermia

  • Benefits: lowers brain metabolism/O₂ consumption and reperfusion inflammatory responses; can improve post-arrest neurologic outcomes.
  • Complications: excessive cooling causes arrhythmias/coagulopathy; hyperglycemia and pneumonia/sepsis occur, requiring temperature and infection monitoring.
  • Humidity: Hess sets humidifier to target core temperature; avoid HME in hypothermia because less exhaled humidity is available.
  • RT effects: interpret gases with temperature documented; hypothermia affects PtcO₂ accuracy and can cause bradycardia < 60/min.
  • Shivering: raises O₂ consumption/CO₂ production and risks ischemia or hypercapnic failure; excessive phrenic-nerve cooling during CABG can paralyze hemidiaphragms.

9Ventilating the post-arrest / brain-injured patient: PaCO₂, PaO₂ and ICP

  • PaCO₂ target: normal 35–45 mm Hg; temporary 30–35 mm Hg only for emergency ICP control or impending herniation.
  • Mechanism: higher PaCO₂ dilates cerebral vessels and raises ICP; lower PaCO₂ constricts them, lowering ICP but risking cerebral ischemia.
  • Airway care: sedate against cough/dyssynchrony ICP spikes; reduce unnecessary suctioning, but clear obstructing secretions.
  • Oxygenation: TBI deck PaO₂ > 60 mm Hg and SaO₂ > 90%; updated TH after ROSC PaO₂ 60–105 and MAP ≥ 65.
  • Hess post-arrest: start 10 breaths/min, titrate PETCO₂ 35–40 mm Hg; minimum FiO₂ for SpO₂ 94%; treat SBP < 90 mm Hg.
Watch outSlide text permits PaCO₂ as low as 26 mm Hg during first 24 hours; exam target is notes/current-practice 35–45, emergency 30–35 only.
SourceRCP 105 Critical Issues in MV — HIE/TBI, S22 · Hess Ch 18 · Hess Ch 41 · Hess Ch 53

10TTM today, from the TH PowerPoint: temperature, who, how long — and the evidence behind it

  • Current exam: adults unresponsive after ROSC: constant 32–37.5 °C for at least 36 hours; protect brain from secondary injury.
  • Support: maintain MAP ≥ 65 mm Hg, individualized; avoid hypoxemia, excessive O₂, and major PaCO₂ abnormalities. Fever prevention is central.
  • Metabolism: cerebral metabolic rate falls about 5–7% per °C cooling; another slide gives 5–8% per 1 °C.

11Why TH is not used routinely in traumatic brain injury

  • TBI: mechanical injury causes edema, impaired perfusion, inflammation, and raised ICP; cooling may lower ICP without improving neurologic outcomes.
  • Exam answer: routine prophylactic hypothermia is not recommended; prevent fever and preserve cerebral perfusion.
  • Evidence: Clifton severe-TBI trial found no survival/neurologic improvement and more complications; selected rescue protocols differ from routine cooling.
  • Risks: hypotension lowers CPP; bradycardia, electrolyte disturbance, hyperglycemia, coagulopathy, and infection may outweigh benefit.
  • Rewarming: proceed slowly to avoid instability and rebound intracranial hypertension.
Watch outHess says adult TBI TH is likely beneficial; use instructor TH deck: routine TH is not recommended to improve outcomes.

12How cooling protects the brain — the deck’s five mechanism charts

  • Cell death: necrosis disrupts membranes and causes inflammation; apoptosis is regulated/programmed death with cell shrinkage, blebs, and DNA fragmentation.
  • Inflammation/oxidation: cooling suppresses inflammatory mediators and reduces free-radical production; it is not itself an antioxidant.
  • Excitotoxicity: excess glutamate increases intracellular calcium and energy use; cooling may reduce glutamate/calcium influx and preserve mitochondrial ATP.
  • Apoptosis/BBB: cooling may reduce caspase activation and preserve the blood–brain barrier; membrane stabilization may reduce edema and hyperexcitability.
  • Fever/seizures: temperature control prevents fever and lowers metabolic demand; seizures still need independent treatment.
  • Reperfusion: injury continues after ROSC through inflammation, microcirculatory dysfunction, and lost autoregulation; hypotension worsens cerebral oxygen mismatch.
  • Qualification: these are proposed or experimentally observed protective mechanisms, without proving improved outcomes for every patient.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 9 S7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

13The three phases — induction, maintenance, rewarming — and shivering

  • Induction: document neurologic status, begin sedation/shivering control, activate protocol, and continuously monitor core temperature/cardiac rhythm; target 32–37.5 °C.
  • Maintenance: control temperature for at least 36 h after ROSC; suggested fluctuation no more than 0.2–0.5 °C; monitor electrolytes, glucose, skin, seizures.
  • Rewarming: controlled 0.2–0.5 °C/hour; watch hypotension, rising K⁺, falling glucose; notes stop potassium several hours before rewarming.
  • After warming: prevent fever; notes specify 72 h after ROSC. Overshoot is rebound hyperthermia; afterdrop is further cooling from returning peripheral blood.
  • Shivering: up to 40% of TTM patients; raises VO₂/VCO₂ and demand, slowing cooling and potentially lowering brain oxygen tension.
  • Treatment: sedation, buspirone/meperidine, and skin counter-warming; monitored neuromuscular blockade is last resort for refractory shivering.
Watch outRewarming ranges differ within deck: 0.2–0.5 °C/h versus 0.25–0.5 °C/h; both are protocol-dependent suggestions.

14Adverse effects of cooling, system by system — cold diuresis, potassium and glucose

  • Cooling: insulin resistance causes hyperglycemia; intracellular shifts/cold diuresis deplete K⁺, magnesium, phosphate, and sometimes calcium.
  • Rewarming: returning insulin sensitivity risks hypoglycemia; K⁺ moves extracellularly, risking rebound hyperkalemia/dysrhythmias. Reassess replacement before warming.
  • Monitoring: most TH protocols use glucose 80–180 mg/dL; check every 1–2 h during rewarming per notes.
  • Cold diuresis: vasoconstriction centralizes blood → ANP release/ADH fall → dilute urine → hypovolemia, lower preload/output, and electrolyte loss.
  • Cardiac: bradycardia and conduction slowing occur; HR 45–55 at 33 °C expected. Deep cooling/low K⁺/Mg²⁺ increase ventricular arrhythmias.
  • Bleeding/infection: platelet dysfunction below about 35 °C, slower clotting below about 33 °C; labs at 37 °C may miss dysfunction, and fever may be masked.
  • Other effects: slow gastric emptying/drug metabolism, retained secretions, reduced cough, skin injury, and shivering may complicate care.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 9 S29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 36, 37, 38, 39

  • ABG strategy: alpha-stat uses 37 °C values; pH-stat uses corrected values. Follow the prescribed strategy; correction is not automatically required.

2Static vs Dynamic Compliance: the difference, the formulas, a worked example

  • Compliance: ΔV ÷ ΔP. Static Cstat = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP), measured during inspiratory pause with no airflow.
  • Dynamic: Cdyn = VT ÷ (PIP − PEEP), using peak pressure while gas flows; the denominator includes resistive pressure.
  • Patterns: increased Raw raises PIP without raising Pplat; lower compliance raises both. Cdyn falls in both cases.
  • Example: VT 320 mL, Pplat 40, PEEP 26 cm H₂O → 320 ÷ (40 − 26) = 320 ÷ 14 ≈ 23 mL/cm H₂O.
  • Difference: dynamic value is lower because it includes resistance; values coincide when zero flow makes PIP = Pplat.

3Carbon Monoxide (CO) Levels: COHb percentages

  • COHb bands: RCP 203 3a/3b: 1–3% WNL, 5–10% smokers, 10–15% heavy smokers, > 20% CO poisoning.

  • Symptoms: Whitaker 20–30% headache/nausea/vomiting; above 40% loss of sensorium/coma. Severe poisoning includes unconsciousness, convulsions, neurologic impairment.

4How CO Is Measured: CO-oximetry, not pulse oximetry

  • Test: measure COHb with blood CO-oximetry; normal PaO₂/SpO₂ after smoke exposure does not exclude CO poisoning.
  • Why: PaO₂ measures dissolved oxygen; conventional two-wavelength pulse oximetry cannot distinguish COHb reliably and can falsely overestimate saturation.
  • Example: deck bedside correction subtracts COHb: SpO₂ 90% − COHb 7% = true saturation 83%.
  • Exhaled screening: CO ppm ÷ 6 ≈ HbCO%; 48 ppm ≈ 8%, only for conscious cooperative patients with moderate poisoning.
  • SpCO limits: noninvasive multiwavelength measurement accuracy ±6%; unsuitable for triage/management. Confirm blood HbCO, and sample near exposure when possible.

5COHb Half-Life and Treatment of CO Poisoning

  • Exam half-lives: room air ~4–5 hours; 100% O₂ ~60–90 minutes; HBO often ~20–30 minutes.
  • Immediate treatment: give 100% O₂ promptly through tight nonrebreather with inflated reservoir; intubate/FiO₂ 1.0 if airway protection or ventilation fails.
  • Hess oxygen: nonrebreather 15 L/min irrespective of SpO₂/PaO₂; continue to a safe HbCO level. Do not delay for HBO decisions.
  • HBO: consider severe neurologic/cardiac involvement; typical 3 atm initially, then 2 or 3 atm, for 90 minutes with two 10-minute air breaks.
  • Limits: HBO benefit remains controversial; unstable patients are poor monoplace candidates. Wheeze/air trapping and fever increase pneumothorax/seizure risk.

6Why Compliance Falls in Burn and Inhalation Injury

  • Chest wall: circumferential rigid eschar plus edema restricts expansion; escharotomy may be needed to restore ventilation.
  • Ventilation: deck VT ≈ 6 mL/kg PBW, Pplat ≤ 30 cm H₂O, appropriate PEEP, and avoidance of ventilator-induced injury.

7CO vs Hydrogen Cyanide

  • CO: impairs hemoglobin oxygen transport/delivery; tissue hypoxia may occur despite apparently adequate dissolved oxygen.
  • Cyanide: burning nitrogen-containing compounds in low oxygen produces HCN; cytochrome oxidase inhibition blocks cellular oxygen use and causes systemic acidosis.
  • Treatment: suspected severe cyanide poisoning gets 100% O₂ plus hydroxocobalamin, which binds cyanide into cyanocobalamin for elimination.

1Access Points: where the cannulas go

3Difference Between VV and VA ECMO

3DKA on the ABG: Anion Gap, Compensation and Kussmaul Breathing

  • Case ABG: pH 6.98, PaCO₂ 24 mm Hg, HCO₃⁻ 6 mEq/L, PaO₂ 98 mm Hg, SaO₂ 97%: severe metabolic acidosis with partial respiratory compensation.

4The Kidney's Function in Acid–Base Balance

  • Division: lungs eliminate volatile carbonic acid as CO₂; kidneys excrete fixed/nonvolatile acids and regulate bicarbonate.
  • Renal response: acidic blood → excrete H⁺/retain HCO₃⁻; alkaline blood → excrete HCO₃⁻/retain H⁺. Proximal tubule reabsorbs most filtered bicarbonate.
  • Processes: glomerular filtration filters solutes; tubular reabsorption reclaims useful material; secretion moves H⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and waste into filtrate.
  • pH 7.31: increase HCO₃⁻ reabsorption/H⁺ secretion; make NH₃ to trap H⁺ as NH₄⁺; use phosphate buffering HPO₄²⁻ + H⁺ → H₂PO₄⁻.
  • Timing: renal compensation takes hours to days; chronic PaCO₂ changes require 3–5 days versus rapid lung responses.
  • Rule: each 10 mm Hg PaCO₂ rise → HCO₃⁻ ↑ ~3.5–4 mEq/L; each 10 mm Hg fall → HCO₃⁻ ↓ ~4–5 mEq/L.
  • Electrolytes: low chloride/potassium promote H⁺ loss and bicarbonate retention → metabolic alkalosis; urinary bicarbonate loss causes metabolic acidosis.

5Renal Failure and the Ventilated Patient: What the RT Sees

  • PPV chain: higher intrathoracic pressure → lower venous return/output → lower renal flow/UOP → renal dysfunction.
  • Renal failure: UOP ↓ while BUN, creatinine, and potassium ↑; azotemia means elevated blood nitrogenous wastes.
  • Four R’s: retention of waste, fluid, potassium, and drugs; drug accumulation can cause toxicity.
  • RT effects: retained fluid reaches lungs → crackles, hypoxemia, pulmonary edema; CPAP/BiPAP or mechanical ventilation may be needed.

3Parenteral Nutrition: When It Is Used, Peripheral vs Central Access, and Its Risks

  • RT/metabolic risks: overfeeding can raise CO₂ and cause respiratory acidosis; carbohydrate overload shifts phosphate intracellularly, causing hypophosphatemia.

4Enteral vs Parenteral: The Differences, and When to Start

  • Enteral preferred: functioning GI tract, fewer infections, lower cost, preserved mucosa/flora and IgA-mediated barrier protection; PN bypass risks gut atrophy.
  • Timing: start EN within 24–48 hours when critically ill patients cannot eat adequately; withhold during hemodynamic compromise until resuscitated/stable.
  • High risk: advance over 24–48 hours with refeeding monitoring; aim > 80% energy/protein goal within 48–72 hours.
  • CPG needs: protein 1.2–2.0 g/kg actual body weight per day; energy 25–30 kcal/kg/d if indirect calorimetry unavailable.

5Aspiration Precautions During Tube Feeding (Head of Bed and More)

  • Residuals: do not check routinely; if checked, avoid holding feeds for GRVs < 500 mL without other intolerance signs.

6Overfeeding, CO₂ Production and the Respiratory Quotient

  • RQ: VCO₂ / VO₂; carbohydrate ≈ 1.0, protein ≈ 0.8, fat ≈ 0.7. Higher RQ means more CO₂ produced.
  • Overfeeding: carbohydrate-driven lipogenesis increases CO₂ and ventilatory demand, causing hypercapnia, weaning difficulty, or prolonged ventilation.
  • Calories: Management deck hydrous dextrose 3.4 kcal/g and fat emulsion 9.1 kcal/g; Hess carbohydrate/protein 4.1 and fat 9.3 kcal/g.
  • Deck concept: fat supplies more calories with less CO₂; pulmonary formulas often provide 40% to 55% fat and less than 40% carbohydrate.
  • CPG: avoid specialty high-fat/low-carbohydrate formulas intended to manipulate RQ in ICU acute respiratory failure; prefer energy-dense/fluid-restricted feeds when appropriate.
Watch outDecks favor fat-based nutrition for ventilated CO₂ retainers; Hess CPG advises against specialty RQ-manipulating formulas in ICU acute respiratory failure. Both prioritize avoiding overfeeding.

7Undernutrition, Refeeding Syndrome and Phosphate: Why Feeding Affects Weaning

  • Undernutrition: respiratory muscles become fuel; reduced MIP/MEP, VC, endurance, immunity, surfactant, and albumin can hinder weaning and promote edema.
  • Risk flags: weight loss 10% or more over 6 months or body weight less than 80% of ideal.
  • Refeeding: restarting nutrition after starvation can cause severe electrolyte shifts, vitamin deficiency, hyperglycemia, arrhythmias, CHF, and respiratory failure.
  • Phosphate: depletion lowers ATP and respiratory-muscle function; monitor closely and replace when needed in acute respiratory failure.
  • Exam cutoff: deck calls phosphate < 1 mg/dL hypophosphatemia; Hess normal adult PO₄ is 2.7–4.5 mg/dL.
  • Stress: hypercatabolic phase may last 7 to 10 days and increase calorie needs by as much as 100%; magnesium deficiency also weakens muscles.

2Diaphragmatic Breathing

  • Diaphragmatic breathing: diaphragm contracts downward; belly expands during inhalation, with minimal chest or shoulder movement and less accessory-muscle use.
  • Purpose: improves breathing efficiency, increases VT and decreases RR; may reduce dyspnea and promote relaxation.
  • Teaching: semi-Fowler’s, forearms relaxed, knees bent; hand below xiphoid. Inhale slowly through nose, pushing abdomen against progressive hand resistance.
  • Breathing control: gentle lower-chest breathing at normal tidal volume and rate, with relaxed shoulders; used between active-cycle maneuvers.

3Pursed-Lip Breathing

  • Exam timing: inhale through nose about 2 s; exhale gently through pursed lips about 4 s, twice as long.
  • Mechanism: expiratory back-pressure splints small airways open, reducing premature collapse and air trapping while slowing breathing.
  • Use: particularly useful in COPD/emphysema; may reduce dyspnea and work of breathing and give patients better breathing control.

4Thoracic Breathing

  • Thoracic breathing: intercostals primarily engage; chest and shoulders rise, diaphragm is less active, and belly stays relatively still.
  • Deck distinction: shallow, less efficient chest breathing is linked with stress and sympathetic activity; diaphragmatic breathing is deeper and relaxing.
  • Thoracic expansion: separate ACB maneuver using deep inspiration and relaxed expiration; limit to three or four deep breaths to avoid fatigue and hyperventilation.
  • Lateral costal breathing: cup lower ribs and have patient breathe around the waist against resistance; useful after abdominal surgery.

5Hyperventilation vs Hypoventilation

  • Normal PaCO₂: 35–45 mm Hg; PaCO₂ varies inversely with alveolar ventilation. Breathing rate alone cannot identify ventilation adequacy.
  • Acute hyperventilation: PaCO₂ < 35 mm Hg with pH above 7.45 → respiratory alkalosis; hypoxemia is the most common cause.
  • Acute hypoventilation: PaCO₂ > 45 mm Hg with pH < 7.35 → respiratory acidosis from inadequate alveolar ventilation.
  • Causes: narcotics, sedatives, obstruction, severe asthma/COPD, neuromuscular weakness or fatigue; rapid, shallow breaths can still produce hypoventilation.
Watch outRCP 103 ABG S11 prints pH > 7.35; its notes define pH above 7.45. Exam answer: pH > 7.45.
SourceHess Ch 50,24 · RCP 103 ABG S4,6,11,12,16

6The Five Side by Side (the "know the difference" table)

  • Diaphragmatic: inspiratory diaphragm descent produces outward belly movement, little shoulder movement, efficient breathing and relaxation.
  • Pursed-lip: inhale through nose about 2 s, exhale about 4 s; expiratory back-pressure reduces airway collapse and air trapping.
  • Thoracic: intercostal chest breathing raises chest and shoulders; shallow and stress-associated, unlike therapeutic thoracic expansion with 3–4 deep breaths.
  • Hyperventilation: ventilation exceeds CO₂ production; PaCO₂ < 35 → ↑ pH → respiratory alkalosis, commonly triggered by hypoxemia.
  • Hypoventilation: ventilation is inadequate for CO₂ production; PaCO₂ > 45 → ↓ pH → respiratory acidosis, even with rapid, shallow breathing.

7Exercise Prescription

  • Progression: Borg < 4, heart rate < 80% predicted maximum and acceptable saturation → increase load for 3 to 5 minutes, then return.

1Pneumothorax: what it is and how it is diagnosed

  • Pneumothorax: air in the pleural space can collapse the lung; simple, open and tension forms differ in pressure buildup and external communication.

3Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

4Difference between Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) and Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

  • MAP: systemic tissue-driving pressure; MAP = [2(DBP) + SBP] ÷ 3 = CO × SVR. Hess normal range: 65–105 mm Hg.
  • Worked MAP: BP 110/70 → 70 + ⅓(110 − 70) = 70 + 13.3 ≈ 83 mm Hg.
  • ICP: pressure within the rigid skull; brain, CSF and venous blood compete for fixed volume. RCP 105 normal: 8–12 mm Hg, critical 20.
  • CPP: CPP = MAP − ICP; MAP 90 − ICP 10 = CPP 80. Trauma goal: CPP > 60 mm Hg.
  • Management: raise MAP or lower ICP to preserve perfusion; avoid SBP < 90 mm Hg, neck distortion, hypoxia, hypercapnia and excessive intrathoracic pressure.

5Difference between MRI and CT scans

8Difference between Blunt Force Trauma and Penetrating Trauma

  • Blunt: external force damages internal structures without necessarily breaking skin; motor vehicle crashes, falls and crush injuries are typical causes.
  • Penetrating: an object enters the chest; stab wounds cause localized track damage, while high velocity > 1500 ft/s causes cavitation and extensive injury.
  • Blunt patterns: contusions, rib fractures, pneumothorax, cardiac injury and deceleration aortic injury; serious injury may lack obvious external signs.
  • Penetrating patterns: tamponade, hemothorax, esophageal injury and small diaphragm tears; both mechanisms can impair ventilation, oxygenation and circulation.

1Difference Between Diagnostic and Therapeutic Bronchoscopy

  • Diagnostic: find the cause through airway inspection, BAL, washing, cytology or biopsy; biopsy samples tissue, while cytology examines cells.
  • Therapeutic: remove plugs/foreign bodies, control bleeding, dilate stenoses, debulk tumors or place stents to relieve airway obstruction.
  • Obstruction: intrinsic tumor may need debulking; external compression may need dilation/stenting; rigid scope provides definitive airway control.
  • Heat safety: reduce FiO₂ to 40% or less during airway heat therapy to prevent ignition; bronchoscopy can serve both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.

2Indications for Bronchoscopy

  • Nine indications: atelectasis, hemoptysis, cough, suspected malignancy, infection, foreign body aspiration, ILD, lung transplant and trauma.
  • Atelectasis/cough: try conservative clearance first for collapse; uncomplicated chronic cough does not routinely require bronchoscopy after appropriate evaluation.
  • Sampling: BAL/washing identifies infection; biopsy diagnoses malignancy; transbronchial biopsy is the gold standard for acute transplant rejection.
  • Foreign body: sudden coughing, wheezing and unilateral diminished sounds; rigid scope is preferred for large objects and pediatric foreign bodies.
  • Major airway problems: rigid bronchoscopy supports ventilation/suction in massive hemoptysis; bronchoscopy diagnoses tracheobronchial trauma and inhalation injury.

3Contraindications to Bronchoscopy

  • Major barriers: inability to oxygenate, unstable cardiovascular status or uncorrectable bleeding risk; avoid status asthmaticus and defer uncontrolled bronchospasm.
  • Oxygen/heart: PaO₂ < 70 mm Hg despite supplemental O₂ generally contraindicates bronchoscopy; Hess recommends waiting at least 6 weeks after MI.

  • Drug holds: aspirin need not stop; clopidogrel/ticagrelor ≥5 days before biopsy; warfarin 3–5 days; unfractionated heparin 4–6 hours; LMWH 24 hours.

4Difference Between Flexible and Rigid Bronchoscopes

  • Flexible: soft 3.5–6 mm shaft, tip rotation up to 210°; reaches distal airways for routine sampling, usually under topical anesthesia with sedation.
  • Rigid: straight steel tube, adult diameter 9–13 mm and length 40 cm; general anesthesia permits airway control, powerful suction and ventilation.
  • Rigid uses: severe central obstruction, large foreign bodies, major bleeding, silicone stents and dilation; pediatric foreign-body instrument of choice.
  • Flexible routes: nose, mouth, ETT or trach; oral route needs bite block. ETT must be ≥1.5 mm larger than scope, typically ≥8 mm ID.
  • History: rigid bronchoscopy began in 1897; RCP 105 gives flexible development in 1966 and US introduction in 1970.

7Difference Between COPD, GERD, Emphysema, and Lung Cancer

  • COPD: progressive irreversible airflow limitation encompassing chronic bronchitis and emphysema; diagnosis requires post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70.
  • Emphysema: permanent distal air-space enlargement with alveolar-wall destruction; reduced recoil, air trapping and ↓ DLCO. Selected advanced cases receive endobronchial valves.
  • Chronic bronchitis: productive cough for ≥3 months in each of 2 consecutive years; the other major COPD component.
  • GERD: stomach contents reflux into esophagus; microaspiration can trigger bronchoconstriction, inflammation and chronic cough.
  • Lung cancer: malignant epithelial neoplasm; bronchoscopy obtains tissue, and EBUS-TBNA helps mediastinal staging. NSCLC and SCLC are the major groups.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 7a S13,15,16,19,31 · RCP 103 COPD S2,3,6,7,14,16; Infectious S23; Lung Cancer S2,4,6,8,33; Asthma S13 · RCP 203 Lssn 3b S14 · RCP 105 Procedures S3 · Hess Ch 33,9,47; Glossary

8Difference Between Lavage, Washing, Brushing, and Biopsy

  • BAL: wedge scope in segmental/subsegmental bronchus to sample distal airways/alveoli; 20–60 mL aliquots, total ≤200 mL, suction < 120 cm H₂O.
  • Washing: 5–10 mL saline aliquots without wedging sample conducting airways; upper-airway flora can contaminate microbiology.
  • Brushing: standard brush collects surface cells for cytology; protected brush collects organisms with less upper-airway contamination.
  • Endobronchial biopsy: forceps obtain tissue from visible lesions; 3–6 samples in formalin, with bleeding the main complication.
  • TBLB: samples parenchyma beyond bronchial wall; minimum 6 pieces, 1–2 mm; pneumothorax 1–6% and significant bleeding (>50 mL) 2–9%.
  • TBNA: needle samples outside airway, including lymph nodes; EBUS guides aspiration in real time. Perform before other sampling to reduce contamination.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 7a S11,12,14,15,16,17 · RCP 105 Procedures S11,12 · RCP 103 Clinical Data S63 · RCP 203 Lssn 3b S15 · Hess Ch 9; Glossary

1Disease States Considered for Lung Transplant

4Lung-Protective Strategies During Mechanical Ventilation for Lung Transplant Patients

  • Donor protection: plateau pressure, ideally peak pressure, < 30 cm H₂O; prevent fluid overload while balancing PEEP against venous return and cardiac output.

5Recipient Candidacy and Exclusion Criteria

  • Transplant window: activation on waiting list to transplantation; ideal timing preserves enough exertional capacity for rehabilitation despite tangible disease progression.
  • Evaluation: multispecialty labs/imaging, cardiac assessment and esophageal studies; sarcoidosis/severe PH adds cardiac MRI, CF adds sinus CT.
  • Table malignancy exclusions: cancer in last 2 years; high-recurrence cancers need 5 years cancer-free. Selected localized cancers are exceptions.
  • Other barriers: uncorrectable major-organ dysfunction, bleeding, resistant infection, active TB, substance addiction, repeated nonadherence and poor rehabilitation potential.
  • Correctable issues: obesity > 130% IBW and inadequate rehabilitation may improve; revised deck states age alone is not an absolute contraindication.
  • HLA: donor-specific antibodies increase rejection risk; lung transplant does not routinely require close HLA matching.

6Primary Graft Dysfunction and Postoperative Care

  • PGD: ischemia/reperfusion acute lung injury within 72 hours; grade using P/F ratio plus pulmonary-edema infiltrates on ICU arrival and daily for 72 hours.
  • Grades: 0 = P/F > 300 without infiltrates; 1 = > 300 with infiltrates; 2 = 200–300 with infiltrates; 3 = < 200 with infiltrates.
  • Treatment: supportive lung-protective ventilation and careful fluids; inhaled vasodilators are often used, with ECMO for severe dysfunction.
  • Postoperative care: PJP/CMV prophylaxis follows risk and center protocol; bedside swallow and FEES precede regular diet to detect aspiration.
  • Recovery: chest-tube removal depends on drainage, air leaks and lung expansion; early ambulation and heparin when bleeding permits reduce VTE risk.

7Donor Evaluation and Management

  • Brain-death prerequisites: core ≥ 36 °C (97 °F), systolic BP ≥ 100 mm Hg, PaCO₂ 35–45 mm Hg, no hypoxia and euvolemia.
  • Clinical exam: absent brain-origin motor, corneal, oculovestibular, gag and cough responses plus positive apnea test.
  • Apnea test: disconnect up to 8 minutes; no breathing with PaCO₂ > 60 mm Hg or > 20 mm Hg above baseline supports brain death.
  • Donor criteria: typically age < 55, ABO compatibility and PaO₂ > 300 mm Hg on FiO₂ 1.0 with PEEP 5 cm H₂O.
  • DCDD: death declaration requires at least 2 minutes observation; usual recovery limit 60 to 120 minutes after support withdrawal, adjusted by protocol.
  • Preservation: chilled low-potassium dextran flush, storage 4–8 °C; warm ischemia worsens reperfusion injury and PGD.
Watch outHess/earlier slides link DCDD with more PGD/BOS; revised deck describes comparable selected-donor outcomes without a consistent increase. EVLP duration varies; 12 hours is not a fixed ceiling.

8Donor Management and Procurement — What Happens Before the Lungs Leave

  • Donor goal: prevent interstitial fluid overload; keep plateau pressure and ideally peak pressure less than 30 cm H₂O while monitoring oxygenation.
  • Hemodynamics: diuretics may require vasopressors; excessive PEEP lowers venous return/cardiac output. Brain-death diabetes insipidus may need desmopressin.
  • Procurement order: sternotomy → intravenous heparin → pulmonary-artery prostaglandin → aortic cross-clamp as pressure falls → chilled Perfadex flush.
  • Perfadex: pulmonary-artery flush cools tissue and removes vascular blood, helping prevent thrombosis and endothelial injury.
  • Storage/matching: 4–8 °C slows metabolism; avoid freezing. Match mainly by height; oversized lungs may reduce grade 3 PGD versus undersized lungs.
SourceHess Ch 46 · RCP 202 Lssn 8a S20,22,23

9The Two Operations, Step by Step — and CPB versus ECMO

  • Single lung: thoracotomy, contralateral one-lung ventilation and pulmonary-artery clamp; anastomoses run bronchus → pulmonary artery → pulmonary veins to left atrium.
  • Bilateral: sequential replacement, often through clamshell incision; replace worst V/Q lung first, then ventilate first allograft while replacing second lung.
  • Ventilation hazard: nonventilated perfused lung creates shunt; in single-lung COPD, compliant native lung may take excessive VT and overdistend.
  • VV ECMO: venous drainage and venous return provide respiratory support only; VA returns arterially for respiratory plus circulatory support.
  • CPB comparison: ECMO had fewer transfusions, PGD and complications with shorter intubation; comparison found no 30-day or 1-year survival difference.

  • Candidacy additions: evaluation may include DEXA and GFR staging; accepted patients may need relocation and benchmarks before active listing.
  • Retransplant: indication after failure of first graft; recipient sensitization makes evaluation and repeat surgery more complex.

1What the respiratory system does: gas exchange, gas distribution and the five processes of respiration

  • Primary functions: gas distribution through upper/conducting airways and gas exchange at the acinus or alveolar level.
  • Ventilation/perfusion: ventilation moves air from atmosphere to lungs; perfusion moves blood through the cardiovascular system.
  • Diffusion: O₂ and CO₂ move down pressure gradients across the alveolar-capillary membrane.
  • Respiration: external exchanges gas between alveoli and blood; internal exchanges between blood and tissues. Ventilation alone is only one respiratory process.
SourceHess Ch 49

2Growth and development: life stages and the five stages of fetal lung development

  • Embryonic: 4–6 weeks; lung bud forms proximal airways, trachea and major bronchi. Memorize table order: embryonic → pseudoglandular → canalicular → saccular → alveolar.
  • Pseudoglandular: 6–16 weeks; conducting airways and near-complete diaphragm develop; survival after premature delivery is highly unlikely.
  • Canalicular: 17–26 weeks; vascular bed, acini and type I/II cells develop; limited gas exchange permits survival with advanced intensive care.
  • Saccular: 27–36 weeks; early gas-exchange units and surfactant expand potential exchange surface; saccules later become alveoli.
  • Alveolar: 36 weeks–beyond birth; birth average ≈ 50 million alveoli, reaching ≈ 300 million by age 8.
  • Life stages: embryo ends 8th gestational week; fetus begins 9th week; neonate ends week 4 after birth; infant continues to 1 year.

3Birth transition and infant vs adult airway differences

  • First breath: approximately 40 to 80 cm H₂O transpulmonary gradient and approximately 40 mL overcome fluid viscosity and alveolar surface tension.
  • Fluid clearance: fluid enters interstitium within several breaths; capillaries and lymphatics may need several hours to remove it.
  • Infant anatomy: larger relative head/tongue, smaller nasal passages, rounder jaw and higher larynx increase obstruction risk and favor nose breathing.
  • Exam narrowest point: infant/child = cricoid ring; adult = glottis. Infant circumferential edema of 1 mm can reduce diameter by more than 50%.

4Upper respiratory tract: nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, pharynx

  • Upper tract: nose/mouth through larynx; lower tract begins at trachea. Nasal mucosa and turbinates filter, warm and humidify air.
  • Sinuses: maxillary, frontal, sphenoid and ethmoid cavities lighten skull and provide resonance; infected postnasal drainage commonly triggers chronic cough.
  • Pharynx: nasopharynx carries air only; oropharynx and laryngopharynx carry air and food, directing swallowed food posteriorly to esophagus.
  • Obstruction/protection: enlarged tonsils/adenoids/uvula can impede breathing; epiglottis covers laryngeal entry during swallowing to prevent aspiration.

5Larynx and glottis

  • Larynx: cartilaginous airway between pharynx and trachea housing vocal cords; produces sound and helps condition inspired air.
  • Landmarks: hyoid sits above larynx; epiglottis is uppermost cartilage; thyroid is largest; cricoid below thyroid is the only complete airway ring.
  • Cricothyroid membrane: between thyroid and cricoid cartilages; emergency opening through it is a cricothyrotomy.
  • Glottis: opening between vocal cords; closes during swallowing, while cord vibration produces sound. Arytenoids and corniculates complete posterior larynx.
SourceHess Ch 49 Table 49-3; Glossary · RCP 105 Airway S63 · Hess Ch 17

6Trachea, carina and the main stem bronchi (angles)

  • Trachea: 10 to 13 cm long, approximately 2.0 to 2.5 cm diameter; 16 to 20 C-shaped cartilages prevent inspiratory collapse.
  • Carina: tracheal bifurcation into mainstem bronchi; lining is pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, damaged by smoking.
  • Angles: right mainstem 20 to 30 degrees from vertical; left 45 to 55 degrees. Rightward aspiration and catheter passage are more likely.
  • ETT position: exam tip target 4–6 cm above carina with head neutral; excessive depth commonly enters right mainstem, causing uneven breath sounds.

7Bronchial tree branching, generations, and the conducting vs respiratory zones

  • Branching: main bronchi → lobar → segmental → subsegmental bronchi → bronchioles; dichotomous branching splits each airway into two smaller branches.
  • Bronchioles: < 1 mm diameter without cartilage; elastic parenchymal forces support them. Terminal bronchioles are the last conducting airway.
  • Zones: conducting airways distribute air without gas exchange; respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts/sacs and alveoli form the gas-exchanging acinus.
  • Figure generations: conducting 0–16; respiratory bronchioles 17–19; alveolar ducts 20–22; alveolar sacs 23.
  • Dead-space example: anatomic dead space ≈ 1 mL per lb; a 180-lb patient has about 180 × 1 mL = 180 mL.

8The lungs: lobes, fissures, segments and gross facts

  • Gross lungs: 5 lobes and 18 segments; adult lungs weigh about 800 g and are about 90% gas, 10% tissue.
  • Right: 3 lobes, 10 segments; horizontal fissure separates upper/middle and oblique fissure separates middle/lower. Right lung is larger and heavier.
  • Left: 2 lobes, 8 segments, separated by oblique fissure; lingular segments correspond in appearance to right-middle-lobe segments.
  • Segments: right upper apical/anterior/posterior; middle lateral/medial; lower superior and anterior/posterior/lateral/medial basal.
  • Left segments: upper apical posterior, anterior, superior/inferior lingular; lower superior, anterior medial basal, lateral basal and posterior basal.
  • Hilum: medial entry for bronchi, vessels and nerves; segment localization guides bronchoscopy, charting, percussion and postural drainage.

9Alveoli, the alveolar-capillary membrane, and alveolar cell types (type I vs type II)

  • Alveoli: approximately 300 million, each 200 to 300 microns; vascular endothelium, interstitium and alveolar epithelium form the diffusion barrier.
  • Type I: flat squamous cells cover 90% to 95% of alveolar surface; thin primary gas-exchange barrier, vulnerable to injury.
  • Type II: cuboidal cells cover 5% to 10%; produce surfactant, replace injured type I cells and move sodium/water toward blood.
  • Surfactant: 90% phospholipids, 10% protein; DPPC lowers end-expiratory surface tension and prevents collapse. Deficiency causes atelectasis.
  • Collateral ventilation: pores of Kohn connect neighboring alveoli; channels of Lambert connect alveoli to adjacent terminal bronchioles.
  • Fluid defenses: tight junctions, active water transport, low pulmonary hydrostatic pressure, surfactant proteins and lymphatic drainage oppose alveolar flooding.
Watch outHess also gives approximately 95% type I and only 5% type II, with glossary type I 90%; learn the chapter’s 90%–95% / 5%–10% ranges.
SourceHess Ch 49; Glossary · RCP 103 ILD S4; ARDS S5,6; COPD S6

10Interstitium and pulmonary lymphatics

  • Interstitium: space between alveolar epithelium and vascular endothelium; extends from visceral pleura to hilum/mediastinum, supporting structure and fluid balance.
  • Matrix: collagen limits overdistention and makes up 15% to 20% of dry lung weight; elastin provides recoil.
  • Compartments: tight space lies at alveolar-capillary interface; loose space surrounds small airways and alveolar structures. Thickening impairs diffusion and causes hypoxemia.
  • Lymphatics: remove excess fluid, protein, bacteria and foreign material; exit at hilum, drain mediastinal nodes and return through thoracic duct.
SourceHess Ch 49 · RCP 103 ILD S4

11Pleurae, pleural space and mediastinum

  • Visceral pleura: covers lung and fissures; vagal innervation without general pain sensation. Parietal pleura lines chest wall and is pain-sensitive.
  • Parietal nerves: intercostal and phrenic supply; irritation may refer pain to neck root and shoulder.
  • Pleural fluid: < 20 mL low-protein fluid allows frictionless movement and couples lung to chest wall; opposing recoil maintains negative pleural pressure.
  • Worked volume: deck estimates 0.26 mL/kg; for 70-kg adult, 0.26 × 70 ≈ 18 mL, compatible with Hess’s < 20 mL.
  • Mediastinum: between pleural sacs, containing thoracic structures except lungs/pleurae; includes heart/pericardium, great vessels, trachea, esophagus and major nerves.
SourceHess Ch 49 Fig 49-12 · RCP 103 Pleura/Chest Wall S3

12Bony thorax and chest-wall mechanics

  • Bony thorax: sternum, ribs, thoracic vertebrae, clavicles and scapulae protect heart/lungs and stabilize chest wall during pressure changes.
  • Sternum: manubrium connects ribs 1–2, body connects directly to ribs 3–7, and xiphoid forms the tip.
  • Ribs: 12 pairs; bucket-handle movement lifts outward/upward while sternum rises like pump handle. Vertebrae permit rib elevation and rotation.
  • AP diameter: normally about half chest width; chronic obstructive air trapping increases it, producing barrel chest.
SourceHess Ch 49 · RCP 103 Clinical Data S24; COPD S6

13Muscles of ventilation: diaphragm (primary) and accessory muscles of inspiration and expiration

  • Diaphragm: primary inspiratory muscle contracts/flattens, lowering intrathoracic pressure; relaxation plus lung recoil produces passive exhalation.
  • Excursion/nerve: ≈ 1.5 cm normally, up to 6 to 10 cm with high effort; phrenic supply C3–C5.
  • Inspiratory accessory muscles: scalene, sternocleidomastoid, pectoralis major, trapezius and external intercostals expand chest; recruitment rises with exercise/distress.
  • Active expiration: internal intercostals, rectus abdominis, external/internal obliques and transverse abdominis help against resistance, high demand, cough or sneeze.
  • Cord injury: injury at or above C3–C5 may paralyze diaphragm; thoracic injury impairs intercostal/accessory muscles and cough, making ventilation diaphragm-dependent.

14Innervation of the respiratory system (summary)

  • Phrenic C3–C5: motor supply to diaphragm; intercostal and phrenic nerves also supply the highly pain-sensitive parietal pleura.
  • Vagus: supplies visceral pleura, which lacks pain sensation; vagus and esophageal plexus pass through superior mediastinum.
  • Olfactory: nasal smell sensation; nasal sensory neurons also initiate sneeze responses.
  • Autonomic control: pulmonary vascular nerves release acetylcholine/norepinephrine; parasympathetic stimulation increases submucosal-gland mucus, while goblet cells lack clear direct innervation.
SourceHess Ch 49; Glossary · RCP 104 Pharm Ch 9 S14,15

15Mucociliary apparatus (mucociliary clearance)

  • Mucociliary escalator: goblet-cell mucus traps debris; cilia beat in watery sol layer and move gel upward for swallowing or expulsion.
  • Exam numbers: ciliated:goblet ratio approximately 5:1; 200 cilia/cell; length 7 µm in large airways, 5 µm or less in smaller bronchioles.
  • Secretions: deck gives 100 mL/24 hours secreted and 6,000 goblet cells/mm²; fluid homeostasis is central, while dehydration impairs secretion production.
  • Layers: gel 0.5–20 µm; periciliary layer ≈ 7–10 micrometers with surfactant at cilia tips separating fluid from gel.
  • Impairment: smoking, CF, COPD, asthma and several drugs impair transport; mucus viscosity comes from mucoglycoproteins.

16Airway and vascular smooth muscle; the pulmonary circulation's tone

  • Smooth muscle: involuntary muscle lines airways and pulmonary vessels; contraction makes cells shorter/wider and changes airway or vessel caliber.
  • Pulmonary circulation: less smooth muscle and lower tone, pressure and resistance than systemic arteries; vessel caliber redistributes regional blood flow.
  • Vasoconstriction: hypoxia, hypercapnia and ↓ pH constrict pulmonary vessels; hypoxia acts directly on smooth muscle to help match ventilation and perfusion.
  • Mediators: acetylcholine/norepinephrine from nerves and histamine/leukotrienes from mast cells alter vascular tone.

17Mast cells, macrophages and dendritic cells

  • Mast cells: 10 to 20 microns; release granule mediators histamine/serotonin, membrane-derived leukotrienes and cytokines; increased activity contributes to asthma.
  • Macrophages: bone-marrow-derived phagocytes remove acinar bacteria, present antigen and produce cytokines, particularly IL-1 and TNF; also called type III cells.
  • Dendritic cells: bone-marrow-derived antigen presenters take antigens to draining lymph nodes and activate T lymphocytes; chronic irritants increase them and steroids reduce them.

  • Thorax/muscles: sternum, ribs, vertebrae, clavicles and scapulae support diaphragm and accessory inspiratory/expiratory muscle activity.

1Check pulses where: adult, child (pediatric) and infant

  • Adult: at and beyond puberty; check the nearest carotid artery for no more than 10 seconds. Absent or uncertain pulse → 30 compressions.
  • Child: 1 year to puberty, about 12–14 yr; check carotid or femoral artery for less than 10 seconds.
  • Pediatric CPR: pulse < 60 beats/min with poor perfusion despite oxygenation and ventilation warrants compressions; infants may show cyanosis or pallor.
  • Infant: neonates through ≈ 1 year; palpate the brachial artery with index and middle fingers inside the upper arm.
  • Assessment: check breathing and pulse together; agonal gasps are inadequate. Unresponsive choking adults or children receive compressions without a pulse check.

2What ensures the best chance of survival

  • Best survival determinant: a trained rescuer ready, willing and able to provide BLS; start CPR within 4 minutes and ALS within 8 minutes.
  • Defibrillation: survival decreases 7–10% per minute without CPR, versus 3–4% per minute with CPR; rapid shock is crucial for VF/pulseless VT.
  • Compressions: uninterrupted high-quality CPR maintains coronary and cerebral perfusion; interruptions lower perfusion pressure and increase mortality.
  • Outcome factors: initial rhythm, time to CPR, time to defibrillation, CPR duration and witnessed status determine prognosis.
  • Devices: only the defibrillator has consistently improved long-term out-of-hospital survival; drugs cannot replace CPR and shock.

3Head-tilt–chin-lift vs jaw-thrust maneuver

  • No suspected C-spine injury: use head-tilt–chin-lift; advancing the jaw lifts the tongue from the posterior pharynx.
  • Suspected C-spine injury: use jaw thrust from behind the head; lift both mandibular angles forward and upward without extending the neck.
  • If jaw thrust fails: gently tilt the head backward until the airway opens; posterior tongue displacement commonly obstructs unconscious patients.
  • Infants: maintain neutral or sniffing position; excessive extension or pressure in soft tissue under the chin can obstruct the airway.
  • After opening: inspect for objects, vomit or blood; remove visible material or suction. Never perform a blind finger sweep.

4MOANS: predicting difficult bag-mask ventilation (deck and Box 18-3)

  • MOANS: M = mask seal; O = obesity/obstruction; A = aged; N = no teeth; S = stiff lungs.
  • Seal problems: facial hair, trauma, secretions, vomit, poor fit and toothless collapsed cheeks impede bag-mask ventilation.
  • Different tools: MOANS predicts mask ventilation difficulty; Mallampati and LEMON predict difficult intubation. Difficult intubation does not necessarily mean difficult bagging.
  • Failure signs: inadequate chest movement, breath sounds or exhaled CO₂; gastric inflation, cyanosis or inadequate SpO₂; worsening hypoxemia causes bradycardia and hypotension.
  • Hess definition: unable to maintain SpO₂ > 90% with 100% O₂ and positive-pressure ventilation; resistance or excessive leak may be responsible.
  • Fixes: keep dentures in, use a properly sized OPA, and switch to two-person ventilation; multiple predictors may require an advanced airway.

5Chain of survival

  • IHCA chain: surveillance/prevention → recognition/emergency activation → immediate high-quality CPR → rapid defibrillation → ALS and post-arrest care.
  • OHCA providers: lay rescuers → EMS → ED → cath lab → ICU; IHCA relies on the code team, cath lab and ICU.
  • High-quality CPR: 100 to 120/min, adult depth at least 2 inches, complete recoil, minimal interruption and no excessive ventilation.
  • Pediatric depth: at least one-third of the AP diameter; about 1½ in for infants and 2 in for children.
  • Prevention: rapid response teams bring critical-care expertise to deteriorating patients; every chain link depends on the preceding links.
SourceHess Ch 18

6C-A-B (CABD) sequence and how it evolved

  • CABD: circulation → airway → breathing → defibrillation; changed from ABCD in 2010 to deliver compressions earlier.
  • BLS start: assess responsiveness and breathing/gasping, activate emergency response, obtain a defibrillator and check pulse for no more than 10 s.
  • If pulseless: give 30 compressions, ≥ 2 in deep, 100–120/min; open airway, give two slow breaths and resume compressions immediately.
  • AED: attach when available, shock if indicated, then resume compressions; untrained rescuers provide hands-only CPR.
  • ACLS: continue compressions, oxygen, vascular access, monitoring and cause treatment; adequate BVM should not be interrupted for an advanced airway.
  • Tube confirmation: physical examination plus qualitative/quantitative ETCO₂; secure the tube and monitor oxygenation and ventilation.
SourceHess Ch 18 · RCP 103 Clinical Data S10

7High-quality chest compressions: rate, depth, hand position and recoil by age

  • Rate: 100–120/min for adults, children and infants; allow complete recoil with equal compression and relaxation time.
  • Depth: adult at least 2 inches (5 cm); child one-third AP ≈ 2 inches (5 cm); infant one-third AP ≈ 1½ inches (4 cm).
  • Hands: adults lower half of sternum with stacked hands and straight arms; children mid-sternum; infants two fingertips or encircling two thumbs.
  • Interruptions: keep < 10 seconds; rotate compressors every 2 minutes or sooner with fatigue. Use a firm surface without delaying compressions.
  • Injury: xiphoid compression can lacerate liver; off-midline hands fracture ribs and may injure lungs. If cracking occurs, check position and continue.
  • Output: compressions generate only 25% to 33% of normal cardiac output; keep hands/fingertips on the chest while releasing pressure.
Watch outOlder Hess passages say “at least 100/min”; use 100–120/min.
SourceHess Ch 18

8Compression-to-ventilation ratios (1 and 2 rescuers) and rescue-breathing rates, by age

  • Adults: 30:2 with one or two rescuers; with an advanced airway, continuous compressions plus 1 breath every 6 s (10 breaths/min).
  • Children/infants: 30:2 with one rescuer; 15:2 with two rescuers. Heuer gives advanced-airway ventilation at 12–20 breaths/min.
  • Pulse without breathing: adult 1 breath every 5–6 s (≈ 10–12 breaths/min); recheck pulse about every 2 min.
  • Pediatric rescue breathing: 1 breath every 3 s; add compressions if pulse remains < 60/min with poor perfusion.
  • Teamwork: one rescuer compresses while the other ventilates and assesses; five cycles of 30:2 take about 2 minutes.
Watch outAdult advanced-airway wording differs: Heuer deck 1 every 6–8 s versus Hess 1 every 6 s; accept 6 s.

9Ventilation during CPR (without and with an advanced airway)

  • Breaths: give 2 breaths of 1 second each, enough for visible chest rise; VT 6 to 7 mL/kg (≈ 500 to 600 mL).
  • Advanced airway: 1 breath every 6 seconds (10/min), without pausing compressions; avoid excessive rate, force and gastric inflation.
  • Air trapping: hypotension during bagging → stop ventilation for 20 to 30 seconds; if BP recovers, resume at 6 to 8 breaths/min.
  • Oxygen: use the highest available concentration during arrest; BVMs deliver 100% O₂, then titrate to the SpO₂ target after ROSC.
  • No chest rise: reposition the airway and try again; an improperly opened or occluded airway most commonly causes ventilation difficulty.
  • Aspiration hazard: forceful breaths inflate the stomach, causing vomiting, aspiration and pneumonia; routine cricoid pressure is no longer recommended.
Watch outHess also says “two slow breaths” and “less than 1 second”; exam answer is 1 second per breath with visible chest rise.

10Bag-valve-mask (manual resuscitator) technique

  • Flow: set O₂ to the manufacturer’s allowed maximum for the exam; never delay ventilation for oxygen. Hess specifies 10 to 12 L/min.
  • Two-person BVM: one rescuer opens the airway and seals the mask with both hands; the other squeezes while both watch chest rise.
  • EC grip: thumb/index form C on the mask; remaining fingers form E along the mandible. Lift the jaw into the mask.
  • Reservoir: at least equal to bag stroke volume; adequate input flow, attached reservoir and slow refill maximize FiO₂.
  • Pre-use checks: occlude and squeeze to test pressure/pop-off; squeeze then occlude to verify inlet reinflation and reservoir deflation. Replace failed bags.
  • Infants: choose appropriate mask/bag size; use pressure monitoring. A Neopuff needs a gas source and seal, plus a backup self-inflating bag.
  • OPA: may improve BMV; avoid in semi-comatose or alert patients. Self-inflating BVM oxygen delivery increases WOB in spontaneously breathing patients.
Watch outFlow wording differs: the instructor’s deck uses manufacturer maximum; Hess gives 10–12 L/min when a number is requested.

11AED and defibrillation: shockable rhythms, energy, pads, and when to shock

  • Rhythms: shock VF and pulseless VT; do not shock asystole or PEA. Untreated VF deteriorates to asystole.
  • Energy: biphasic manufacturer recommendation, e.g. initial 120–200 J; unknown setting → maximum available. Monophasic adult dose = 360 J.
  • Child dose: Heuer gives biphasic 2 J/kg first, then at least 4 J/kg; higher subsequent doses may be considered.
  • P-A-I-D: power on → attach pads → interpret with nobody touching → defibrillate after clearing; immediately resume CPR for about 2 minutes.
  • Pads: upper-right chest below clavicle and left chest below armpit; dry wet skin. Child pads for < 8 years; adult pads if unavailable.
  • Pediatric timing: unwitnessed/out-of-hospital arrest → about 2 minutes of CPR before AED; witnessed/in-hospital arrest → AED as soon as available.
  • After analysis: resume CPR even if no shock is advised because the patient remains pulseless; switch rescuers at each 2-minute analysis.

12Drugs during CPR (epinephrine, amiodarone and the rest) and routes

  • Epinephrine: 1 mg IV/IO push (10 mL of 1:10,000) every 3–5 minutes for arrest; vasoconstriction improves coronary/cerebral perfusion.
  • Amiodarone: refractory VF/pulseless VT → 300 mg IV/IO push, then 150 mg; magnesium sulfate 1–2 g only for torsades or suspected low Mg.
  • Lidocaine: VF/pVT 1 to 1.5 mg/kg; refractory dose 0.5–0.75 mg/kg in 3–5 min, total 3 mg/kg.
  • Bicarbonate: deck dose 0.5–1 mEq/kg, keep pH > 7.15; selected hyperkalemia, overdose, ketoacidosis or prolonged arrest requires adequate ventilation.
  • Atropine: symptomatic bradycardia 0.5 mg every 3–5 min, max 3 mg (0.04 mg/kg); absent from Hess’s cardiac-arrest algorithm.
  • Routes: prefer IV/IO without delaying CPR/shock; IO proximal tibia or humerus, needle not left > 3–4 hours.
  • ET route: NAVEL = naloxone, atropine, vasopressin, epinephrine, lidocaine; 2–2.5 × IV dose in 10 mL NS/sterile water, then 5–10 rapid ventilations.

13Capnography (PETCO₂) during CPR: targets and ROSC

  • PETCO₂: 0 means no pulmonary blood flow; < 10 mm Hg indicates poor/absent flow → improve CPR quality.
  • ROSC: an abrupt sustained rise, typically 40 mm Hg, indicates restored circulation; rising PETCO₂ otherwise reflects improving flow during CPR.
  • Prognosis: not > 10 mm Hg after 20 minutes in an intubated patient may inform a multimodal decision to stop resuscitation.
  • Arterial line: relaxation-phase diastolic pressure < 20 mm Hg also calls for better CPR; pulse/BP and spontaneous arterial waves suggest ROSC.
  • After ROSC: start 10 breaths/min and target PETCO₂ 35–40 mm Hg; waveform capnography confirms/monitors ETT position and CPR quality.
Watch outNot in your sources: a separate numeric PETCO₂ target during compressions beyond the < 10 mm Hg quality threshold and > 10 mm Hg after 20 min.
SourceHess Ch 18, 2 · RCP 203 Lssn 3b S23–24

14Advanced airways during CPR, and the impedance threshold valve

  • Timing: effective BVM preserves uninterrupted compressions and defibrillation; early intubation is usually unnecessary, and first 15-minute adult IHCA intubation correlates with decreased survival.
  • ETT indications: cannot ventilate by BVM or protect the airway, with a trained rescuer available; confirm placement using several techniques including waveform capnography.
  • Failed attempt: stop, ventilate and oxygenate before retrying; interrupt compressions only long enough to visualize the cords.
  • Supraglottic options: laryngeal tubes are easier to place blindly; LMA offers easier insertion and greater airway security than BVM.
  • ITV: limits air entry during recoil, lowers intrathoracic pressure by 6 to 8 mm Hg and increases venous return/cardiac output.
  • ITV contraindications: pulmonary edema or CHF; usable with LMA, cuffed ETT or tight mask.
Watch outHess’s “no substitute for tracheal intubation” Key Point differs from its timing discussion: effective BVM and uninterrupted compressions take priority.
SourceHess Ch 18

15Post–cardiac arrest care

  • Oxygen/ventilation: titrate minimum FiO₂ for SpO₂ 94%; start 10 breaths/min and target PETCO₂ 35–40 mm Hg without hyperventilation.
  • Hypotension: treat SBP < 90 mm Hg with ≈ 1–2 L NS/LR IV/IO and vasopressor infusion; optimize vital-organ perfusion.
  • Infusions: epinephrine 0.1–0.5 mcg/kg/min, dopamine 5–10 mcg/kg/min, or norepinephrine 0.1–0.5 mcg/kg/min.
  • Cardiac cause: obtain 12-lead ECG; STEMI or high suspicion of AMI warrants coronary reperfusion and transfer to a capable unit.
  • Hess TTM: comatose adults after ROSC → 32 °C to 36 °C for at least 24 hours; monitor continuously and match humidifier temperature.
  • H’s: hypovolemia, hypoxia, hydrogen ion/acidosis, hypo-/hyperkalemia and hypothermia are reversible causes.
  • T’s: tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, toxins, pulmonary thrombosis and coronary thrombosis; most post-resuscitation deaths occur in the first 24 hours.

16Choking (foreign body airway obstruction) by age

  • Mild obstruction: forceful cough and good air exchange → encourage coughing, monitor and activate EMS if persistent or worsening.
  • Severe obstruction: cannot speak/cry, weak cough, poor air exchange, inspiratory noise or cyanosis → adult/child abdominal thrusts.
  • Thrust position: thumb side of fist at midline above navel, below breastbone; deliver separate quick upward thrusts. Pregnant/obese victims receive chest thrusts.
  • Infant: never abdominal thrusts; alternate 5 head-down back blows and 5 chest thrusts until relieved or unresponsive.
  • Unresponsive: lower to ground, activate EMS and start compressions without pulse check; inspect before breaths and remove only visible objects.
  • Alone: activate EMS after ≈ 5 cycles / 2 minutes; never use a blind finger sweep.
SourceHess Ch 18

17Definitions, risk factors, newborns and ethics

  • Cardiac arrest: cessation of mechanical cardiac activity with absent circulation; CPR is indicated for cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest or both.
  • Pediatric causes: respiratory disease/arrest commonly precedes arrest; other causes include SIDS, sepsis, neurologic disease and trauma.
  • Newborn assessment: term gestation, breathing/crying and good tone all present → no resuscitation; ≈ 10% need help initiating breathing.
  • Newborn thresholds: PPV for HR < 100, apnea or gasping; compressions for HR < 60 despite ventilation.
  • CHD risk: smoking is the most important modifiable factor; hypertension, inactivity, obesity, stress, diabetes and cholesterol also contribute.
  • Ethics: initiate CPR under implied consent; withholding needs a physician’s order. Valid DNR, irreversible death, rescuer danger or no physiologic benefit preclude CPR.
  • Termination: treating physician considers delays, comorbidities, pre-arrest state and initial rhythm; not starting and stopping resuscitation are ethically equivalent.
SourceHess Ch 18

1How to Tell Any Two Modes Apart (the Framework Behind Every Mode on the List)

  • Mode framework: identify control variable, trigger/cycle and breath sequence; manufacturer names vary, so learn function rather than name.
  • Control: only pressure or volume can be controlled at once; controlling volume lets pressure vary, and controlling pressure lets volume vary.
  • Breaths: patient triggers and cycles → spontaneous; machine triggers or cycles either event → mandatory. Patient triggering alone does not mean spontaneous.
  • Sequences: CMV = all mandatory; IMV = mandatory plus spontaneous; CSV = all spontaneous, such as CPAP or PS.
  • Patterns: VC-CMV, VC-IMV, PC-CMV, PC-IMV and PC-CSV; VC-CSV is invalid because preset VT requires machine cycling.
  • Feedback: open-loop delivers settings unchanged; closed-loop monitors and adjusts, including PSV, MMV, ASV, PAV, PRVC and NAVA.

2VC vs PC: the Difference and When to Use Each

  • VC: guarantees VT with variable pressure; constant flow and VT maintain desired PaCO₂ unless the high-pressure limit interrupts delivery.
  • PC: guarantees pressure while VT varies with effort, compliance and resistance; time-cycled, pressure-limited breaths may be time- or patient-triggered.
  • Waveforms: VC usually has square flow and rising mountain-shaped pressure; PC has descending ramp flow and square table-shaped pressure.
  • ARDS: in VC, falling compliance raises pressure; in PC, falling compliance lowers VT. PC may limit excessive pressure but requires VT monitoring.
  • Calculation: PIP = pressure control + PEEP; PC 20 + PEEP 10 → PIP = 30 cm H₂O.
  • Lung protection: RCP 203 accepts either mode at VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW; Pplat > 30 in VC suggests switching to PC at ≤ 30.
Watch outExam answer: RCP 202 ranks VC 1st choice, PC 2nd choice. RCP 203 and Trauma accept either with lung protection.

3CMV (and How It Differs from Assist/Control)

  • Control-mode CMV: time-triggered preset VT/rate without patient triggering or spontaneous breathing; reserve for deeply sedated, paralyzed or apneic patients.
  • Control indications: tetanus/seizures, complete rest, crushed chest or paradoxical chest movement; awakening may cause severe dyssynchrony.
  • A/C: every patient- or time-triggered breath gets full preset support; set 12 with patient triggering 24 → 24 full breaths.
  • Hess CMV: means A/C; all breaths mandatory, set rate a minimum, with no spontaneous breaths between mandatory breaths.
  • Complications: rapid triggering can cause respiratory alkalosis, auto-PEEP and high mean pressure; complete diaphragm inactivity for 18 to 69 hours causes atrophy.
  • Safety: low exhaled volume and low inspiratory pressure alarms are critical when the patient depends entirely on the ventilator.
Watch outCMV naming conflicts: decide by patient triggering. No patient trigger → Chang/202 control mode; patient triggers with full support → A/C/Hess CMV.
SourceRCP 105 Operating Modes S14–17; Principles S84 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S4 · Hess Ch 19–20

4IMV vs SIMV (and Why SIMV Usually Comes with Pressure Support)

  • IMV: fixed-interval mandatory breaths with spontaneous breaths between; unsynchronized delivery can stack breaths, causing hyperinflation, auto-PEEP and barotrauma.
  • SIMV: synchronizes mandatory delivery to effort within a small window; spontaneous breaths between can have any VT, with PS applied only to those breaths.
  • Timing example: SIMV RR 10 → 60 ÷ 10 = mandatory breath every 6 seconds; effort at second 5.8 delivers it immediately.
  • A/C comparison: set rate 10 and patient triggers 20 → A/C gives 20 mandatory; SIMV gives 10 mandatory + 10 spontaneous.
  • Use: partial support, muscle preservation and weaning; adding PS reduces WOB, fatigue and weaning failure.
  • Exam anchor: SIMV = IMV as the breath sequence, unless the question specifically asks about old unsynchronized IMV and stacking.
SourceRCP 105 Operating Modes S18–21; Principles S85 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S8 · Hess Ch 19–20 · Final Exam Study Guide

5Pressure Support (PS/PSV): the Breath Type Inside SIMV, Volume Support and VV+

  • PSV: patient-triggered, pressure-limited and flow-cycled; only supports spontaneous breaths, with VT determined by effort, compliance and resistance.
  • Cycling: inspiration ends at a preset percentage of peak flow, often around 25%; apnea or inadequate drive makes PSV inappropriate.
  • Deck targets: VT 8–10 mL/kg IBW and spontaneous frequency < 25/min; modern lung-protective notes often use 6–8 mL/kg IBW.
  • Worked example: 5′10″ male → IBW = 50 + 2.3 × 10 = 73 kg; 73 × 8 = 584 mL; 73 × 10 = 730 mL.
  • Titration: low PS causes fatigue/tachypnea; excessive PS activates expiratory muscles. Indications include low spontaneous VT, tachypnea and increased WOB.
SourceRCP 105 Operating Modes S22–25 · Hess Ch 20

6MMV (Mandatory, or Minimum, Minute Ventilation)

  • MMV: a SIMV-related function guaranteeing preset minimum minute ventilation; low spontaneous V̇E automatically increases mandatory volume-cycled breath frequency.
  • Example: target V̇E = 8 L/min; spontaneous 9 L/min needs no added ventilation, while 6 L/min prompts mandatory breaths to fill the deficit.
  • Limitation: adequate total minute ventilation does not guarantee adequate VT for every breath; monitor breathing pattern and reliable effort.
  • Use: weaning, transition from full support and variable drive during neuromuscular disease or recovery from sedation; lowering MMV transfers work to the patient.
  • Comparison: MMV fills the minute-volume gap; ASV chooses rate/VT; PAV amplifies the patient’s effort proportionally.
Watch outHess/Principles frame MMV as IMV with set mandatory rate a maximum; the exam’s 202 deck simply says low V̇E increases mandatory rate.

7PRVC (Pressure-Regulated Volume Control) and Automode

  • PRVC: hybrid pressure-controlled breaths with automatically adjusted pressure to reach target VT at the lowest possible PIP.
  • Settings: set RR, VT, FiO₂ and PEEP; worsening compliance raises inspiratory pressure, while improving compliance lowers it.
  • Mechanism: lower flow reduces resistance/pressure and lengthens inspiratory time so target volume can still be delivered.
  • Aliases: AutoFlow, Adaptive Pressure Ventilation, Volume Control+, Volume Targeted Pressure Control and Pressure Controlled Volume Guarantee.
  • Cautions: worsening mechanics can raise pressure despite the volume target; excessive effort/VT can cause inappropriate withdrawal of support.
  • Automode: no trigger for 12 s adult, 8 s pediatric or 5 s neonatal → PRVC; two consecutive patient-triggered breaths → Volume Support.
Watch outDeck wording also calls PRVC “Volume-Controlled”; exam classification is the 202 slide 4 hybrid: pressure-controlled breaths targeting VT. Hess says VT is not precisely controlled.

8VV+ (Volume Ventilation Plus, Puritan Bennett 840)

  • VV+: PB 840 option combining VC+ mandatory breaths and Volume Support spontaneous breaths; VC+ is Puritan Bennett’s PRVC equivalent.
  • VC+: preset VT and I-time with variable pressure/flow; a test breath estimates compliance, and variable flow meets patient demand.
  • Active exhalation valve: vents excessive pressure from breathing or coughing to improve synchrony compared with standard VC.
  • Volume Support: set target VT only; patient effort determines frequency/minute ventilation and respiratory demand determines I-time.
  • Response: less effort → higher PS; more effort → lower PS. Common use is post-anesthesia recovery/weaning.
  • PSV versus VS: PSV guarantees pressure with variable volume; VS targets volume with variable pressure. Both are patient-triggered, pressure-limited and flow-cycled.

9PAV (Proportional Assist Ventilation)

  • PAV: no target flow, volume or pressure; pressure assistance is proportional to effort, elastance, resistance and ventilatory demand.
  • Assist components: flow assist offsets airway resistance; volume assist offsets elastic restriction. More effort produces more assistance; no effort produces none.
  • Deck setting: % assist, e.g. 50%, 70%; notes say overcome 80% of elastance and airflow resistance.
  • Hess range: support 5% to 95%, typically targeting patient WOB 0.5 to 1.0 J/L; pressure recalculated every 5 ms.
  • Requirements: intact drive, functional neuromuscular system and accurate mechanics; leaks reduce accuracy, so use alarms and backup modes.
  • Risks: excessive assist causes runaway volume/pressure; insufficient assist increases WOB. Not useful with weak drive or weak muscles.
Watch outFor a single exam assist figure use the deck’s 80%; Hess gives the adjustable 5% to 95% range.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 4 S15 · Hess Ch 19–20

10APRV (Airway Pressure Release Ventilation)

  • APRV: inverse-ratio, pressure-controlled IMV allowing unrestricted spontaneous breathing at both levels; set FiO₂, Phigh, Plow, Thigh and Tlow.
  • Timing: no VT or RR setting; Thigh/Tlow determine release frequency. Exam deck: less than 1.5 s at Plow.
  • Adult settings: Plow 0–8 cm H₂O; Phigh for 4–8 mL/kg PBW, below 30–35 cm H₂O; Thigh 3–5 s; Tlow 0.2–0.8 s.
  • Release: pressure drop permits exhalation/CO₂ removal; short Tlow creates intrinsic PEEP. Hess ends Tlow at 50% to 75% of peak expiratory flow.
  • Use: selected hypoxemic failure/ARDS, usually with intact drive; recruitment may improve oxygenation and reduce sedation, but routine first-line use lacks support.
  • Weaning: reduce release frequency; at zero the patient is essentially on CPAP. Large pressure gradients or spontaneous effort may create excessive VT/transpulmonary pressure.
Watch out202 deck permits spontaneous breathing without requiring it; RCP 203 selects patients able to trigger enough breaths for adequate minute ventilation.
Watch outExam Tlow < 1.5 s contains Hess/RCP 203 adult 0.2–0.8 s; Hess spends 80% to 95% of the cycle at Phigh.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 4 S22–23; Lssn 7b S22 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S26 · RCP 105 Weaning S24 · Hess Ch 20, 38

11HFOV (High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation)

  • HFOV: tiny VT near/below anatomic dead space with active inspiration and expiration; deck frequency 3–15 Hz = 180–900 breaths/min.
  • Adjustments: CO₂ removal improves with ↑ amplitude or ↓ frequency; oxygenation improves with ↑ mean airway pressure or ↑ FiO₂.
  • Use: refractory hypoxemic failure after conventional methods; routine adult ARDS use is not recommended because benefit is unproven and harm possible.
  • Wean oxygen: reduce FiO₂ to 40%, then mPaw by 2–3 cm H₂O every 4–6 h to 22–24 cm H₂O.
  • Transition: PCV f 20–25, I:E 1:1, PEEP 12; titrate VT 6–8 mL/kg with Pplat < 35 and mPaw < 20 cm H₂O.
  • Relative contraindications: hypotension/unstable cardiovascular status, air trapping/dynamic hyperinflation and neonatal intracranial hemorrhage.

12All the Listed Modes Side by Side

  • VC versus PC: VC guarantees VT with variable pressure/square flow; PC guarantees pressure with variable VT/decelerating flow and time cycling.
  • CMV/A/C: Chang control mode forbids patient triggering; Hess A/C gives full support to patient/time triggers with set rate as minimum.
  • SIMV/MMV: SIMV mixes synchronized mandatory and spontaneous breaths; MMV adds mandatory breaths when measured minute ventilation falls below its minimum.
  • PRVC/VV+: PRVC adjusts pressure for target VT; VV+ combines mandatory VC+ with spontaneous Volume Support.
  • PAV: flow-cycled spontaneous assistance proportional to effort; no target flow/volume/pressure and no assistance for apnea.
  • APRV: timed cycling between Phigh/Plow with spontaneous breathing at both levels; no preset VT or RR.
  • HFOV: amplitude, Hz, mPaw and FiO₂ control tiny-volume oscillations; reserve for refractory hypoxemia rather than routine adult ARDS.

13What Causes Patient–Ventilator Asynchrony: Definition, Consequences, and Trigger Asynchrony

  • Asynchrony: breath delivery out of phase with patient effort; increases WOB, oxygen cost, discomfort, sedation needs and ventilation duration.
  • Missed triggers: insensitive settings, weak muscles or auto-PEEP prevent triggering; negative pressure dips and slowed expiratory flow reveal ineffective effort.
  • Trigger adjustment: usual pressure trigger 1 to 2 cm H₂O below PEEP/CPAP; nearer zero is more sensitive, e.g. −3 → −1.
  • Auto-PEEP fixes: lower minute ventilation, shorten I:E, bronchodilate and clear secretions; applied PEEP may help obstructive small-airway trapping.
  • Auto-triggering: leaks, condensation, cardiac oscillation or excessive sensitivity trigger unwanted breaths; correct the cause and reduce sensitivity.
  • Reverse triggering: machine inflation triggers neural effort, potentially producing a second breath and excessive combined VT contributing to VILI.
Watch outHess lists trigger, cycle and mode asynchrony; the deck’s three categories are trigger, flow and cycle.
SourceRCP 105 Waveforms S38, 43, 48, 51, 54; Management S33 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S78–79 · Hess Ch 20

14Flow Asynchrony (Flow Starvation) and Cycle Asynchrony (Premature vs Delayed)

  • Flow starvation: inspiratory demand exceeds delivery; downward pressure scalloping, tachypnea, retractions and chest–abdominal paradox suggest inadequate flow.
  • Flow fixes: increase VC flow/change pattern or switch to PC/PS; adjust pressure/rise time and treat pain/anxiety driving excess demand.
  • Dip timing: early inspiratory dip means more flow needed; late dip suggests inspiration ended before effort and more volume/longer delivery is needed.
  • Premature cycling: short I-time allows continued effort and double triggering; stacked breaths may deliver up to twice set VT.
  • Delayed cycling: long I-time causes active exhalation, abdominal activation and end-inspiratory pressure spike; obstruction or leaks can prevent PS cycling.
  • Delayed-cycle fixes: lower PS, raise termination-flow threshold, switch to time-cycled PC or set maximum inspiratory time; patient-triggered I-time ≤ 1.5 s.
  • Air trapping: expiratory flow fails to return to baseline before the next breath, causing auto-PEEP, stacking and increased WOB.
SourceRCP 105 Waveforms S33, 39–40, 42, 44, 49–50 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S27, 80 · Hess Ch 20

15Mode Asynchrony and the Fix Sequence (Settings → Mode → Sedation → Paralysis)

  • Mode asynchrony: SIMV’s varying breath types challenge adaptation; PRVC may reduce support when pain/acidosis drives VT above target.
  • Other causes: awakened patient in control mode, inverse-ratio ventilation, old IMV stacking, low SIMV rate, or inappropriate PS.
  • First fix: identify waveform/cause, check tube kinks, secretions and circuit, then adjust VT, rate, flow, PEEP, sensitivity, rise time and cycling.
  • Next steps: reconsider mode, then use analgesia/sedation for persistent anxiety or discomfort; PCV, APRV or VC+ may improve synchrony.
  • Paralysis last: NMBAs hide unresolved problems; always provide ventilation and sedation with paralysis.
  • ARDS: marked asynchrony may justify VT 8 mL/kg provided Pplat ≤ 30 cm H₂O; spontaneous effort can worsen asynchrony.
SourceRCP 105 Waveforms S38; Operating Modes S14, 18, 28 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S24 · RCP 104 Pharm Ch 18 S28, 34 · Hess Ch 20

16ASV, ATC, and IRV: the Other "Advanced Modes" Slides

  • ASV: closed-loop minute-ventilation target; enter IBW and desired ventilation, then the ventilator chooses rate, I-time, I:E and pressure using mechanics/Otis equation.
  • ASV values: target 0.1 L/min/kg, adjustable 25% to 350%, estimated dead space 2.2 mL/kg; VT may exceed 10 mL/kg IBW in near-normal lungs.
  • ATC: dynamically offsets artificial-airway resistance during inspiration and expiration; smaller ETT or higher flow requires greater pressure compensation.
  • IRV: normal I:E 1:1.5 to 1:3 (sometimes 1:4); inverse ratio uses 2:1 to 4:1.
  • IRV benefit: longer inspiration increases mean airway pressure and recruitment, improving oxygenation; APRV permits spontaneous breathing with inverse timing.
  • IRV hazards: reduced venous return, auto-PEEP, overdistension/barotrauma, pulmonary edema and dyssynchrony; often needs more sedation, sometimes paralysis.

17Ventilator Variables: Pressure, Volume, Flow and Time

  • Variables: volume is delivered amount in mL/L; pressure is delivery force in cm H₂O; flow is delivery rate in L/min.
  • Time: TI includes flow time plus hold; TE spans expiration. Total cycle time = TI + TE = 60/f; I:E = TI:TE.
  • Worked flow: VT = flow × TI; 60 L/min = 1 L/s, delivered for 1 s → 1 L tidal volume.
  • Control: VC presets volume/flow; PC presets pressure over time; time control leaves flow, pressure and volume dependent on mechanics.
  • Phase variables: trigger starts inspiration, limit holds a value, cycle ends inspiration; baseline pressure is PEEP, and flow baseline is zero.
  • Pressure example: PEEP 5 and PIP 25 → 25 − 5 = 20 cm H₂O above PEEP; true lung driving pressure uses Pplat − PEEP.
  • Whitaker: VC breaths are flow limited/time cycled; PC breaths are pressure limited and flow or time cycled.
SourceRCP 105 Principles S2–3, 62, 75, 78; Operating Modes S5, 23, 30; Waveforms S6 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S21 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 S27 · Hess Ch 19 · Whitaker

18Independent vs Dependent Variables

  • Independent variable: what the ventilator sets/guarantees; the dependent variable changes with patient mechanics. True VC presets both flow and volume.
  • VC example: VT 500 mL at PIP 20; ARDS develops → VT remains 500 mL while PIP rises to 35 cm H₂O.
  • PC example: 20 cm H₂O with VT 500 mL; ARDS develops → pressure remains 20 while VT falls to 300 mL.
  • Falling compliance: VC raises PIP and Pplat without changing their difference; PC lowers VT.
  • Rising resistance: VC raises PIP and PIP − Pplat without changing Pplat; PC lowers VT only while flow continues through end-inspiration.
  • PC volume: higher driving pressure, effort or longer I-time while flow persists raises VT; higher auto-PEEP lowers it.
SourceRCP 105 Principles S33, 75, 79; Operating Modes S4 · RCP 203 Lssn 3a S52–55 · Hess Ch 19–20

19Trigger: What Starts the Breath

  • Trigger: starts inspiration using time, pressure, flow, volume or diaphragm electrical activity; sensitivity specifies the signal change needed.
  • Time triggering: clinician-set rate controls timing; rate 12 → breath every 5 seconds, with no preceding negative pressure deflection.
  • Pressure triggering: effort lowers airway pressure; typically set 1 to 2 cm H₂O below PEEP/CPAP to minimize work without auto-triggering.
  • Flow example: base flow 10 L/min with sensitivity 3 L/min triggers when expiratory flow falls to 7 L/min.
  • Sensitivity: −5 cm H₂O may miss efforts; −0.5 cm H₂O or 0.5 L/min may auto-trigger unwanted breaths.
  • Auto-PEEP: adds a threshold load that may defeat pressure and flow triggering; patient effort must overcome it before triggering.
  • Windows: CMV uses trigger windows; IMV uses short synchronization windows, where an effort starts a breath still counted as machine-triggered.
SourceRCP 105 Operating Modes S5, 14, 16; Management S30, 33; Waveforms S11–12 · Hess Ch 19–20

20Cycle: What Ends the Breath (and How Trigger–Limit–Cycle Labels Each Mode)

  • Cycle: ends inspiration; volume cycling ends at preset VT, time cycling at preset I-time, and flow cycling at the termination-flow threshold.
  • Exam anchor: VC is volume-cycled; PC is time-cycled; PSV is flow-cycled, commonly around 25% of peak flow.
  • PSV label: patient-triggered, pressure-limited, flow-cycled; Volume Support uses the same trigger/limit/cycle pattern.
  • PCV label: time- or patient-triggered, pressure-limited, time-cycled; VC A/C is patient- or time-triggered and volume-cycled.
  • Pressure cycling: reaching high-pressure alarm ends a breath, such as coughing during VC; this alarm event does not change CMV into IMV.
  • Breath classification: patient both triggers and cycles → spontaneous; machine controls either event → mandatory. Flow cycling is patient cycling.
SourceRCP 105 Operating Modes S5, 14, 16, 22–23, 30; Initiation S11; Waveforms S31; Principles S80 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S9, 17, 19 · Hess Ch 19

21PEEP: What It Is, What It Does, and How Much to Use

  • PEEP: raises end-expiratory baseline pressure to prevent collapse, increase FRC, recruit alveoli, reduce shunt and improve oxygenation.
  • Initial setting: 5 cm H₂O for refractory hypoxemia; deck NBRC range 2–6. CPAP applies PEEP during entirely spontaneous breathing.
  • Titrate: use PaO₂, saturation, compliance, waveforms and cardiovascular tolerance; optimal PEEP gives greatest benefit with fewest adverse effects.
  • Worked example: 10 cm H₂O was optimal because increasing to 12 cm H₂O lowered both PaO₂ and compliance.
  • Pressure–volume loop: set PEEP just above the lower inflection point; RCP 203 specifies 2–3 cm H₂O above it.
  • Weaning: lower FiO₂ to ≤ 50% before reducing PEEP; RCP 203 optimal PEEP maintains SpO₂ ≥ 88–90% without lowering BP.
  • ARDS: higher PEEP may benefit P/F ≤ 200 but harm P/F > 200; Hess tables target PaO₂ 55–80 mm Hg or SpO₂ 88%–95%.

22PEEP Complications and the "Dangerous Level" (> 10 cm H₂O)

  • Exam danger thresholds: PEEP > 10 cmH₂O, P̄aw > 30 cmH₂O or PIP > 50 cmH₂O increase alveolar rupture/barotrauma risk.
  • Major downside: increased intrathoracic pressure decreases venous return, preload, cardiac output and BP; high CVP can coexist with reduced preload.
  • Other harms: barotrauma/pneumothorax, impaired cerebral venous drainage with increased ICP, reduced hepatic perfusion and dead space from compressed pulmonary capillaries.
  • Pressure pairing: PEEP > 15 cm H₂O plus intra-abdominal pressure > 20 cm H₂O can harm cardiovascular, renal and pulmonary function.
  • ARDS: individualize PEEP; avoid levels causing Pplat > 30 cm H₂O (except with abnormal chest wall mechanics) or driving pressure greater than 15 cm H₂O.
  • Recruitment response: improved compliance can lower lung stress; increased end-inspiratory transpulmonary pressure raises stress, so monitor hemodynamics.

23Auto-PEEP (Intrinsic, Inadvertent, Occult PEEP)

  • Auto-PEEP: incomplete exhalation traps gas and raises end-expiratory lung volume; total PEEP = set PEEP + auto-PEEP.
  • Causes: obstruction/high compliance, high minute ventilation, high rate, low inspiratory flow, excessive PS, long I-time or inverse I:E shorten effective emptying.
  • Detection: expiratory flow fails to return to zero before the next breath; missed triggers, hyperinflation and hypotension support the diagnosis.
  • Measurement: relaxed patient, end-expiratory pause pressure minus set PEEP; worked example 12 − 5 = 7 cm H₂O of auto-PEEP.
  • Fix first: decrease rate/VT, increase VC inspiratory flow or decrease PC TI, and treat obstruction with bronchodilators/clearance.
  • Applied PEEP: after settings changes, RCP 203 permits up to 80–90% of measured auto-PEEP only in spontaneously breathing patients; monitor pressures/total PEEP.
  • Consequences: greater trigger work, lower venous return/CO, hypotension and barotrauma; auto-PEEP raises VC PIP and lowers PC VT.
Watch outHess uses 75% to 85% rather than the deck’s 80–90% applied PEEP; avoid increasing total PEEP.
Watch outUse long expiratory time constant for air trapping; the RCP 203 “short” wording contradicts its own slow-emptying explanation.

24High Pressures and Their Causes (PIP vs Plateau)

  • Targets: Pplat < 30 cm H₂O; driving pressure = Pplat − PEEP, target < 15 cm H₂O. PIP includes resistive plus elastic pressure.
  • Pressure pattern: rising PIP alone suggests airway resistance or increased flow; rising PIP and Pplat together suggests reduced compliance.
  • Causes: resistance rises with secretions, kink/biting, bronchospasm or circuit obstruction; compliance falls with ARDS, edema, atelectasis, pneumothorax or abdominal distention.
  • Plateau: measure during relaxed end-inspiratory pause 0.5 to 1.5 s; zero flow removes airway resistance from the pressure measurement.
  • Alarm: set high pressure 10 to 15 cm H₂O above observed PIP; RCP 203 initially uses 35 cm H₂O, then 10 above PIP.
  • Worked resistance: Raw = (PIP − Pplat) ÷ V̇; (43 − 40) ÷ 0.33 = 9 cm H₂O/L/s.
  • Worked compliance: Cstat = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP); 320 mL ÷ (40 − 26) = 23 mL/cm H₂O.

25Low Pressures and Their Causes (Leaks, Disconnection, Loss of PEEP)

  • Low-pressure alarm: 10 to 15 cm H₂O below observed PIP; low exhaled-volume alarm 100 mL below expired VT detects leaks/disconnection.
  • Causes: circuit disconnection/leak, cuff leak or ventilator malfunction; low PEEP usually reflects circuit or cuff leakage.
  • Waveform leak: expired VT remains below set VT every breath, with consistently lower PIP; pressure may still return normally to baseline.
  • Air-trapping contrast: expired VT is low for 2 to 3 breaths, then exceeds set VT as trapped gas escapes.
  • Other settings: apnea delay 15- to 20-sec; high frequency 10/min over observed total frequency; FiO₂ alarms 5% to 10% over/below analyzed FiO₂.
  • Response: assess patient → ventilator → cause → correction; never silence an alarm and walk away. Improving mechanics can also lower VC PIP.

26Ventilator Circuits: Single-Limb vs Double-Limb (Dual-Limb "Wye")

  • Single-limb with valve: home/transport circuits use a patient-end pneumatic exhalation valve; high control pressure closes it for inspiration, low pressure permits exhalation.
  • Dual-limb: ICU circuit has inspiratory/expiratory limbs, 15-mm patient connector and ventilator-mounted expiratory valve; added devices beyond the wye increase dead space.
  • Single-limb leak port: NIV uses a calibrated leak instead of exhalation valve; pressure/flow regulation creates PEEP and estimates patient volume after leak subtraction.
  • Compressible volume: circuit expansion reduces delivered VT; modern ventilators often compensate. Neonatal low-compliance tubing minimizes loss.
  • Worked loss: circuit factor 150 mL ÷ 50 cm H₂O = 3 mL/cm H₂O; PIP 60 and PEEP 10 → 3 × 50 = 150 mL lost.
  • Condensation: cooling causes the most common circuit-patency threat; use heated wire, water traps or temporary HME; temperature probe near wye targets 37°C.
  • Troubleshooting: suspected major failure → disconnect and manually ventilate with O₂ plus PEEP valve; follow manufacturer circuit configuration and change visibly soiled circuits.
SourceRCP 105 Principles S63; Management S36–40; Initiation S14 · RCP 203 Lssn 4 S66–68, 70–72 · Hess Ch 19–20

27Exhalation (Expiratory) Valves: What They Do and What Goes Wrong

  • Valve job: expiratory valve closes during inspiration and opens for exhalation; it also controls PEEP through circuit-pressure regulation.
  • Single-limb: a separate pneumatic line pressurizes the patient-end valve to block inspiratory outflow; residual valve pressure often sets PEEP.
  • Active valve: permits spontaneous breathing during mandatory pressure breaths/APRV; VC+ vents excess cough pressure to maintain synchrony.
  • Expiratory obstruction: causes low-volume plus high-pressure alarms and risks rebreathing, asphyxia or barotrauma; blocked single-limb NIV ports are especially dangerous.
  • Ribavirin: crystals can obstruct valves, sensors and ETT; check valve function, rising peak pressure, falling ventilation and visible buildup.
  • Safety: emergency expiratory valve supplies another exhalation channel if the main valve blocks; heat expiratory HEPA filters to prevent condensation blockage.

28The Equation of Motion

  • Equation: Pvent + Pmus = E·V + R·V̇; elastic pressure is E·V, resistive pressure is R·V̇, and elastance is inverse compliance.
  • Bedside form: Paw + Pmus = VT/C + R × V̇ + PEEP + PEEPi (+ inertance); inertance usually has little effect.
  • Support: spontaneous breathing Paw = 0; full ventilatory support Pmus = 0; partial support combines muscle and ventilator pressure.
  • Mechanics: asthma increases resistive load; ARDS increases elastic load. VC fixes volume/flow so pressure varies; PC fixes pressure so volume/flow vary.
  • Worked plateau: Pplat = VT/C + PEEP; 320 ÷ 23 ≈ 14, then 14 + 26 ≈ 40 cm H₂O.
  • Worked peak: R 9 cm H₂O/L/s × flow 0.33 L/s ≈ 3; add resistive pressure to plateau: 40 + 3 = 43 cm H₂O.
  • Auto-PEEP: adds pressure load requiring muscle/ventilator compensation; mechanics fitting assumes Pmus is zero and becomes less valid with active breathing.
SourceRCP 105 Principles S62, 71–74 · Hess Ch 19–20, 51, Glossary

29Ventilation Around Surgery — Intraoperative Settings and Postoperative Failure

  • Intraoperative protection: VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW with low PEEP, plateau pressure < 30 cm H₂O and the lowest FiO₂ maintaining adequate oxygenation.
  • Mechanisms: lower volume limits volutrauma; PEEP limits collapse/atelectrauma. Protective settings are associated with fewer postoperative respiratory-failure and pneumonia complications.
  • Postoperative ventilation: residual anesthesia, inadequate ventilation/oxygenation or surgical complications may require support; atelectasis, diaphragmatic dysfunction and V̇/Q̇ mismatch impair function.
  • Risk: PPCs occur in approximately 7% with normal preoperative lungs; risk-factor groups can reach 70%, with upper-abdominal/thoracic surgery rates 20% to 70%.
  • Prevention: prolonged invasive ventilation increases VAP risk; timely extubation, preventive care and selected NIV may help.
  • Revised teaching: evidence does not support routine incentive spirometry alone to prevent PPCs; selected lung-expansion/clearance therapies remain listed for atelectasis.

2How HFNC works, who it is for, and when it is failing (ROX index)

  • How it works: precise FiO₂, upper-airway dead-space washout, less upper-airway resistance and modest positive pressure reduce work of breathing.
  • Pressure: with the mouth closed, pharyngeal pressure rises about 1 cm H₂O for every 10 L/min; opening the mouth can lose that effect.
  • Main indication: acute hypoxemic respiratory failure; HFNC decreases intubation compared with conventional oxygen. Evidence for COPD, asthma or cardiogenic edema is insufficient.
  • ROX: (SpO₂/FiO₂) ÷ respiratory rate; ≥ 4.88 predicts success, < 3.85 → consider intubation, 3.85–4.77 → monitor closely.

3Patient–ventilator asynchrony on NIPPV: causes and fixes

  • Main cause: unintentional leak can cause missed triggers, auto-triggering and delayed cycling; fit the interface and minimize leak first.
  • Trigger: adjust sensitivity to balance effort against auto-triggering; increase EPAP to counter auto-PEEP and treat the underlying disease.
  • Cycle: reduce leak, adjust the flow-cycle setting or pressure support, or use time-cycled pressure control instead of flow-cycled pressure support.
  • Flow: adjust inspiratory pressure, flow or VT and rise time for comfort; neuromuscular patients often tolerate a slower rise.
  • Apnea: use a backup rate in S/T mode for apnea or periodic breathing, especially at night.
  • Deck leak rules: Total Leak normally 7–25; never exceed 60. “---” or “0” means the mask is too tight.

4NIPPV guideline: who should and should not get it

  • Use NIV: COPD exacerbation, acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, postoperative failure, chest trauma with acute respiratory failure, and prevention after extubation in high-risk patients.
  • Selection: spontaneously breathing, cooperative, able to protect the airway; distress, RR > 25/min or pH < 7.35 with PaCO₂ > 45 mm Hg support NIV.
  • Exclude: arrest, unstable hemodynamics, high aspiration risk, copious secretions, inability to protect the airway, or inability to fit a mask.
  • Other contraindications: pH < 7.20, upper-airway obstruction, uncontrolled arrhythmias, active upper GI bleeding, nausea and vomiting in the RCP 203 deck.
  • Guideline says no: COPD hypercapnia without acidosis, prevention after extubation in non-high-risk patients, or established post-extubation respiratory failure.
  • No guideline recommendation: asthma-related acute respiratory failure or de novo acute respiratory failure such as ARDS.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 1b S1, Lssn 7b S27 · RCP 105 NIV S4 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 Ch 11 S49, S51 · Hess Ch 21

5Goals of NIPPV, how success is judged, and when to quit

  • Main goal: reverse acute respiratory failure without an artificial airway; improve oxygenation, ventilation, distress, fatigue, compliance and atelectasis while treating the cause.
  • Start: prioritize comfort and respiratory-muscle unloading; begin low, hold the mask before securing it, and avoid an overly tight fit.
  • Adjust: IPAP and ΔP control ventilation; FiO₂ and EPAP control oxygenation. Titrate FiO₂ to SpO₂ > 90%.
  • Success: PaO₂ rises and PaCO₂ falls; a faster PaCO₂ fall supports success. VT > 10 mL/kg predicts NIV failure.
  • Failure: continued deterioration 30 minutes after starting NIV → intubate. A new contraindication or worsening ventilation/oxygenation also warrants considering intubation.
  • Weaning readiness: RR < 25, HR < 110, IPAP < 15, EPAP < 10, compensated pH > 7.35, SpO₂ > 90% on < 50%.
  • Wean: underlying condition mostly resolved; extend daytime breaks first, then provide at least one more night of support.
Watch outExam: deterioration after 30 minutes → intubate (NIV deck). Hess says consider intubation if no improvement within 1 to 2 hours.
SourceRCP 105 NIV S3–S4, S6, S11–S12 · RCP 203 Lssn 7 Ch 11 S52, S95 · Hess Ch 21

6Cardiogenic pulmonary edema: what it is and how it presents

  • Cause: most commonly left-sided heart failure; elevated pulmonary hydrostatic pressure pushes low-protein transudate into the interstitium and alveoli.
  • Starling forces: hydrostatic pressure 10–15 mm Hg pushes out; oncotic pressure 25–30 mm Hg holds in. Hydrostatic pressure exceeding 25–30 mm Hg causes edema.
  • Signs: pink, frothy sputum, crackles, tachypnea, orthopnea, PND and Cheyne-Stokes respirations; early hypoxemic respiratory alkalosis can progress to respiratory acidosis.
  • LVEF: normal 55–70%; < 40% means heart failure; < 35% is severe with increased arrhythmia risk.
  • CXR: cardiomegaly (> 50% of thoracic width), Kerley A & B lines, central bat’s-wing opacities and transudative pleural effusion.
  • Management order: decrease preload → decrease afterload → increase contractility; add O₂, ventilatory support if needed and arrhythmia control.
SourceRCP 103 Pulmonary Edema S6–S8, S14–S16, S18, S20, S22, S24, S27, S30–S33 · Hess Ch 40

7CPAP/NIV for cardiogenic pulmonary edema: why it works and what level

  • Best-supported level: CPAP 5 to 10 cm H₂O for acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema; mask CPAP or NIV decreases intubation and improves survival.
  • Why: positive pressure decreases preload and afterload, improves compliance, decreases intrapulmonary shunt and raises PaO₂.
  • Single starting value: 10 cm H₂O with 100% O₂; titrate to SpO₂ > 90% and avoid > 20 cm H₂O.
  • Selection: awake, cooperative, upper-airway control; the deck excludes SBP < 90 mm Hg, MI, GCS ≤ 8 or need for intubation.
  • Switch: hypopnea or hypercapnia → BiPAP with a backup rate; hypoxemia with normal PaCO₂ → increase EPAP.
Watch outExam names Hess Ch 21: use CPAP 5–10 cm H₂O. RCP 105’s first-line FAQ answer is CPAP 8–12 cmH₂O with 100% O₂.
SourceHess Ch 21, 40 · RCP 103 Pulmonary Edema S35 · RCP 105 NIV S12, Operating Modes S11, S13 · RCP 203 Lssn 4 Ch 6 S43, Lssn 7 Ch 11 S16

8Hemodynamics: Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) range, formula, and why NIV can drop it

  • MAP: pressure driving tissue perfusion over the cardiac cycle; normal 65–105 mm Hg in Hess, not the arithmetic mean of systolic and diastolic pressure.
  • Formula: MAP = Diastolic + ⅓(Systolic − Diastolic), or [2(DBP) + SBP] ÷ 3.
  • Example: BP 110/70 → MAP = 70 + ⅓(110 − 70) = 70 + 13.3 ≈ 83 mm Hg.
  • Minimums: MAP 60 mm Hg for tissue perfusion; MAP 70 mm Hg for cerebral perfusion in the decks.
  • NIV effect: increased intrathoracic pressure decreases venous return, preload, cardiac output and BP; monitor hemodynamics because instability excludes NIV.
SourceRCP 105 Hemodynamic S8, Monitoring S38, Operating Modes S9, NIV S4 · RCP 203 Lssn 3a Ch 4 S46 · RCP 104 Ch 21 S8 · RCP 202 Lssn 4 S6 · Hess Ch 3, 21, 43

9Leak ports on NIV (vented masks, single-limb circuits, leak monitoring)

  • Leak port: the passive exhalation route in single-limb bilevel circuits; it sits near the patient in the circuit or in the mask.
  • Never obstruct it: blocking the exhalation port can cause CO₂ rebreathing, asphyxia or barotrauma; direct vented air away from the patient.
  • Less rebreathing: put the port and O₂ entry in the mask, increase expiratory pressure, or use a plateau exhalation valve.
  • Pressure floor: many bilevel ventilators use minimum expiratory pressure 3 to 4 cm H₂O; extra flow helps flush the hose.
  • V60 Total Leak: normally 7–25; never exceed 60. “---” or “0” means the mask is too tight.
  • Equipment: conventional ventilator masks use a standard elbow without a leak port; full oronasal masks need a safety inlet valve.
SourceHess Ch 21 · RCP 105 NIV S13 · RCP 203 Lssn 4 Ch 6 S42, S68, S72 · RCP 202 Lssn 1b S3

10Difference between PEEP, PAP, and pharyngeal pressure

  • PEEP: pressure remaining at end-exhalation; keeps alveoli open, raises FRC and improves oxygenation. EPAP = PEEP; CPAP applies it during entirely spontaneous breathing.
  • PAP: positive airway pressure includes CPAP, BiPAP and APAP. IPAP includes EPAP; PS = IPAP − EPAP.
  • Example: IPAP 15 / EPAP 5 → PS 10 and PEEP 5; a critical-care ventilator sets PS 10 above PEEP 5.
  • Pharyngeal pressure: upper-airway pressure; HFNC raises it about 1 cm H₂O per 10 L/min with the mouth closed.
  • Gastric inflation: pressure above the esophageal opening pressure sends gas into the stomach; Hess recommends inspiratory pressure ≤ 20 cm H₂O.
  • Hemodynamic PAP: pulmonary artery pressure, measured with the PA-catheter balloon deflated; deck normal 15–25 / 6–12 mm Hg.
  • PEEP raises pulmonary PAP: expanded alveoli compress pulmonary capillaries, increasing PVR; read the question to distinguish airway from artery pressure.
Watch outExam pulmonary PAP: use the deck’s 15–25 / 6–12 mm Hg; Hess gives PASP 15–30, PADP 4–12, mean PAP 9–16 mm Hg.
Watch outHess limits inspiratory pressure to ≤ 20 cm H₂O; RCP 203 says IPAP < 20–25 cm H₂O. The conflicts table recommends ≤ 20 cmH₂O.

11BiPAP settings, adjustment rules and alarms

  • Pressures: IPAP controls ventilation and VT; EPAP raises FRC and improves oxygenation. PS = IPAP − EPAP and is never < 4.
  • Example: IPAP 15 / EPAP 5 → PS 10. Raise IPAP alone for ventilation; raise IPAP equally whenever raising EPAP to preserve PS.
  • Adjustments: high CO₂ → increase IPAP or PS; low CO₂ → decrease IPAP or PS; oxygenation → adjust FiO₂ or EPAP.
  • Starting range: IPAP 8–12, EPAP 4–6, backup rate 10–12 in the NIV deck; S/T backup sits two to five breaths below spontaneous RR.
  • V60 pressure alarms: HIP 5 to 10 above IPAP; LIP 2 to 3 below IPAP; LIP T resembles apnea time.
  • Other alarms: Lo Rate one above set RR (12 → 13); Lo V̇E normally 4 or 5 L/min; VT and high RR use actual measurements.
  • Rise/Ramp: lower Rise means faster pressurization; Ramp Off or 5–45 minutes may reduce anxiety but can delay therapeutic pressure in acute care.
Watch outExam starting range: NIV deck 8–12 / 4–6; single starting value: Operating Modes 8/4. Its notes give 8–10 / 4–5.

12NIV interfaces: advantages and disadvantages

  • Nasal mask: less aspiration risk, dead space and claustrophobia; easier speech and secretion clearance. Mouth breathing causes leakage; a chin strap may help.
  • Oronasal mask: better seal but increased dead space, aspiration and regurgitation risk; failure of flow can cause asphyxia without a safety valve.
  • Acute failure: oronasal or total face masks are usually better tolerated than nasal interfaces; full-face fits significant mouth breathing.
  • Nasal pillows: smallest and most comfortable, but the deck does not recommend them for BiPAP.
  • Total face/helmet: total face cannot deliver aerosolized medication; helmet risks rebreathing, poorer synchrony and asphyxia.
  • Fit: allow one or two fingers beneath headgear; pressure sores → reduce tension, change mask style or use hydrocolloid dressing.

1Signs and symptoms of pneumonia

  • Pneumonia: inflammation and consolidation of lung tissue, most frequently from infection; alveolar fluid impairs gas exchange and may cause atelectasis.
  • Symptoms: fever, chills, fatigue, productive cough, dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain; sputum may be purulent, green, rust-colored or blood-streaked.
  • Exam: tachypnea, crackles, bronchial breath sounds, whispered pectoriloquy and dull percussion over consolidation; hypoxemia increases work of breathing.
  • Fever: bacterial > 101 °F and viral < 101 °F in the deck; HR, RR and BP may rise.
  • Supporting findings: leukocytosis with increased bands or leukopenia, plus infiltrates on CXR or CT.
SourceRCP 103 Infectious S2–S3, S26 · Hess Ch 36

2How pneumonia is diagnosed

  • Diagnosis: clinical findings plus imaging and microbiology; no single test diagnoses pneumonia. An infiltrate is necessary, and some appear only on CT.
  • CXR: consolidation, air bronchograms, possible abscess or pleural effusion; ABG may progress from hypoxemic respiratory alkalosis to hypoxemic respiratory acidosis.
  • Sputum: Gram stain and culture are the microbiologic cornerstone; positive at ≥ 10⁶ CFU/mL. Recent antibiotics cause false negatives; contamination causes false positives.
  • Urine antigen: useful with inadequate sputum; unaffected by antibiotics. Legionella testing detects only serogroup 1.
  • Blood cultures: draw from two sites before antibiotics in hospitalized patients; not recommended for outpatient CAP.
  • BAL: not standard for CAP; useful in severe, unusual or immunocompromised cases. Positive at ≥ 10⁴ CFU/mL.
  • Procalcitonin: rises with bacterial rather than viral infection; mainly helps decide when to stop antibiotics.
SourceRCP 103 Infectious S27 · Hess Ch 36

3Categories of pneumonia

  • CAP: acquired through normal social contact without nosocomial risk factors; S. pneumoniae is the most common bacterial cause.
  • HAP: ≥ 48 hours after admission, not present or incubating on arrival; Hess specifies patients who are not mechanically ventilated.
  • VAP: pneumonia in intubated, ventilated patients; use ≥ 48 hours after intubation for the exam.
  • HCAP: older category for recent healthcare exposure, nursing-home residence or recent IV treatment; Hess says it was retired in 2016.
  • Patterns: bronchopneumonia is patchy bronchi-plus-alveoli disease; lobar involves lobes; interstitial affects tissue between alveoli.
  • Other groups: typical/atypical, aspiration, chronic and immunocompromised-host pneumonia; TB is the most important chronic pneumonia pathogen.
Watch outExam VAP timing: ≥ 48 hours after intubation. The RCP 105 slide says “within 48 hours,” contradicted by its updated notes.
Watch outFor a categories question, answer CAP / HAP / VAP. The deck defines HCAP, but Hess retires it as a separate entity.
SourceRCP 103 Infectious S6–S10, S14, S16–S18, S25, S39–S40 · RCP 105 Procedures S43 · RCP 203 Lssn 6a Ch 9 S59 · Hess Ch 36

4Atypical pneumonia: how it differs from typical pneumonia

  • Typical: abrupt rigors, high fever, pleuritic pain and rusty or purulent sputum; lobar consolidation with air bronchograms and elevated WBC.
  • Atypical: subacute flu-like illness, dry cough, moderate fever, malaise and extrapulmonary symptoms; diffuse interstitial or peribronchial opacities.
  • Most common atypical: Mycoplasma pneumoniae causes walking pneumonia, especially in children and young adults in crowded settings.
  • Mycoplasma: lacks a cell wall, so penicillin and other β-lactams do not work; cold hemagglutinins may cause hemolytic anemia.
  • Organisms: Mycoplasma, Chlamydophila, Chlamydia psittaci, Coxiella, Legionella and respiratory viruses; atypicals escape standard sputum Gram stain and culture.
  • Treatment: macrolides such as azithromycin are the go-to; doxycycline also covers atypicals and is the drug of choice for C. psittaci.
Watch outAtypical WBC: Hess says usually normal; RCP 103 says moderately elevated. If forced to choose, use Hess, the named source.
SourceRCP 103 Infectious S11–S15 · RCP 104 Ch 14 S26, S29 · Hess Ch 36, 56

5Legionella infections

  • Legionella: an atypical CAP organism, usually L. pneumophila; inhaled mist from contaminated water systems causes Legionnaires disease.
  • Clues: cough, fever and dyspnea with confusion, abdominal pain or diarrhea; of the atypicals, Legionella is most likely to cause ARDS.
  • Testing: needs specialized testing; urinary antigen sensitivity 70%–90%, specificity nearly 99%, and detects only serogroup 1.
  • False negatives: up to 20% of Legionella pneumonias are other serogroups; a negative urine antigen does not cover those infections.
  • First-line: a fluoroquinolone or macrolide; newer macrolides include azithromycin and clarithromycin.
  • Prevention: sterile water in ventilator humidifiers is prudent because hospital tap water can contain Legionella; efficacy has not been validated.
Watch outExam: Legionella is atypical (Hess and RCP 104). RCP 103 incorrectly groups it on the typical CAP slide.

6Chlorhexidine

  • Other bundle components: sucralfate rather than H₂ antagonists and subglottic ETTs that continuously remove secretions in RCP 203.
  • Four prevention points: cuff pressure 20–30 cm H₂O, HOB 30–45°, hand hygiene and extubate as early as safely possible.

7Ventilator-associated pneumonia: incidence, diagnosis, organisms, treatment

  • Incidence: VAP affects 10–25% of ventilated patients; mortality 33–50% in the deck. Microaspiration above the ETT cuff is the primary mechanism.
  • Greatest VAP risk: prolonged mechanical ventilation in the deck; the ETT bypasses defenses and is Hess’s number-one nosocomial pneumonia risk factor.
  • Bedside diagnosis: new/progressive infiltrate plus fever, WBC change and purulent secretions; purulent secretions are the most important bedside finding.
  • Quantitative cultures: endotracheal aspirate ≥ 10⁵–10⁶ CFU/mL; BAL ≥ 10⁴ CFU/mL; protected specimen brush ≥ 10³ CFU/mL.
  • Onset: early within 4 days without MDR risk → community organisms; late > 4–5 days or MDR risk → resistant organisms.
  • Treatment: culture, then immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics; de-escalate with results. MDR coverage includes antipseudomonal agents plus vancomycin or linezolid.
  • Duration: Hess gives 7 days for VAP and ≤ 7 days for HAP; no improvement by day 3 means failed therapy.
Watch outExam incidence/mortality: deck 10–25% / 33–50%; Hess incidence is 15% to 26% and questions older attributable-mortality estimates.

8Aspiration pneumonia vs aspiration pneumonitis

  • Aspiration pneumonia: lower-airway injury may be toxic, obstructive or infectious; aspiration causes nearly all anaerobic lung infections.
  • Pneumonitis: noninfectious chemical injury from gastric contents; it can appear hours later, peaks at 12–26 h and may progress to ARDS.
  • Course: usually resolves in ~72 h if no secondary infection develops in the deck; gastric fluid pH ≤ 2.5 is often fatal.
  • CXR: dependent segments; supine → posterior upper-lobe and superior lower-lobe segments; upright → lower lobes.
  • Risks: reduced consciousness, arrest, anesthesia, neurologic disorders, GERD, vomiting, recent intubation and dysphagia.
  • Infectious treatment: aspiration pneumonia → clindamycin in the deck; Hess also lists β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors, carbapenems or moxifloxacin.
Watch outExam pneumonitis resolution: deck ~72 h without secondary infection; Hess says resolves by 48 hours.
SourceRCP 103 Infectious S19–S22 · RCP 104 Ch 14 S37 · Hess Ch 36

9CAP severity scoring (CURB-65, PSI) and prevention

  • CURB-65: 1 point each for confusion, BUN ≥ 20 mg/dL, RR ≥ 30/min, low BP and age ≥ 65 years.
  • BP criterion: systolic < 90 mm Hg or diastolic ≤ 60 mm Hg; either meets the CURB-65 blood-pressure point.
  • Disposition: score 0–1 → likely outpatient; 2 → likely hospital; ≥ 3 → hospital and possibly ICU.
  • PSI: classes I–II → outpatient; III → observation or admission; IV or higher → hospitalization, often ICU.
  • ICU: 1 major criterion (invasive ventilation or septic shock) or 3 minor criteria by IDSA/ATS.
  • Prevention: pneumococcal vaccination and annual influenza vaccination for everyone older than 6 months.
SourceHess Ch 36 · RCP 103 Infectious S4

10Postoperative Pneumonia — and the Atelectasis It Gets Confused With

  • Postoperative pneumonia: third most common postoperative complication; risk factors include immunosuppression, malnutrition, COPD and age over 65.
  • Clues: fever, chills, purulent sputum, hypoxemia, tachypnea and pleuritic pain; treat with antibiotics guided by suspected pathogens and cultures.
  • Atelectasis: reversible alveolar collapse with reduced lung volume and impaired gas exchange; reported in 90% of anesthetized patients.
  • CXR: increased opacity plus volume-loss signs such as fissure displacement, elevated diaphragm and mediastinal shift; air bronchograms may occur.
  • Fever discriminator: atelectasis alone does not cause postoperative fever; look for infection or another cause.
  • Prevention/treatment: pain control, deep breathing, coughing, mobilization, oxygen, indicated airway clearance and lung expansion with incentive spirometry, PEP or CPAP.
SourceHess Ch 39 · RCP 202 Lssn 8b S14–S16, S19

1Atelectrauma vs Biotrauma vs "Pneumotrauma" (Barotrauma) vs Volutrauma

  • Atelectrauma: injury from repeated alveolar collapse and reopening; smaller VT, recruitment and PEEP that prevents derecruitment reduce shear stress.
  • Volutrauma: alveolar overdistention from excessive VT; ARDS leaves fewer healthy alveoli, so use about 6 mL/kg predicted body weight.
  • Barotrauma: alveolar rupture and air leak from excessive pressure; pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum and subcutaneous emphysema are examples.
  • Pressure protection: plateau pressure < 30 cm H₂O is the deck’s most important target; PEEP prevents collapse while low VT prevents overdistention.
  • Biotrauma: VILI releases inflammatory cytokines into the lungs and bloodstream, potentially contributing to systemic inflammation and multiorgan dysfunction.

2Rates of Mortality for Acute Lung Injury (ALI)

  • ALI: an older category eliminated by Berlin; board-oriented older cutoffs are ALI ≤ 300 and ARDS ≤ 200 for P/F.
  • Chang ARDS mortality: 10% to 90%; the wide range reflects different diseases, definitions, treatments and populations.

3Patients with the Highest Incidence of ARDS

  • Highest risk: patients with sepsis, with a 40% ARDS incidence in Hess; sepsis is the most common adult cause in the decks.
  • Other major risks: gastric aspiration, transfusions and severe trauma; about one-third of patients with witnessed aspiration develop ARDS.
  • Direct injury: begins in the lungs, especially pneumonia or gastric aspiration; pulmonary contusion and inhalation injury are other examples.
  • Indirect injury: begins outside the lungs, especially sepsis, trauma with shock and blood-product transfusion.
  • Frequency: ARDS accounts for 10–15% of ICU admissions; about 25% of mechanically ventilated patients have ARDS in the deck.
  • Timing: clinical signs appear 6–72 hours after the insult and progress rapidly; high pressure and VT increase risk during prolonged ventilation.
SourceRCP 103 ARDS S7–S9 · RCP 105 Procedures S29–S30, S35 · Hess Ch 38

4What Causes Decreased Lung Compliance in ARDS

  • Starting injury: inflammation increases alveolar-capillary permeability → protein-rich interstitial and alveolar edema → stiff, poorly compliant lungs.
  • Surfactant: type II cell injury reduces secretion, and protein-rich edema inactivates surfactant → alveolar collapse and atelectasis.
  • Hyaline membranes: fibrin and cellular debris line alveolar walls, increasing stiffness and decreasing compliance.
  • Later fibrosis: some patients enter fibroproliferation after ARDS lasting longer than 3 to 7 days; compliance can stay poor despite improving hypoxemia.
  • Result: rapid shallow breathing, increased work of breathing, shunt and severe hypoxemia; more pressure is needed to inflate the lungs.
  • PEEP: alveolar recruitment can improve compliance; PFTs show restriction with reduced volumes and normal or increased FEV₁/FVC.
SourceRCP 103 ARDS S2–S5, S14, S17 · RCP 105 Procedures S31, S34, Principles S7 · RCP 202 Lssn 2 S37 · Hess Ch 38, 51

5Berlin Definition and P/F Severity Categories

  • Berlin: onset within 1 week, bilateral opacities and respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload.
  • Oxygenation: measure PaO₂/FiO₂ with at least 5 cm H₂O of PEEP or CPAP; exclude effusions, collapse or nodules explaining the opacities.
  • Severity: mild 201–300, moderate 101–200, severe ≤ 100; use these bands for ARDS severity questions.
  • Example: PaO₂ 58 mm Hg on FiO₂ 0.60 → P/F = 58 ÷ 0.60 = 96.7 (≈ 97), severe ARDS.
  • Check PaCO₂: hypoventilation can lower PaO₂ and P/F while raising PaCO₂; rule it out before interpreting the ratio as ARDS.

6Lung-Protective Ventilation for ARDS

  • Core settings: VT ≈ 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, Pplat < 30 cm H₂O and driving pressure ≤ 15 cm H₂O.
  • Driving pressure: ΔP = Pplat − PEEP; low VT limits overdistention while PEEP prevents derecruitment.
  • Oxygenation targets: PaO₂ 55–80 mm Hg and SpO₂ 88–95%; adjust FiO₂ and PEEP together using the PEEP/FiO₂ table.
  • Table example: FiO₂ 30% → PEEP ≈ 5; 60% → ≈ 10; 100% → 20–24.
  • Permissive hypercapnia: low VT 4–7 mL/kg may allow PaCO₂ > 50 mm Hg; the Burn deck requires pH ≥ 7.25.
  • Prone: more than 12 hours per day in severe ARDS; Hess reports mortality benefit in patients with P/F < 150.
  • Support: the ARDS deck favors immediate invasive ventilation with full support; Hess excludes NIV for ARDS respiratory failure or progression despite HFNC.

1Acetylcysteine (N-Acetylcysteine, NAC, Mucomyst)

  • NAC/Mucomyst: mucolytic whose free sulfhydryl group breaks mucoprotein disulfide bonds, lowering mucus viscosity.

  • Do not mix: sodium ampicillin, amphotericin B, erythromycin lactobionate, tetracyclines or aminoglycosides in the same nebulizer with NAC.

2Neuromuscular Blocking Agents: Depolarizing vs Nondepolarizing

  • Safety first: NMBAs provide paralysis without sedation or analgesia; give analgesia first, sedation next, then paralysis, with mechanical ventilation.
  • Depolarizing: succinylcholine activates ACh receptors, causing fasciculations then sustained depolarization and flaccid paralysis; there is no reversal.
  • Succinylcholine timing: onset 60–90 seconds, clinical duration 10–15 minutes in the NMBA deck; plasma pseudocholinesterase deficiency prolongs paralysis.
  • Major hazards: succinylcholine causes hyperkalemia and can trigger malignant hyperthermia; rising ETCO₂ precedes fever, and dantrolene treats it.
  • Nondepolarizing: competitive ACh antagonists without fasciculations; pancuronium is longest acting, rocuronium/vecuronium/cisatracurium intermediate, mivacurium shortest.
  • Organ failure: choose cisatracurium for Hofmann degradation; pancuronium depends mainly on kidneys and vecuronium mainly on liver.
  • Reversal: neostigmine increases competing ACh; sugammadex encapsulates only rocuronium and vecuronium, with renal excretion of the complex.
Watch outExam succinylcholine timing: NMBA deck 60–90 seconds / 10–15 minutes; Hess says 30 to 60 seconds / 4 to 5 minutes.
Watch outExam organ failure: NMBA deck says avoid succinylcholine and use cisatracurium; Hess says renal/hepatic decline does not require succinylcholine dose reduction.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 18 S4, S6, S13–S14, S16–S28, S31, S33 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S42–S43, S48 · Hess Ch 57

3Sedatives

  • Sedation: reduced awareness; analgesia is pain relief. Benzodiazepines, barbiturates and propofol act through GABA; provide separate analgesia when needed.
  • Benzodiazepines: midazolam, lorazepam and diazepam provide anxiolysis, sedation and amnesia; flumazenil reverses them. They augment opioid respiratory depression.
  • Midazolam: onset 1 to 3 min, duration 15 to 30 min; induction 0.1 to 0.3 mg/kg IV; active metabolite accumulates in renal failure.
  • Propofol: rapid IV sedative; 1 to 2.5 mg/kg IV push, maintenance 0.05 to 0.2 mg/kg/min; watch hypotension, apnea, bradycardia and triglycerides.
  • Dexmedetomidine: selective α₂ agonist giving sedation without respiratory depression; can continue through extubation. Watch hypotension, bradycardia and sinus arrest.
  • Etomidate: hemodynamically stable RSI choice without analgesia; onset 30 to 60 s, duration 3 to 5 min.
  • Ketamine: analgesia, amnesia and sedation while preserving respiratory drive; useful with hypotension or bronchospasm, but may cause disturbing reemergence dreams.
Watch outExam pain control: propofol has no useful analgesia; add an opioid. One Chang slide says analgesia only at high dose; Hess says none.
Watch outDeck propofol adverse effects include laryngospasm/bronchospasm; Hess says it reduces airway resistance and is useful in bronchospasm. Know both wordings.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 20 S19–S20, S22, S42 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S51, S53–S55, S61, S63–S67, S71–S72, Airway S42 · Hess Ch 57 · Hess Ch 17

4Opiates (Opioids)

  • Opioids: narcotic analgesics for moderate to severe pain; respiratory depression is the most important hazard and increases with benzodiazepines.
  • Reversal: naloxone (Narcan), including nasal spray; PCA patients should have a bag-mask and naloxone available in the room.
  • Morphine: standard reference opioid with onset about 5 minutes; boluses can release histamine. Hydromorphone is 5–10 times stronger than morphine.
  • Fentanyl: almost immediate onset and no histamine release with boluses; large boluses may cause muscle rigidity.
  • Deck receptors: mu → sedation, kappa → hypnosis, sigma → delirium; naloxone blocks them with greatest affinity for mu.
  • Other effects: constipation, nausea, vomiting, cough suppression and tolerance; methylnaltrexone treats opioid-induced constipation.
Watch outExam receptor list: use deck mu/kappa/sigma; Hess lists opioid receptors as mu/delta/kappa.
Watch outInhaled opioids: the deck contradicts itself about improved effectiveness; Hess finds no evidence for breathlessness. No clear exam pick is supplied.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 20 S19, S31–S32, S34 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S58 · Hess Ch 57

5Half-Life of Medications

  • Half-life: time for plasma drug concentration to decrease by one-half; longer half-life means more prolonged effects and greater accumulation risk.
  • Risk factors: advanced age and impaired hepatic or renal function can prolong half-life and increase drug toxicity.
  • Examples: adenosine ~12 seconds; propofol under 30 minutes; diazepam about 24 hours in younger adults, approaching 96 hours in older adults.
  • Other examples: omalizumab 17–22 days; theophylline highly variable; lorazepam much longer than midazolam.
  • NMBA half-life, min: mivacurium 2; atracurium 20; cisatracurium 20–30; vecuronium 60–130; rocuronium 80–100; pancuronium 110–140; succinylcholine unknown.
  • Maintenance rule: dosing rate (mg/hr) = Clp (L/hr) × plasma concentration (mg/L) to maintain a steady drug level.
Watch outNot in your sources: numeric half-lives for other ICU sedatives and opioids beyond the supplied examples.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 2 S18, Ch 18 S21, Ch 21 S16, S45, Ch 23 S6 · RCP 202 Lssn 2 S39 · Hess Ch 57 · Heuer (chapter not stated)

6Routes of Administration

  • Enteral: absorbed through the GI tract; oral, sublingual, buccal and rectal routes are included in Hess.
  • Parenteral: usually injection; IV, IM, SC, IO and intrathecal in the deck. Transdermal patches, inhalation and topical preparations are other routes.
  • Inhaled advantages: smaller doses, fewer side effects, rapid onset, respiratory targeting and painless administration; bypasses the GI tract and first-pass metabolism.
  • Drug rules: nondepolarizing NMBAs and propofol are IV only; epinephrine has no oral bioavailability, making oral administration unacceptable.
Watch outExam: classify intrathecal as parenteral (deck); Hess groups it with percutaneous or mucosal routes.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 2 S2, S4–S5, Ch 1 S19, Ch 18 S21 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S65 · Hess Ch 57

7Bioavailability

  • Bioavailability: proportion of a drug reaching systemic circulation; IV is the standard at 100% because the dose goes directly into blood.
  • First-pass effect: hepatic metabolism before systemic circulation reduces oral bioavailability; higher doses or sublingual, transdermal or IV routes may be needed.
  • Examples: food reduces zafirlukast bioavailability by about 40%; epinephrine has no oral bioavailability.
  • L/T ratio: lung dose ÷ (lung dose + GI dose); a higher ratio means more efficient delivery to the respiratory tract.
  • Raise L/T: efficient devices, high first-pass metabolism of swallowed drug, mouth washing/rinsing/spitting, a reservoir, correct instruction and breath holding.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 2 S2, S12, S17, S21–S23 · Hess Ch 57

8Organs of Elimination

  • Main organs: liver = drug metabolism; kidneys = drug excretion. Lungs, saliva and sweat are minor elimination routes.
  • Renal function: CrCl or eGFR primarily determines how quickly and completely drugs are eliminated; renal failure increases accumulation and toxicity.
  • Benzodiazepines: metabolized in the liver to active and inactive metabolites, then excreted mainly in urine.
  • NMBA choices: pancuronium → kidneys; vecuronium → liver; cisatracurium → organ-independent Hofmann degradation and preferred with liver/kidney failure.
  • Other pathways: succinylcholine and mivacurium use plasma cholinesterase; the sugammadex–drug complex is excreted through kidneys.
Watch outExam organ failure: NMBA deck says avoid succinylcholine and use cisatracurium; Hess says no succinylcholine dose reduction is needed.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 2 S10, S17–S18, Ch 18 S23–S24, S27, S33 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S54, Principles S40 · RCP 202 Lssn 3 S20 · Hess Ch 57

9Creatinine vs Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN)

  • Creatinine: muscle-metabolism waste, filtered with little reabsorption; more specific and reliable for kidney function than BUN, but affected by muscle mass.
  • BUN: urea formed in liver during protein breakdown; reflects protein metabolism, hydration and renal perfusion, making it less kidney-specific.
  • RCP 202 normals: BUN 7–20 mg/dL; creatinine men 0.7–1.3 mg/dL, women 0.6–1.1 mg/dL.
  • Ratio: BUN/creatinine normally about 10:1 to 20:1; high BUN with normal creatinine suggests dehydration or high protein intake.
  • Both high: suggests kidney disease or AKI; elevated BUN and creatinine together are called azotemia.
  • Creatinine limitation: may not increase until more than 50% of renal function deteriorates; CrCl is more precise.
  • Normal CrCl: men 97 to 137 mL/min, women 88 to 128 mL/min; decreases 6.5 mL/min per decade after age 40.
Watch outExam: use RCP 202 BUN 7–20 mg/dL and creatinine men 0.7–1.3 / women 0.6–1.1 mg/dL. Other decks and Hess give different normal ranges.
SourceRCP 202 Lssn 3 S23, S26 · RCP 103 Clinical Data S53 · RCP 104 Ch 13 S12 · RCP 105 Principles S39–S40 · Hess Ch 5

10Monitoring NMBAs and What Strengthens or Weakens the Block

  • Train-of-four: stimulate 4 times at 2 Hz over 2 seconds; more twitches means less paralysis.
  • NMBA deck: 0 twitches = ~100% blockade; 1–2 = ~90–95%; 3 = ~80%; 4 = < 75%. ICU goal is 1–2 twitches.
  • Recovery: diaphragm is last paralyzed and first to recover; rule out ventilator problems before paralyzing an agitated patient.
  • Comfort clues: tachycardia, hypertension, diaphoresis and lacrimation may indicate discomfort when paralysis masks clinical signs.
  • Stronger block: inhaled anesthetics, aminoglycosides, acidosis, hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia potentiate paralysis in the deck.
  • Weaker block: alkalosis and hypercalcemia inhibit paralysis; remember the deck’s rule, acidotic and low electrolytes = more paralysis.
Watch outExam: use NMBA deck ~90–95% blockade at 1–2 twitches and its 1–2 goal. Chang gives 80% to 90% and calls 3 twitches suitable for synchrony.
SourceRCP 104 Ch 18 S31–S32, S34–S36 · RCP 105 Weaning/Pharmacotherapy S45, S49, S56
