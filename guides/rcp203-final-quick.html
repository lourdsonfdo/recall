Pneumonia, CXRs, Pleural Effusions, Accessory Muscles, Extubation Parameters

1Pneumonia (PNA): what it is, signs and symptoms, diagnosis

  • Pneumonia: infection and inflammation of the gas-exchange lung → alveolar fluid and consolidation; causes include bacteria, viruses, fungi or aspiration.
  • Signs: fever, tachycardia, tachypnea; dry cough may become productive, yellow/green, rusty or bloody.
  • Consolidation: ↑ fremitus, dull percussion, bronchial sounds/crackles and whispered pectoriloquy; CXR shows increased density and air bronchograms.
  • Diagnosis: clinical findings plus an imaging infiltrate; sputum culture and sensitivity help identify the pathogen.
  • ABG: early respiratory alkalosis with hypoxemia; severe disease → respiratory acidosis with hypoxemia.
Source103 Infectious S2, 26, 27 · Lssn 2a S38, 39, 50 · Lssn 3b S15 · Hess Ch 36

2Pneumonia categories, and typical vs atypical pneumonia

  • CAP: acquired through normal social contact; S. pneumoniae is the most common bacterium.
  • HAP/VAP: HAP develops ≥ 48 hours after admission and was not present at admission; use ≥ 48 h after intubation for VAP.
  • Typical: abrupt rigors, high fever, pleuritic pain, rusty/purulent sputum and lobar consolidation; usually ↑ WBC.
  • Atypical: flu-like illness, dry cough, moderate fever, usually normal WBC and diffuse interstitial/peribronchial opacities.
  • Organisms: Mycoplasma causes walking pneumonia and is the most common atypical cause; atypical pneumonia → think macrolide.
Watch outVAP wording conflicts: give ≥ 48 h after intubation; the RCP 105 slide saying “within 48 hours” contradicts its updated notes.
Source103 Infectious S7, 9, 12, 13, 16, 17, 18 · Lssn 6a S59 · 105 Procedures S43 · 104 Pharm Ch 14 S29 · Hess Ch 36

3Reading a CXR

  • Start: verify patient, orientation and view; RIPE = Rotation, Inspiration, Position, Exposure. Compare previous films and check tubes/lines.
  • White: consolidation, atelectasis, edema or effusion; black → emphysema or pneumothorax. Blunted costophrenic angle → pleural fluid.
  • Diaphragm: flattened → hyperinflation; elevated → volume loss or weakness. Kerley B lines → interstitial pulmonary edema.
  • Heart: width > 50% of chest width on PA → cardiomegaly; AP magnifies the heart.
  • ETT: tip 4–6 cm above the carina; too deep usually enters the right mainstem.
  • Special views: lateral decubitus finds free pleural fluid; expiration shows small pneumothorax. Air bronchograms locate opacity within lung.
SourceLssn 2a S52–57 · Hess Ch 7

4Pleural effusion

  • Effusion: fluid in the pleural space → restricted expansion and reduced compliance; common causes are CHF, cancer and pneumonia.
  • Transudate: systemic pressure changes → thin, low-protein fluid; causes include CHF (most common), cirrhosis and nephrotic syndrome.
  • Exudate: inflammation → high-protein, cloudy fluid; infection, malignancy or autoimmune disease. Fluid pH < 7.30 suggests exudate.
  • Findings: dull percussion, ↓ fremitus and diminished/absent sounds; large effusion shifts trachea away. Earliest CXR sign = blunted costophrenic angle.
  • Thoracentesis: diagnoses fluid type or relieves respiratory distress; up to about 1500 mL removed. Hazards include pneumothorax and re-expansion edema.
Source103 Pleura S2, 5, 6, 7, 8, 14, 20 · 103 Clinical Data S66, 67 · Lssn 2a S50 · Lssn 3b S35 · Hess Ch 9

5Accessory muscles

  • Inspiration: sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, upper trapezius and pectoralis major; Hess also lists external intercostals.
  • Expiration: normally passive; internal intercostals and abdominal muscles become active with significant expiratory resistance.
  • At rest: accessory muscle use means increased WOB; thoracoabdominal paradox or accessory muscle use during an SBT suggests stopping it.
  • Fatigue: diaphoresis, declining VT and rising PaCO₂ with accessory muscle use suggest impending ventilatory failure; paralysis masks accessory muscle use.
SourceLssn 2a S37 · Lssn 3a S60 · Lssn 7 S93, 94 · 105 Weaning S26 · 105 Initiation S5 · 104 Pharm Ch 18 S34 · Hess Ch 49

6Extubation (weaning) parameters

  • RSBI: f ÷ VT (L); 38 ÷ 0.3 = 127 → not ready. General predictor < 100; NBRC commonly uses < 105.
  • Strength: NIF −20 to −30 cm H₂O in 20 sec; VC > 10 mL/kg; general spontaneous VT ≥ 5 mL/kg.
  • Screen: P/F ≥ 150–200, PEEP ≤ 5–8, FiO₂ ≤ 0.4–0.5 and pH ≥ 7.25.
  • SBT: RCP 203 uses 30–120 min after a 2–5 min screen; failed trial → find cause and repeat every 24 hours.
  • NHLBI SBT: SpO₂ ≥ 90% and/or PaO₂ ≥ 60, VT ≥ 4 mL/kg PBW, RR ≤ 35, pH ≥ 7.30, no distress.
  • Extubation: pass SBT, maintain gas exchange, protect the airway and clear secretions; most serious complication = laryngospasm.
Watch outUse deck RSBI < 100 (NBRC < 105), VC > 10 mL/kg (Hess 15 mL/kg), NIF −20 to −30 cm H₂O and minute-ventilation ceiling 10 L/min (Hess 12 L/min).
Watch outRCP 203 SBT 30–120 min; RCP 105 up to 30 min. General VT ≥ 5 mL/kg; NHLBI ≥ 4 mL/kg PBW. Weaning deck P/F ≥ 150; RCP 203 ≥ 150–200.
SourceLssn 2b S16, 18, 19, 45 · Lssn 3a S52, 59, 60 · Lssn 6a S62, 63 · Lssn 7 S90–94 · 105 Weaning S11–19 · 105 Monitoring S30 · Hess Ch 20

7Extubation Readiness — the Checks That Come After the SBT

  • Before testing: daily sedation interruption in mechanically ventilated patients stable enough to tolerate it permits neurologic and extubation assessment.
  • After SBT: assess extubation separately; patent upper airway, adequate cuff leak, airway protection and effective secretion clearance are required.
  • Protection: positive gag reflex and ability to raise the head; deep cough with suctioning supports secretion clearance.
  • Alert patient: MEP > 60 cm H₂O and/or peak cough flow > 60 L/min supports extubation.
  • Peak cough flow: > 160 L/min strong; 60–160 L/min weak but possibly adequate; < 60 L/min inadequate for extubation.
SourceLssn 8b S22–25

8Thoracentesis and Chest Tubes: What the RT Does, and How the Drainage System Works

  • Thoracentesis: needle/catheter removes pleural fluid; ultrasound guides it. No absolute contraindications; relative: uncooperative patient, severe bleeding disorder, severe bullous disease.
  • Positioning: conscious patient sits up leaning slightly forward on a bedside table; immobile patient affected side down at the bed’s edge. Consider a cough suppressant.
  • During/after: keep SpO₂ ≥ 90%; call for dyspnea, hypotension, chest pain or hemoptysis; post CXR or ultrasound rules out pneumothorax.
  • Fluid: transudate if pleural fluid protein < 25 g/L (normal serum protein); exudate if > 35 g/L.
  • Chest tube: 24–36 Fr; pneumothorax → 4th or 5th intercostal space, anterior axillary line; 15–20 cm H₂O suction. Crepitus at the site after removal = air leaking again.
  • Three chambers: collection; water seal (2 cm under water) protects the lung; suction-chamber water level, not wall suction, sets the suction.
SourceRCP 203 Lssn 10 Ch 16 S37–40, 43–44

Pharmacology Calculations

1Units and Conversions2The Proportion Method and mg/kg Dosing3Percentage-Strength Solutions: % → mg/mL → Dose4Diluent Does Not Change the Dose5Ratio Solutions, w/w – w/v – v/v, and Diluting…

1Units and Conversions

  • Volume: mL and cc are identical; 3 mL = 3 cc. Milli- means one-thousandth of the base unit.
  • Mass: 1,000 mg = 1 g; move the decimal 3 places. Example: 4,500 mg = 4.5 g.
  • Household: 1 teaspoon = 5 mL = 60 drops; 1 tablespoon = 15 mL (3 tsp) = 180 drops.
  • Cup: 1 cup = 240 mL (8 fl oz); convert to consistent units before calculating.
Source104 Pharm Ch 4 S4, 14

2The Proportion Method and mg/kg Dosing

  • Proportion: Known Dose ÷ Known Amount = Desired Dose ÷ Desired Amount; convert units first, then cross-multiply.
  • Weight dosing: mg/kg × weight gives the dose; use the concentration to calculate preparation volume.
  • Worked example: caffeine citrate for a 1.5-kg infant: 20 × 1.5 = 30 mg loading; 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 mg daily.
Source104 Pharm Ch 4 S8, 9 · Pharm Ch 8 S28 · Pharm Ch 10 S17, 21

3Percentage-Strength Solutions: % → mg/mL → Dose

  • Percent strength: grams per 100 mL; Drug % × 10 = mg/mL. Convert concentration before calculating dose.
  • Worked example: 0.5% albuterol → 0.5 × 10 = 5 mg/mL; 2.5 ÷ 5 = 0.5 mL.
  • Formula: concentration × volume = dose; dose ÷ concentration = volume. Keep mg (weight) separate from mL (volume).
Source104 Pharm Ch 4 S12–15 · Pharm Ch 6 S18

4Diluent Does Not Change the Dose

  • Diluent: changes treatment time and total volume; it does not change the amount of active drug.
  • Example: 2.5 mg albuterol remains 2.5 mg when mixed with 2, 5 or 10 mL saline.
  • Nebulizer: most aerosolize poorly below ~1 mL because of dead volume; aim for about 2.5–3 mL total.
Source104 Pharm Ch 4 S16, 17

5Ratio Solutions, w/w – w/v – v/v, and Diluting a Stock Solution

  • Solutions: w/w, w/v and v/v describe percent types; pharmacology uses w/v. A 1:100 solution contains 1 g in 100 mL.
  • Ratio example: 1:200 epinephrine = 1000 mg/200 mL = 5 mg/mL; 0.5 mL contains 2.5 mg.
  • Dilution: V₁ × C₁ = V₂ × C₂; 5 mL of 10% from 20% requires 2.5 mL stock + 2.5 mL water.
  • Concentration: 10,000 ppm = 1%; albuterol 2.5 mg/0.5 mL = 5 mg/mL = 0.5%.
Watch outHess prints 5% for albuterol 2.5 mg/0.5 mL; this is an arithmetic error. Give 0.5% on the exam.
SourceLssn 3a S73 · Hess Ch 55, 14

Ventilator Changes After ABGs

1The decision table: which setting fixes which A…2Calculating the new rate for a target PaCO₂3When the ABG says change the vent but you shoul…4Oxygenation targets, FiO₂ limits, FiO₂ vs PEEP5Adjusting BiPAP after the ABG6Which Knob for Hypoxemia — the 60-60 Rule, and…

1The decision table: which setting fixes which ABG value

  • Respiratory acidosis: ↑ set rate first, keeping 12–24/min; up to 35/min in acute lung injury.
  • If rate fails: adjust VT within 4–8 mL/kg PBW while limiting Pplat; in PC, VT follows ∆P = PIP − PEEP.
  • Respiratory alkalosis: ↓ frequency or VT only when overventilation is the cause; preserve compensatory ventilation.
  • Hypoxemia: low PaO₂ with near-normal PaCO₂ → ↑ FiO₂; low PaO₂ with high PaCO₂ → improve ventilation first.
  • Refractory hypoxemia: P/F < 200 → add or ↑ PEEP; increasing FiO₂ does not lower PaCO₂.
  • Targets: goal pH 7.35–7.45; chronic retainers may have normal pH with PaCO₂ ≥ 55, so do not automatically target 40.
  • Permissive hypercapnia: at rate/VT/Pplat limits, allow PaCO₂ to rise while pH stays above 7.20–7.25; preserve lung protection.
Watch outThe same RCP 203 deck gives Pplat ≤ 30–35 cm H₂O and a safer PC limit of 30 cm H₂O or less; use ≤ 30 to be safe.
SourceLssn 7 S31, 36, 38 · 105 Initiation S12, 13 · 105 Management S4, 6, 8, 11, 26 · 105 Monitoring S30, 34 · 105 Procedures S37

2Calculating the new rate for a target PaCO₂

  • Rate example: set rate 14 × current PaCO₂ 60 ÷ desired PaCO₂ 40 = 840 ÷ 40 = 21 breaths/min.
  • Why: with constant CO₂ production, PaCO₂ varies inversely with alveolar ventilation; increasing ventilation lowers PaCO₂.
  • COPD: excessive rate shortens expiration → air trapping and auto-PEEP; consider this before increasing frequency.
Watch outNot in your sources: a printed, labeled new-rate formula or any new-VT formula; the slide provides only the numeric rate example.
Source105 Management S4 · 105 Initiation S12 · Hess Ch 20

3When the ABG says change the vent but you should not

  • Overventilation: low PaCO₂ from improper settings → reduce f, VT or PS after establishing the cause.
  • Compensation: low PaCO₂ from hypoxia or metabolic acidosis → preserve ventilation and treat the underlying problem.
  • Anxiety: find the cause; do not reduce ventilatory support just to normalize PaCO₂.
  • Oversedation/fatigue: correct the cause of rising PaCO₂; increasing frequency is not the proper action in the described scenario.
  • Metabolic disorder: no ventilator adjustment for metabolic acidosis or alkalosis; do not normalize a compensatory PaCO₂.
Source105 Management S24, 26, 27 · 105 Monitoring S29, 30

4Oxygenation targets, FiO₂ limits, FiO₂ vs PEEP

  • Routine healthy patient: PaO₂ 80–100 with SpO₂ ≥ 92% on the lowest effective FiO₂.
  • If < 50% O₂ is insufficient: accept PaO₂ ≥ 55–60 with SpO₂ ≥ 88%; chronic hypercapnia target PaO₂ 55–60.
  • ARDS: lung-protective goals are PaO₂ 55–80 and SpO₂ 88–95%; PEEP recruits shunting alveoli and increases FRC.
  • Choose: acceptable oxygenation on < 50% O₂ → adjust FiO₂; otherwise adjust PEEP. Improve oxygenation → lower FiO₂ to ≤ 50% first.
  • Escalate: P/F ≤ 200, or ≥ 50% O₂ with PEEP ≥ 12 failing → recommend NHLBI ARDS protocol.
Watch outGive RCP 203 routine PaO₂ 80–100 / SpO₂ ≥ 92%; ARDS 55–80 / 88–95%. RCP 105 routine targets differ.
Watch outFiO₂ ceilings conflict (< 50% versus < 60%); use < 50% on the exam.
SourceLssn 7 S7, 39 · 105 Initiation S17 · 105 Management S11, 16 · 105 Procedures S37, 38 · 105 Monitoring S38 · Hess Ch 20

5Adjusting BiPAP after the ABG

  • Pressure support: IPAP − EPAP = ∆P; IPAP/∆P controls ventilation, while FiO₂/EPAP controls oxygenation.
  • From 10/5: oxygenation only → 12/7; ventilation only → 15/5; both → 15/7.
  • EPAP: increase IPAP equally whenever EPAP rises; keep IPAP < 20–25 cm H₂O to avoid gastric insufflation.
SourceLssn 7 S52, 54, 57

6Which Knob for Hypoxemia — the 60-60 Rule, and When to Lower Which

  • Low oxygen: PaO₂ < 60 torr or SaO₂ < 90% → ↑ FiO₂ if below 0.60; otherwise ↑ PEEP.
  • 60-60 rule: PaO₂ > 60 torr on FiO₂ < 0.6 → mainly V̇/Q̇ imbalance; PaO₂ < 60 torr on FiO₂ > 0.6 → shunting.
  • 50-50 rule: PaO₂ < 50 on FiO₂ > 0.5 → significant shunt, add PEEP; oxygen-responsive mismatch → ↑ FiO₂.
  • Too high: PaO₂ > 100 torr → lower the more dangerous setting; oxygenation improving → FiO₂ 0.50 or less, then PEEP.
  • Examples: PEEP 10 cm H₂O/FiO₂ 75% → lower FiO₂; PEEP 18 cm H₂O/FiO₂ 45% → lower PEEP.
  • CO₂: with VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW and Pplat < 30 cm H₂O, prefer RR changes to normalize pH.
SourceLssn 8b S41, 43

Lung Sounds: All of Them, and What Causes Each

1Normal Breath Sounds and Where They Belong2Diminished or Absent Breath Sounds3Crackles (Rales): Fine and Coarse4Wheezes, Rhonchi and Stridor5Pleural Friction Rub, Grunting and Voice Sounds6Breath Sounds by Condition

1Normal Breath Sounds and Where They Belong

  • Vesicular: soft, low-pitched sounds over peripheral lung; inspiration lasts longer than expiration. Location determines whether a sound is normal.
  • Bronchovesicular: moderate pitch/intensity over upper sternum and between scapulae; inspiration and expiration are equally long.
  • Bronchial: loud, high-pitched, tubular over trachea; peripheral bronchial sounds suggest consolidation or atelectasis transmitting large-airway sound.
Watch outHess figure wording conflicts with its table and the deck; give low-pitched for vesicular breath sounds.
SourceLssn 2a S46, 47 · 103 Clinical Data S41 · 103 ARDS S16 · Hess Ch 1, 62

2Diminished or Absent Breath Sounds

  • Diffuse decrease: shallow breathing, low airflow or hyperinflation; consider emphysema and acute asthma. Air/fluid or obesity can block transmission.
  • Unilateral decrease: bronchial intubation, pneumothorax, atelectasis or pleural effusion; postoperative basal decrease commonly reflects atelectasis.
  • Silent asthma chest: airflow is too low to produce wheezing; this is a late, critical warning of imminent respiratory arrest.
SourceLssn 2a S46 S50 · 103 Clinical Data S43 · 103 Asthma S28 · Hess Ch 1, 20

3Crackles (Rales): Fine and Coarse

  • Crackles: discontinuous popping from reopening airways/alveoli or secretions; “crackles” is the preferred term for rales.
  • Fine: high-pitched, end-inspiratory, usually basal; pulmonary edema, ARDS, atelectasis and ILD. Bibasilar “Velcro-like” crackles suggest idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
  • Coarse: excessive large-airway secretions; may clear with coughing or suctioning. Coarse tracheal crackles indicate retained secretions needing suction.
  • Pattern: pneumonia produces crackles over the involved lobe; pulmonary-edema crackles spreading higher suggest worsening CHF.
SourceLssn 2a S46, 47 · 103 Pulm Edema S16 · 103 ARDS S16 · 103 ILD S41 · 103 Atelectasis S13, 26 · 103 Infectious S26 · 103 Bronchiectasis S21, 22 · 103 COPD S32 · 103 Pulm Edema S37 · 103 Clinical Data S42 · Hess Ch 1, 17, 22, 34

4Wheezes, Rhonchi and Stridor

  • Wheeze: musical, usually expiratory from narrowed bronchi; smooth-muscle constriction, edema or mucus. Think asthma/COPD → bronchodilator.
  • Rhonchi: low-pitched, rumbling or snoring from larger-airway secretions; may clear or change after coughing.
  • Stridor: high-pitched inspiratory crowing from upper-airway obstruction; croup or post-extubation edema → racemic epinephrine.
  • Severity: severe asthma may produce inspiratory and expiratory wheezes; an isolated pediatric wheeze may indicate foreign-body aspiration.
SourceLssn 2a S47 · 103 Clinical Data S43 · 103 Asthma S20, 28 · 103 COPD S12 · 103 Bronchiectasis · 105 Procedures S15 · Lssn 6b S9 · Hess Ch 1, 22, 62

5Pleural Friction Rub, Grunting and Voice Sounds

  • Pleural rub: localized grating/creaking from inflamed pleural surfaces rubbing together; heard with pleurisy and sometimes pleural effusion.
  • Grunting: distressed newborn closes the glottis to maintain lung volume; recognize it as respiratory distress.
  • Voice sounds: consolidation increases bronchophony, egophony (“e” → “a”) and whispered pectoriloquy; atelectasis may diminish whispered pectoriloquy.
  • Crackle timing: early inspiratory → opening atelectatic bronchi; late fine inspiratory → collapsed peripheral airways, as in edema, fibrosis or pneumonia.
Watch outNot in your sources: monophonic versus polyphonic wheeze descriptions; the instructor’s slide 47 sound tables are images.
SourceLssn 2a S47 · 103 Pleura S14 · 103 Pulm Edema & PVD S54 · 103 ILD S41 · 103 Clinical Data S43 · 103 Atelectasis S13 · Hess Ch 1

6Breath Sounds by Condition

  • Asthma/COPD: wheezes, diminished sounds, prolonged expiration and hyperresonance; consolidation → bronchial sounds/crackles, ↑ fremitus and dullness.
  • Pneumothorax: absent sounds, hyperresonance and trachea away; effusion → diminished/absent sounds, dullness and ↓ fremitus.
  • Atelectasis: diminished/absent sounds or crackles, dullness and trachea toward; upper-airway obstruction → stridor.
  • Edema/fibrosis: pulmonary edema produces crackles and sometimes wheezes; pulmonary fibrosis produces vesicular sounds with crackles.
SourceLssn 2a S50 · 103 Infectious S26 · 103 COPD S12 · 103 Asthma S20 · 103 Pleura S14 · 103 Atelectasis S13 · 103 Pulm Edema S16 · Lssn 7 S14 · Hess Ch 1

Orthopnea, Kussmaul Breathing, Cheyne-Stokes and Biot's Respirations

1Orthopnea2Kussmaul vs Cheyne-Stokes vs Biot: pattern and…3Why each pattern happens

1Orthopnea

  • Orthopnea: uncomfortable breathing lying down, typically relieved by sitting or standing; commonly CHF, pulmonary edema or fluid overload.
  • Mechanism: lying flat shifts blood centrally → increased filling pressures and interstitial edema; ask how many pillows the patient uses.
  • PND: air hunger wakes the patient 1 to 2 hours after falling asleep; commonly suggests cardiac dysfunction.
  • Platypnea: opposite of orthopnea—difficulty breathing unless lying flat; severe COPD and sleep apnea can also cause orthopnea.
Watch outPND timing differs: give 1–2 hours for this exam (RCP 203); RCP 103 and Hess say several hours.
SourceLssn 2a S10 · Lssn 7 S14 · 103 Clinical Data S17 · 103 Edema/PVD S15 · Hess Ch 1, 40, 35, 27, 52

2Kussmaul vs Cheyne-Stokes vs Biot: pattern and cause

  • Kussmaul: deep, fast, labored, regular breathing without apnea; compensation for severe metabolic acidosis, classically DKA.
  • Cheyne-Stokes: crescendo-decrescendo breathing alternating with apnea; CHF, stroke, brain injury/↑ ICP or central sleep apnea.
  • Biot’s: equal-sized irregular breaths or bursts followed by apnea lasting 10 to 30 seconds; brainstem/CNS injury, ↑ ICP or meningitis.
Watch outHess Ch 4 allows normal/reduced Kussmaul frequency; give deep and fast from the deck (Hess Ch 52 also says rapid).
Source103 Clinical Data S25 · Lssn 2a S21 · 202 Lssn 3 S24 · 105 Principles S40 · 103 Neuro S60 · Hess Ch 1, 4, 52

3Why each pattern happens

  • Kussmaul: metabolic acidosis drives hyperventilation to expel CO₂; compensation may remain incomplete despite very low PaCO₂.
  • Cheyne-Stokes: delayed lung–brain CO₂ communication in low cardiac output makes the respiratory response repeatedly overcorrect.
  • Apnea: begins when PaCO₂ falls below the apneic threshold; treat cardiac dysfunction, and ASV helps this central apnea pattern.
  • Biot’s: impaired central respiratory control from brainstem injury, ↑ ICP or meningitis produces irregular breathing and apnea.
Source202 Lssn 3 S24 · 103 ABG S36 · 103 Clinical Data S25 · 103 Edema/PVD S15 · 103 Sleep Apnea S8, 36 · 103 Neuro S60 · Hess Ch 45

Volume, Flow and Pressure Measuring Devices; Spirometer Calibration

1Vane Respirometer: What It Measures and Its Err…2Wright Respirometer: Bedside Setup and Use3Peak Flowmeter4Pressure Manometer5Capnometer6The 3-L Syringe in PFTs: When and Why7Standard Deviation (SD) Accepted Ranges in QC

1Vane Respirometer: What It Measures and Its Errors

  • Vane respirometer: Wright/Haloscale gas flow spins a vane; gears translate rotations into volume on a dial. Flow is unidirectional.
  • Errors: overreads high flows because of momentum; underreads low flows because of friction. Accuracy within 2% of recommended flow range.
  • Use: normal/controlled-flow volume measurement; never forced flows or volumes, which can damage the device.
SourceLssn 4 S95 · Hess Ch 54

2Wright Respirometer: Bedside Setup and Use

  • Measures: bedside VT, f and V̇E in spontaneous breathing; also slow vital capacity in neuromuscular disease.
  • Setup: one-way breathing valve plus HEPA filter; attach respirometer to the exhalation side for expired volumes.
  • Read: switch ON during inspiration, time the maneuver, switch OFF to freeze the reading; read before resetting.
  • Example: VT = V̇E ÷ f; 8 L/min ÷ 16/min = 0.5 L = 500 mL.
SourceLssn 4 S96, 97 · Lssn 2b S15, 18

3Peak Flowmeter

  • PEFR: maximum forced expiratory flow reflects larger airways; effort dependent, so use for monitoring rather than diagnosis.
  • Normal: male 450–550 L/min; female 320–470 L/min. Monitor asthma control and pre/post-bronchodilator effect.
  • Example: actual ÷ predicted × 100; 7 ÷ 10 L/sec = 70%. < 80% predicted suggests expiratory obstruction; check effort.
  • Personal-best zones: green 80–100%, yellow 50–80%, red < 50%; use the same device routinely.
Watch outHess uses green > 80% and yellow 50–79%; give deck zones 80–100%, 50–80%, < 50%.
SourceLssn 2b S14 · Lssn 3a S71 · 103 Asthma S34 · Hess Ch 8, 32

4Pressure Manometer

  • Manometer: measures pressure; a one-way valve allowing exhalation but blocking inspiration measures MIP/NIF; flip it for MEP.
  • NIF: more negative means stronger inspiratory muscles; measure in an alert, cooperative patient against an occluded device.
  • Cuff: goal 20–30 cm H₂O; attaching the manometer loses volume, so adjust the cuff pressure after measuring.
  • Manual ventilation: use a manometer to monitor airway pressure in infants and children.
SourceLssn 2b S19, 57, 58, 59 · 105 Weaning S15, 50 · Lssn 4 S59 · Hess Ch 54

5Capnometer

  • Terms: capnometry = CO₂ number; capnography = number plus waveform; capnogram = waveform. Most devices use infrared absorption.
  • Baseline: inspiratory PETCO₂ = 0 torr; PETCO₂ normally runs 1–5 torr below PaCO₂, with a wider difference in high deadspace.
  • Mainstream: heated sensor at the airway, mainly intubated patients; allow warm-up and avoid placement causing rebreathing.
  • Sidestream: most useful in non-intubated patients; condensate blocks sampling tubing and delayed response alters the waveform.
  • Calibration: 5% CO₂ (PCO₂ 38 torr), accepted 4.7–5.3%; auto-zero corrects offset, not gain.
Watch outSlide 38 says “Vented pts,” but its notes and slide 42 say non-intubated; give non-intubated for sidestream.
SourceLssn 2b S10 · Lssn 3b S22 · Lssn 5b S38, 39, 41, 42 · Hess Ch 2

6The 3-L Syringe in PFTs: When and Why

  • Daily calibration: before patient testing, inject a known 3.0-L syringe volume; perform the volume-spirometer leak check first.
  • Acceptance: ±3% or ±50 mL, whichever is larger; for 3 L, accept 2.91–3.09 L.
  • Linearity: inject 3 L at 0.5 s, 6 s and between; readings must stay within 100 mL of 3.0 L.
  • BTPS: OFF during calibration; leaving it on reads ~10% high. Low reading → leak; high → zeroing, BTPS or temperature.
  • Failure: stop patient testing, correct the cause and repeat calibration; record results on the daily QC log.
Watch outHess allows ±3.5% (2.90–3.10 L); give deck ±3% and 2.91–3.09 L.

2Paradoxical breathing

  • Motion: flail segment moves inward during inspiration and outward during expiration; the rest of the thorax moves oppositely.

Obstructive vs Central Sleep Apnea

1OSA vs CSA: the core difference2Risk factors and symptoms3Diagnosis: PSG and AHI4Treatment: OSA vs CSA

1OSA vs CSA: the core difference

  • OSA: effort without airflow → upper-airway collapse; PSG shows continuing thoracoabdominal effort and increasingly negative esophageal pressure.
  • CSA: no airflow and no effort → absent respiratory drive; no thoracoabdominal movement or esophageal pressure swings.
  • Mixed: starts central and ends obstructive; predominantly mixed events are classified and treated as OSA.
Source103 Sleep Apnea S5–11 · Hess Ch 45

2Risk factors and symptoms

  • OSA risks: obesity is the strongest predictor; neck > 17 inches in men or > 16 inches in women, male sex and age > 65.
  • OSA symptoms: loud snoring, witnessed apneas, morning headache, dry mouth, daytime sleepiness and mood/memory changes.
  • CSA: associated with heart failure, CNS disease, substances and high altitude; symptoms are milder, and snoring and daytime sleepiness are less common than in OSA.
Source103 Sleep Apnea S8, 20, 23–25 · Hess Ch 45

3Diagnosis: PSG and AHI

  • Diagnosis: full-night attended in-lab PSG is gold standard; AHI = (apneas + hypopneas) ÷ sleep hours.
  • Example: 100 events ÷ 8 h = 12.5/hr → mild; mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe > 30 events/hr.
  • OSA: AHI ≥ 15/hr without symptoms or ≥ 5/hr with symptoms, with 75% obstructive events; CSA has more than 50% central events.
  • Hypopnea: airflow ↓ ≥ 30% for ≥ 10 s with ≥ 3% SpO₂ drop or arousal.
Watch outHess requires at least 4% desaturation for hypopnea; give the deck’s ≥ 3% drop or arousal.
Source103 Sleep Apnea S6, 14, 18, 19, 20 · Lssn 7 S15 · Hess Ch 45

4Treatment: OSA vs CSA

  • OSA: weight loss, avoid alcohol/sedatives and supine sleep; CPAP is first-line and splints the upper airway open.
  • Control: optimum AHI < 5/hr; CPAP intolerance → mask/humidification/ramp adjustments, auto-titration, pressure relief or BiPAP.
  • CSA: treat the cause first; persistent CSA → CPAP/BiPAP; ASV targets hyperventilation-related CSA.
  • Backup: bilevel S/T supplies breaths during apnea; set 2–5 breaths below spontaneous rate.
Watch outFor “which CSA type does ASV treat,” give hyperventilation-related CSA; Hess warns against ASV in symptomatic HF with LVEF ≤ 45%.
Source103 Sleep Apnea S35, 36, 37 · Lssn 7 S15, 49, 56 · Hess Ch 45, 21

Heliox

1Heliox Concentrations2Methods of Delivering Heliox3Flowmeter Correction Factor (worked example)4Heliox in Infants, and Heliox Driving a Nebuliz…

1Heliox Concentrations

  • Mixtures: 80% He/20% O₂, 70% He/30% O₂ or 60% He/40% O₂; always provide at least 20% O₂.
  • Mechanism: low helium density reduces WOB through narrowed large airways; useful in obstruction or severe asthma.
  • Limit: if FiO₂ > 0.40 is needed, helium content is unlikely to provide clinical benefit; premixed cylinders avoid pure-helium anoxia.
SourceLssn 4 S92 · Hess Ch 14, 32

2Methods of Delivering Heliox

  • Spontaneous: tight-fitting nonrebreathing mask meeting inspiratory demand, or high-flow cannula; invasive delivery needs a cuffed airway.
  • Monitor: continuously measure FiO₂ with an O₂ analyzer and active alarms; use a tight system because helium leaks easily.
  • Equipment: use a helium-calibrated flowmeter or correction factor; ventilator delivered/measured VT may change.
  • Nebulizer: increase driving flow 50% to 100%; reservoir-mask flow often 12 to 15 L/min keeps the bag inflated.
SourceLssn 4 S92, 93 · Hess Ch 14

3Flowmeter Correction Factor (worked example)

  • Correction: O₂ flowmeter underreads heliox; actual flow = indicated flow × mixture correction factor.
  • Factors: 80/20 → 1.8; 70/30 → 1.6; 60/40 → 1.4. More helium means a larger correction.
  • Example: 80/20 at indicated 10 L/min → 10 × 1.8 = 18 L/min actual; desired 15 L/min → 15 ÷ 1.8 = 8.3 L/min.
SourceLssn 4 S93 · Hess Ch 14

4Heliox in Infants, and Heliox Driving a Nebulizer

  • Infants: heliox oxyhood can cause heat loss/hypothermia; high-flow nasal cannula is usually the better answer.
  • Delivery: spontaneous breathing → tight nonrebreathing mask or HFNC; invasive ventilation → cuffed airway and heliox-capable ventilator.
  • Bronchodilator: heliox-driven jet nebulization may improve delivery in severe acute obstruction when standard therapy fails.
  • Driving flow: deliberately increase 50% to 100% for nebulizer efficiency; this differs from correcting an O₂ flowmeter reading. Monitor FiO₂ with active alarms.
SourceLssn 8a S25, 26

1All Lung Volumes and Capacities

  • Volumes: VT 0.5 L; IRV 3.1 L; ERV 1.2 L; RV 1.2 L remains after maximal expiration.
  • IC: VT + IRV; 3.1 + 0.5 = 3.6 L, calculated from supplied volumes.
  • FRC: RV + ERV = 2.4 L; resting end-expiratory gas must be measured separately from spirometry.
  • VC/TLC: VC = VT + IRV + ERV, normal 4.8 L; TLC = RV + VC = FRC + IC, normal 6.0 L.
SourceLssn 3a S77 · 105 Weaning S11 · Lssn 2b S18, 75 · Hess Ch 8

2Spirometry Values: FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, FEF25–75%, PEFR

  • FVC/FEV1: FVC is forced exhaled volume from TLC; FEV1 is first-second volume. Deck normal ≥ 80% predicted for each.
  • Ratio: FEV1/FVC normal ≥ 70–80%, age dependent; < 70% → obstruction. FEF25–75% ≤ 70% predicted suggests small-airway obstruction.
  • Example: PEFR actual ÷ predicted × 100; 7 ÷ 10 L/sec = 70% → below < 80% cutoff.
  • Quality: at least three error-free maneuvers; two largest FVC and two largest FEV1 assessed separately must be within 150 mL.
Watch outHess favors LLN over fixed predicted cutoffs; use deck 80% / 70% cutoffs on this exam.
SourceLssn 3a S71, 75, 79 · Lssn 5b S29 · Lssn 2b S20 · Lssn 4 S98 · Hess Ch 8

3Pre/Post Bronchodilator Response

  • Test: repeat spirometry at peak response, usually 15–30 minutes after bronchodilator; % change = (post − pre) ÷ pre × 100.
  • Positive: adult FEV1 improves ≥ 12–15% and by ≥ 200 mL; both changes are required by RCP 203.
  • Example: FEV1 3.0 L → 3.3 L; (3.3 − 3.0) ÷ 3.0 × 100 = 10% → not reversible.
Watch outRCP 103 says ≥ 12% and/or ≥ 200 mL; give RCP 203 ≥ 12–15% and ≥ 200 mL.
SourceLssn 3a S76 · 103 Asthma S23 · Hess Ch 8

4Lung-Volume Measurement (FRC → RV, TLC)

  • N₂ washout: breathe 100% O₂; end at exhaled N₂ < 1.5% for 3 breaths. Rising N₂ suggests a leak.
  • Helium dilution: closed-circuit rebreathing with ~10% He and CO₂ absorption; leak falsely increases FRC.
  • Body box: Boyle’s law measures all thoracic gas, including trapped gas/bullae; dilution and washout can underestimate FRC in obstruction.
  • FRC formulas: N₂ = exhaled N₂ volume ÷ (FEN2 − FEN2 end); He = system volume × (FHe − FHe end) ÷ FHe end.
  • Interpret: normally FRC = TGV; TGV > FRC means trapped gas. Small FRC gives a steeper body-box slope.
SourceLssn 2b S75 · Lssn 4 S32 · Hess Ch 8

5Bronchoprovocation and the Methacholine Challenge

  • Use: suspected asthma with nondiagnostic spirometry; methacholine directly provokes bronchospasm through cholinergic action.
  • Positive: methacholine FEV1 fall ≥ 20%; report PD20, the dose causing that fall, and give albuterol afterward.
  • Other tests: mannitol ≥ 15% fall from baseline or ≥ 10% between doses; exercise 10% fall in FEV1.
  • Avoid: moderate/severe obstruction; no bronchodilators or exercise on test day. Reschedule 4–6 weeks after chest infection.
Watch outNot in your sources: a PC20 concentration cutoff for methacholine.
Source103 Asthma S23 · 104 Pharm Ch 5 S18 · Hess Ch 8

6Obstructive vs Restrictive Disease

  • Obstruction: FEV1/FVC < 70%; FEV1 falls, RV/RV:TLC rise with air trapping, and TLC may rise with hyperinflation.
  • Restriction: normal/↑ ratio with ↓ FVC; TLC < 80% predicted confirms restriction, while spirometry alone only suggests it.
  • DLCO: low in emphysema/ILD; normal or high in asthma/chronic bronchitis; normal with restriction suggests chest-wall cause.
  • Examples: asthma, COPD and bronchiectasis obstruct; ILD, atelectasis, effusion and Guillain-Barré restrict.

  • Tracheostomy: bypasses obstruction, assists secretion removal and provides long-term ventilation; upper-airway warming, humidification and filtration are lost.

1CVP Catheter

  • CVP: central venous tip near RA measures right-heart filling pressure/RV preload; low suggests hypovolemia, high may suggest overload.
  • Placement: subclavian or IJ → SVC near RA; level transducer at fourth intercostal space, midaxillary line, and read end-expiration.

2PA (Swan-Ganz) Catheter

  • Swan-Ganz: balloon-tipped PA catheter measures CVP, PAP, PCWP, CO and SvO₂; passes SVC → RA → RV → PA.
  • Balloon: at most 1.5 mL; PAP with balloon deflated, PCWP with momentary inflation at end-expiration, then deflate immediately.
  • PCWP: reflects LA pressure/LV preload; > 18 mm Hg suggests cardiogenic pulmonary edema; ARDS usually normal or low.
  • Thermodilution: proximal injection of 10 mL iced/room-temperature fluid; distal thermistor detects temperature change to determine CO.

4Diuretic Classes

  • Loops: most potent, ascending loop of Henle; furosemide is drug of choice for pulmonary edema. Hazards: hypokalemia, alkalosis, depletion and ototoxicity.
  • Thiazides: distal tubule; hydrochlorothiazide for mild HTN/chronic CHF, not acute edema. Can lower K⁺ and raise glucose.
  • K-sparing: distal tubule/collecting duct; spironolactone, eplerenone, triamterene or amiloride; hyperkalemia risk.
  • Osmotic: mannitol for cerebral edema/ARF; may worsen CHF or pulmonary edema by increasing plasma volume.
  • CAI: proximal-tubule acetazolamide treats metabolic alkalosis, glaucoma or altitude sickness; can cause metabolic acidosis.

5The Role of Diuretics

  • Purpose: remove extracellular fluid → lower BP, pulmonary edema and WOB; loop diuretics reduce preload in cardiogenic edema.

  • Hazards: hypovolemia, hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis; diuretic-induced alkalosis → reduce/stop drug and give chloride/potassium.
  • Limits: not primary ARDS therapy; routine sustained use is not recommended in preterm CLD. Mannitol initially raises plasma volume.

C(a-v)O₂

1How to Calculate C(a-v)O₂, With a Worked Example

  • Difference: C(a-v)O₂ = CaO₂ − CvO₂; arterial minus mixed venous content reflects tissue O₂ use.
  • Content: CaO₂ = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.003 × PaO₂); CvO₂ substitutes SvO₂ and PvO₂.
  • Arterial example: Hb 15, SaO₂ 97%, PaO₂ 100 → 15 × 1.34 × 0.97 + 100 × 0.003 = 19.797.
  • Venous example: SvO₂ 75%, PvO₂ 40 → 15 × 1.34 × 0.75 + 40 × 0.003 = 15.195.
  • Resting example: source-rounded contents give 20 − 15 = 5 mL/100 mL; unrounded difference 4.6.
Watch outHess prints dissolved content “0.03,” but its own arithmetic uses 0.3; use factor 0.003 in calculations, as RCP 203 Lssn 3a does.
SourceHess Normal Values · Lssn 3a S15 · Lssn 7 S41 · 105 Weaning/Pharm S14 · Hess Ch 50

2What Widens or Narrows It

  • Widens: increased extraction lowers CvO₂; at unchanged CaO₂ this widens C(a-v)O₂. Exercise, CHF and severe anemia increase extraction.
  • Narrows: markedly increased CO, as in sepsis, reduces extraction and raises CvO₂; critically ill PvO₂ < 35 mm Hg suggests tissue hypoxia.
  • Disease trap: large effusion increases C(a-v)O₂; ARDS, ILD, lung cancer, PE and severe OSA keep it normal in the disease decks.
Watch outSvO₂ ranges differ; give deck 75% (68–77%) rather than Hess 65–75%.

1The Four Masks Side by Side

  • Partial rebreather: variable FiO₂ 0.40–0.70 at 8–15 L/min; reservoir without valves; keep bag at least partly inflated.
  • NRB: variable FiO₂ 0.6–0.8 at 10–15 L/min; one-way valves; bag must not empty by more than half.

2Partial Rebreathing vs Nonrebreathing Mask: The Valve Difference

  • Partial rebreather: reservoir mask without valves mixes source O₂, bag O₂ and some room air through side ports.
  • NRB: adds bag-to-mask and exhalation one-way valves; removing valves converts it to a partial rebreather.
  • Safety: maintain flow so bag never collapses; NRB must not empty by more than half. O₂ failure/stuck valves can cause suffocation.

3Air-Entrainment Mask and Aerosol Mask: How the FiO₂ and Flow Are Set

1V/Q Mismatch vs Shunt (vs Dead Space): The Differences

  • V̇/Q̇ mismatch: ventilation and perfusion both present but unequal; most common cause of hypoxemia, usually improves with O₂.
  • Shunt: perfusion without ventilation, V/Q = 0; least responsive to O₂. Recruit with PEEP/CPAP.
  • Dead space: ventilation without perfusion, V/Q = infinity; PE is the most common board answer.
  • Causes: asthma/COPD commonly low V/Q; atelectasis, pneumonia, ARDS and edema can shunt; PE causes high V/Q, not shunt.
Source105 Principles S16, 19, 21, 22 · 103 Edema/PVD S52 · Hess Ch 50

2Telling Them Apart: the 100% O₂ Test and the Shunt Equation

  • 100% O₂ test: low-V/Q PaO₂ rises strongly, shunt responds poorly; normal adults reach PaO₂ > 600 mm Hg.
  • Equation: Qs/Qt = (CcO₂ − CaO₂) ÷ (CcO₂ − CvO₂); normal RCP 203 shunt < 5%.
  • Worked example: FiO₂ 1.0, PaCO₂ 40 → PAO₂ = 1.0 × (760 − 47) − 40/0.8 = 663 mm Hg.
  • Contents: CcO₂ 22.1, CaO₂ 18.3, CvO₂ 15.2 mL/dL → Qs/Qt = 3.8 ÷ 6.9 = 0.55 = 55%, severe shunt.
Watch outRCP 105 calls ≤ 10% normal; give RCP 203 < 5%. The example slide omits the ÷ 6.9 step.
Source105 Weaning/Pharm S13, 14 · Lssn 3a S35 · 105 Principles S23 · Hess Ch 50

3RCP 203 Bedside Rules: When It's Shunt, and What to Do

  • Significant shunt: generally P/F < 200 or satisfactory oxygenation cannot be maintained on ≥ 50% O₂; treat with CPAP/PEEP.
  • A-a gradient: 5–15 mm Hg on room air normal; 66–300 mm Hg → V/Q mismatch, O₂ up to 60%.
  • Large gradient: > 300 mm Hg → shunting, ventilator with PEEP; proning can re-expand dorsal lung and decrease refractory shunt.
SourceLssn 7 S6, 30 · 105 Weaning/Pharm S13 · 105 Initiation S7

4Good Lung Down — Positioning as a V̇/Q̇ Intervention

  • Good lung down: dependent lung receives more perfusion; placing healthy lung downward improves ventilation–perfusion matching and oxygenation.
  • Example: left-lung pneumonia → right lateral decubitus, with right healthy lung down; identify the diseased side carefully.
  • Exceptions: abscess, massive hemoptysis or infant unilateral PIE → good lung up to prevent contamination or overdistention.
SourceLssn 8b S29

1How to Tell Any Two Modes Apart

  • Compare modes: identify controlled variable, trigger/cycle and breath sequence; control either volume or pressure, while the other varies.
  • Sequence: CMV all mandatory; IMV mandatory plus spontaneous; CSV all spontaneous. Patient triggering alone does not make a breath spontaneous.
  • Deck ranking: VC first choice, PC second, PS assists spontaneous breaths; know VC-A/C, VC-SIMV, PC-A/C and PC-SIMV.

2VC vs PC: the Difference and When to Use Each

  • VC: guarantees VT, pressure varies; constant square flow can mismatch patient demand, and falling compliance raises pressure.
  • PC: guarantees pressure, VT/flow vary with mechanics and effort; pressure-limited, time-cycled with descending flow.
  • Example: PIP = PC + PEEP; 20 + 10 = 30 cm H₂O. ARDS lowers VT in PC but raises pressure in VC.
  • Choose: VC for constant VT/PaCO₂; consider PC when VC needs Pplat > 30, with pressure limit ≤ 30.

3CMV (and How It Differs from Assist/Control)

  • Chang CMV: time-triggered preset VT/rate, no patient triggering; sedation ± paralysis needed for complete rest, seizures or crushed chest.
  • A/C: time- or patient-triggered, every breath fully supported; set 12 and patient triggers 24 → 24 full breaths.
  • Hazards: rapid A/C triggering may cause respiratory alkalosis/auto-PEEP; control CMV disconnection risks apnea/hypoxia. Low-volume/pressure alarms matter.
Watch outCMV means control-only in Chang, but A/C in Hess/Principles; answer from whether the patient is allowed to trigger.

4IMV vs SIMV

  • IMV: fixed-interval mandatory breaths plus spontaneous breathing; unsynchronized breath stacking can cause auto-PEEP, hyperinflation and barotrauma.
  • SIMV: synchronization window matches mandatory breath to effort; PS applies only to spontaneous breaths between mandatory ones.
  • Example: set 10, patient triggers 20 → A/C 20 mandatory; SIMV 10 mandatory + 10 spontaneous.
  • Support: used for partial support; inadequate spontaneous assistance risks fatigue. Mandatory SIMV rate cannot exceed the set rate.

5Pressure Support (the Spontaneous Breath in SIMV)

  • PSV: patient-triggered, pressure-limited, flow-cycled; supports spontaneous breaths only, so an apneic patient cannot use it alone.
  • VT: varies with effort, compliance and resistance; cycling often occurs around 25% of peak inspiratory flow.
  • Indications: low spontaneous VT, high spontaneous rate and increased WOB; target spontaneous rate < 25/min.
  • Example: 5′10″ male, IBW = 50 + 2.3 × 10 = 73 kg; slide 8–10 mL/kg IBW → 584–730 mL.

6MMV (Mandatory/Minimum Minute Ventilation)

  • MMV: guarantees minimum minute ventilation; spontaneous V̇E below target → mandatory breaths fill the deficit.

7PRVC (Pressure-Regulated Volume Control)

13Patient–Ventilator Asynchrony: What It Is and Trigger Asynchrony

14Flow and Cycle Asynchrony

  • Flow starvation: concave/scalloped inspiratory pressure trace, tachypnea and retractions; increase flow/change pattern or switch VC to PC/PS.
  • Premature cycling: I-time too short → persistent effort, double triggering and breath stacking; late pressure dip means more volume wanted.
  • Delayed cycling: active exhalation and end-inspiratory spike; decrease PS, raise termination-flow setting or switch to PC.
  • Air trapping: expiratory flow fails to return to baseline; patient-triggered I-time should remain ≤ 1.5 s.

16Ventilator Variables: Pressure, Volume, Flow and Time

  • Variables: volume in mL/L, pressure in cm H₂O, flow in L/min and time in seconds; normal adult inspiratory flow 40–60 L/min.
  • Timing: TI + TE = 60/f; I:E = TI:TE. Trigger starts, limit constrains, cycle ends, baseline is PEEP.
  • Example: VT = flow × TI; 60 L/min = 1 L/s × 1 s = 1 L.
  • Control: VC presets volume/flow; PC presets pressure. Whitaker describes VC breaths as flow-limited and time-cycled.

17Independent vs Dependent Variables

  • Independent: what the ventilator guarantees; VC guarantees volume/flow and pressure depends on mechanics, while PC guarantees pressure.
  • VC example: ARDS develops at VT 500/PIP 20 → VT still 500, PIP 35; reduced compliance raises PIP and Pplat.
  • PC example: pressure 20 cm H₂O/VT 500 → pressure still 20, VT 300; higher driving pressure or longer flowing TI increases VT.
Source105 Principles S75, 79 · Lssn 3a S52, 53, 54, 55 · Hess Ch 20

18Trigger: What Starts the Breath

  • Trigger: starts inspiration; time triggering follows set rate, while patient triggering senses pressure/flow change or diaphragm electrical activity.
  • Examples: rate 12 → every 5 s; base flow 10 L/min with sensitivity 3 → triggers at 7 L/min.
  • Sensitivity: closer to zero is more sensitive; too insensitive → missed triggers/fatigue, too sensitive → autocycling.
  • Auto-PEEP: raises the effective triggering load; significant auto-PEEP can prevent either pressure or flow triggering.
Source105 Modes S5, 14, 16 · 105 Management S33 · Lssn 7 S79 · Hess Ch 19, 20

19Cycle: What Ends the Breath

  • Cycle: ends inspiration; deck VC is volume-cycled, PC time-cycled and PSV flow-cycled.
  • PSV/VS: patient-triggered, pressure-limited and flow-cycled; inspiration ends when flow reaches the termination threshold.
  • PCV: time- or patient-triggered, pressure-limited and time-cycled; high-pressure alarm can pressure-cycle a VC breath.
Source105 Modes S5, 22, 30 · 202 Lssn 4 S19 · 105 Initiation S11 · 105 Waveforms S31 · Hess Ch 19

20PEEP: What It Is, What It Does, How Much

  • PEEP: elevated end-expiratory baseline pressure; CPAP is PEEP during fully spontaneous breathing, and EPAP is PEEP.
  • Effect: recruits alveoli, raises FRC, reduces shunt and improves oxygenation; indicated for refractory hypoxemia/low compliance.
  • Initial: 5 cm H₂O, NBRC range 2–6; optimal PEEP gives benefit with least harm and preserves BP.
  • Optimize: highest static compliance in VC or greatest exhaled VT at constant ∆P in PC; maintain SpO₂ ≥ 88–90%.

21PEEP Complications and the Dangerous Level

  • Exam risk threshold: PEEP > 10 cmH₂O, P̄aw > 30 cmH₂O or PIP > 50 cmH₂O increases alveolar-rupture/barotrauma risk.
  • Main downside: reduced venous return → reduced CO → hypotension; excess PEEP can also increase dead space.
  • Other effects: pneumothorax, possible ↑ ICP, increased PAP/CVP and reduced hepatic perfusion.

22Auto-PEEP

  • Auto-PEEP: incomplete exhalation causes intrinsic pressure/dynamic hyperinflation; high rate, obstruction, long I-time or breath stacking increase risk.
  • Find: expiratory flow fails to reach zero before next inspiration; missed triggering and hypotension are clues.
  • Measure: relaxed patient, end-expiratory pause; pause pressure − set PEEP = 12 − 5 = 7 cm H₂O.
  • Fix: ↓ RR/VT, ↑ VC inspiratory flow, ↓ PC TI and treat obstruction; allow more exhalation time.
Watch outFor spontaneously breathing patients, give RCP 203 applied PEEP up to 80–90% of auto-PEEP; Hess says 75% to 85%.

23High Pressures and Their Causes

  • PIP alone rises: increased resistance—secretions, bronchospasm, kinked/bitten ETT, airway edema, circuit water or increased inspiratory flow.
  • PIP/Pplat rise: reduced compliance—ARDS, edema, pneumonia, atelectasis, pneumothorax, effusion, mainstem intubation or ascites.
  • Alarm: Ch 11 initially 35 cm H₂O, then 10 above observed PIP; reaching limit ends the breath.
  • Safety: expiratory circuit obstruction risks rebreathing/asphyxia/barotrauma; PIP < 50 and Pplat < 30 are the chosen ceilings.
Watch outChang permits Pplat < 35 and high alarm 10 to 15 above PIP; give < 30 and Ch 11 alarm 10 above observed PIP.

24Low Pressures and Their Causes

  • Low pressure: think disconnection, circuit leak, cuff leak or ventilator malfunction; examine patient before ventilator and identify the cause.
  • Graphics: repeated expired VT below set VT with low PIP suggests leakage; volume–time curve failing to return to baseline supports a leak.
  • Alarm: Ch 11 low inspiratory pressure = 10 cm H₂O below observed PIP or PC set pressure.
  • Other clues: low PEEP often means circuit/cuff leak; improving mechanics can also lower PIP without equipment failure.
  • VT alarms: Ch 11 uses 150 mL above and below set VT; low PEEP usually indicates circuit/cuff leak.
Watch outGive Ch 11 low-pressure alarm 10 cm H₂O below PIP and VT alarms 150 mL above/below set VT; RCP 105 uses different margins.

25Ventilator Circuits

  • Circuits: ICU double limb returns expiration to ventilator valve; single limb with valve serves home/transport; NIV leak-port circuits lack a valve.
  • Dead space: wye-to-airway volume, including added HME; neonatal tubing should have low compliance to reduce compressed-volume losses.
  • Example: compressed volume = 3 × (60 − 10) = 150 mL lost; corrected VT = expired VT − compressed volume.
  • Care: change visibly soiled/malfunctioning circuits, not routinely; suspected major problem → resuscitator and PEEP valve ventilation.
Source105 Principles S63 · Lssn 4 S66, 67, 68, 70, 71 · 105 Management S36, 40 · 105 Initiation S14 · Lssn 5a S20 · Hess Ch 19, 20

26Exhalation Valves

  • Exhalation valve: closes during inspiration, opens for exhalation and controls PEEP; ICU versions are internal electromechanical valves.
  • Active valve: permits breathing during mandatory pressure breaths and vents cough pressure in VC+; emergency valve provides a second exit.
  • Obstruction: can cause rebreathing, asphyxia and barotrauma; check NIV exhalation ports, condensate and ribavirin crystallization. Heat expiratory HEPA filters.
SourceLssn 4 S67, 68, 70, 71, 72 · 105 Principles S59, 63 · 202 Lssn 4 S18 · 104 Pharm Ch 13 S33 · Hess Ch 20

27Static and Dynamic Compliance: Calculation and Normal Ranges

  • Static: Cstat = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP), measured without flow; ignore PIP when calculating static compliance.
  • Dynamic: Cdyn = VT ÷ (PIP − PEEP); resistance lowers it below static compliance when airflow is present.
  • Example: VT 320 mL, Pplat 40, PEEP 26 → 320 ÷ 14 ≈ 23 mL/cm H₂O.
  • Same patient: PIP 43 → Cdyn = 320 ÷ 17 ≈ 19; deck intubated-adult Crs normal 40–80 mL/cm H₂O.
Watch outStatic-compliance ranges differ; give RCP 203 intubated adult 40–80 mL/cm H₂O rather than other sources’ 50–100 or 60–100.
Watch outNot in your sources: a normal dynamic-compliance range.

28Minute Ventilation vs Alveolar Ventilation

  • Minute ventilation: V̇E = VT × f; total gas moved each minute, including dead space.
  • Alveolar ventilation: V̇A = f × (VT − V_D); fresh gas reaching alveoli. PaCO₂ varies inversely with V̇A.
  • Example: 125-lb patient, f 15, VT 400 mL → V̇A = 15 × (400 − 125) = 4125 mL/min.
  • Compare: same patient V̇E = 15 × 400 = 6000 mL/min; low VT increases the dead-space fraction.
SourceLssn 2b S15, 17 · 105 Principles S15 · 105 Initiation S12 · 105 Procedures S35 · Hess Ch 50, 20

29Relative Humidity and Rain-Out

  • Humidity: RH = content ÷ capacity at that temperature × 100%; fully saturated 37 °C gas holds 44 mg/L.
  • Artificial airway: bypassed humidification plus dry medical gas → thick secretions/obstruction; heated humidifier target near 100% RH and 37 °C.
  • Rain-out: cooling humidified gas condenses in tubing; condensation threatens circuit patency and may obstruct flow.
  • Manage: heated wires and water traps; drain contaminated condensate away from patient, never into airway or nebulizer reservoir.
Source105 Management S37, 38, 39 · Lssn 4 S47, 48 · Lssn 5a S20, 21 · Hess Ch 54, 15

30Cuff Pressures

  • Cuff target: ETT/trach 20–30 cm H₂O; most hospitals/NBRC hospital use 25 cm H₂O as high limit.
  • Hazards: > 25–30 reduces mucosal perfusion → ischemia/necrosis; < 20 permits aspiration and VAP risk.
  • Measure: at least once per shift and after relevant changes/leaks; attaching manometer loses volume, so measure and adjust.
  • Seal needs > 30: evaluate undersized tube; progressively rising pressure suggests dilation/tracheomalacia or cuff-system failure. Minimal-leak technique is no longer recommended.
  • Device trap: LMA ≤ 60 and King LT ~60 cm H₂O; these are not ETT cuff targets.
Watch outRCP 105 < 25 mm Hg conflicts with the chosen target; give 20–30 cm H₂O. Tracheal perfusion pressure is not cuff pressure.
SourceLssn 2b S57, 58, 59 · Lssn 3a S68 · Lssn 6a S24, 28, 31, 32, 42, 60 · 105 Airway S86 · Hess Ch 17

31The Equation of Motion

  • Equation: Pvent = E × V + R × V̇; elastic load plus resistive load, with elastance inverse to compliance.
  • With muscles: Paw + Pmus = (flow × resistance) + (volume/compliance) + PEEP + auto-PEEP; full support has Pmus = 0.
  • Causes: asthma increases resistive load; ARDS increases elastic load. VC pressure varies, while PC volume/flow vary.
  • Example: Pplat = 320/23 + 26 ≈ 40; resistive pressure 9 × 0.33 ≈ 3 → PIP 43.
Source105 Principles S62, 71, 72, 73, 74 · Hess Ch 19, 20, 51

32I:E Ratio — Computing It, Changing It, and the Flow That Follows

  • Timing: I-time = volume (L) ÷ flow (L/sec); E-time is remaining cycle time; duty cycle = I-time ÷ (I-time + E-time).
  • Worked example: VC A/C, rate 15/min, VT 600 mL, I:E 1:4 → V̇E 9.0 L/min and duty cycle 20%.
  • Flow: 9.0 ÷ 0.20 = 45 L/min; this calculation applies to flow-controlled VC A/C, while PC/PRVC set I-time directly.
  • Air trapping: lower rate or shorten inspiration to move I:E toward 1:4 or 1:5, allowing full exhalation.
  • Permissive hypercapnia: pH below 7.25–7.30 → recommend increased rate or sodium bicarbonate; pH sets the limit.
  • ATC: dynamically compensates artificial-airway resistance during spontaneous breaths; program airway type and size.
SourceLssn 8b S44–48, 50, 51

33The ARDSNet Protocol Step by Step (Ch 14)

  • Start: calculate PBW; VT 8 mL/kg PBW, reduce by 1 mL/kg at intervals ≤ 2 h to 6 mL/kg PBW.
  • Goals: Pplat ≤ 30 cm H₂O, pH 7.30–7.45, PaO₂ 55–80 torr or SpO₂ 88–95%; rate no more than 35/min.
  • Pplat: measure every 4 h and after VT/PEEP change; > 30 → lower VT in 1 mL/kg steps to minimum 4 mL/kg.
  • Low Pplat: < 25 with VT < 6 → raise VT by 1 mL/kg until Pplat > 25 or VT = 6 mL/kg.
  • Acidosis: pH > 7.15 and < 7.30 → raise rate until pH > 7.30 or PaCO₂ < 25, maximum 35.
  • Severe: pH < 7.15 → rate 35; if persistent, raise VT in 1 mL/kg steps until pH > 7.15; consider NaHCO₃.
SourceLssn 9a S16, 17, 18

34Recruitment Maneuvers and Decremental PEEP — the Revised Ch 11 Deck

  • Idea: an RM raises transpulmonary pressure to reopen alveoli; PEEP keeps them open.
  • Now: RMs are not routinely recommended, especially prolonged high-pressure RMs in ARDS; lung-protective ventilation, PEEP and prone positioning come first.
  • Safety: only in hemodynamically stable patients — MAP ≥ 65 mm Hg (contraindicated, or stop, at MAP ≤ 60–65); avoid with hypotension, untreated pneumothorax, air leaks, raised ICP, pulmonary hemorrhage.
  • Example: PEEP 20 + 20 above PEEP → PIP about 40, 1–3 minutes, stop if the patient deteriorates.
  • Success: better oxygenation and compliance — PC: larger VT at the same pressure; VC: lower Pplat at the same VT.
  • Decremental PEEP: step PEEP down and set it slightly above where compliance starts to fall (deck example: 18).
  • Graphics: flow not back to zero = auto-PEEP; volume not back to baseline = air leak.
Watch outThe revised deck defines stable as MAP ≥ 65 (was 60–65) but still lists MAP ≤ 60–65 as the contraindication and stop point; it dropped the PEEP 25 / PIP 45 repeat.

2What Each Pressure Tells You

  • PIP: includes airway resistance and elastic pressure; Pplat uses an end-inspiratory no-flow hold and reflects lung/chest-wall compliance.
  • Resistance: PIP − Pplat is transairway pressure; depends on airway resistance and inspiratory flow.
  • Elastic driving pressure: Pplat − PEEP; distinguish this from the PC pressure gradient used to adjust VT.

3Lung Condition When PIP and Pplat Rise or Fall

  • PIP only rises: increased airway resistance—bronchospasm, secretions, airway edema, circuit water or obstructed ETT.
  • Both rise: decreased compliance—ARDS, edema, fibrosis, atelectasis, pneumothorax or effusion; think stiff lungs.
  • Repeated low PIP: suspect circuit leak; falling pressures with clinical improvement may instead reflect better mechanics.
  • PC: fixed pressure means worsening compliance lowers VT; resistance lowers VT only if flow continues to end-inspiration.
Source105 Principles S2, 13 · 105 Waveforms S9, 53 · Lssn 3a S52, 53, 54, 55 · Hess Ch 32

4PEEP Too Low vs Too High

  • Too little PEEP: alveolar collapse → shunt and hypoxemia; ARDS may need more PEEP to maintain recruitment.
  • Optimal: keeps SpO₂ ≥ 88–90% without lowering BP; too much overdistends alveoli and lowers CO.
  • Risk: PEEP > 10 cm H₂O increases barotrauma risk; this is a risk threshold rather than an absolute ceiling.
Watch outChang flags > 10, while RCP 203 uses higher optimal-PEEP examples; Hess limits PEEP if Pplat > 30 or driving pressure > 15.

5Effects on the Body When Pressures Rise: Venous Return and UOP

  • Hemodynamics: PPV/PEEP increases intrathoracic pressure → reduced venous return, preload and CO → hypotension.
  • Kidneys: reduced CO/perfusion plus hormonal changes lower urine output and retain sodium/water; oliguria < 20 mL/hr.
  • CVP: may rise while venous return falls, so high CVP does not necessarily mean high effective preload.
  • Other: high pressures may raise ICP; kidneys are more sensitive than liver, while reduced LV afterload may benefit left-heart failure.
Source105 Principles S31, 37, 38, 39, 41 · 105 Management S46 · 105 Modes S9 · Lssn 1 S35, 36 · Lssn 7 S30, 84 · Hess Ch 20

6Auto-PEEP: What It Is and How to Find It

  • Auto-PEEP: trapped end-expiratory pressure from incomplete exhalation, common in COPD/asthma; high rates, large VT and long I-time worsen it.
  • Detect: expiratory flow fails to reach zero before the next breath; causes trigger difficulty, hyperinflation, hypotension and barotrauma.
  • Measure: relaxed patient with end-expiratory pause; auto-PEEP = pause pressure − set PEEP = 12 − 5 = 7 cm H₂O.
SourceLssn 2b S41, 42 · Lssn 3a S57 · Lssn 7 S72 · Hess Ch 20, 51

7How to Resolve Auto-PEEP

  • Time: decrease RR; in VC increase flow or lower VT, in PC shorten TI; adjust I:E to extend expiration.
  • Obstruction: bronchodilators, steroids and suctioning help restore airflow; treat the cause as well as ventilator timing.
  • Applied PEEP: up to 80–90% of measured auto-PEEP only in spontaneously breathing patients to reduce triggering effort.
Watch outHess says 75% to 85%; give RCP 203 80–90% for spontaneously breathing patients.

11Bioavailability

  • First pass: liver metabolism reduces oral activity; higher doses or alternative routes may be needed. Food and lipid solubility affect absorption.
  • L/T ratio: lung dose ÷ (lung dose + GI dose); mouth rinsing, reservoir, instruction and breath hold improve delivery efficiency.

1The Pressure-Volume (P-V) Loop: How to Read It

  • P-V loop: pressure x-axis, volume y-axis; inspiratory upswing right, expiratory return left; slope ΔV/ΔP reflects compliance.
  • Resistance: airway-opening minus alveolar pressure is PTA = flow × resistance; dotted inner line represents alveolar pressure.
  • Breaths: spontaneous loop oval/symmetric, controlled loop sharply angled, assisted loop starts with small trigger effort then machine assistance.

2Compliance Changes on the P-V Loop

  • Low compliance: flatter loop shifted right toward pressure axis; increased PIP/alveolar pressure with unchanged transairway pressure.
  • High compliance: steeper loop shifted left toward volume axis; emphysema stretches easily despite poor recoil.
  • Improvement: recruitment/surfactant makes the curve more vertical; in PC, worsening compliance also lowers delivered VT.

3Overdistension and the Inflection Points

1Step 1: the Air-to-O₂ Ratio (Magic Box or Formula)

  • Magic Box: air parts = 100 − FiO₂; O₂ parts = FiO₂ − 20. Hess formula instead uses 21 for room air.
  • Example: 60% → 40 : 40 = 1:1; 40% → 60 : 20 = 3:1.
  • Table: 0.24 → 25:1; 0.28 → 10:1; 0.30 → 8:1; 0.35 → 5:1; 0.40 → 3:1.
  • Higher FiO₂: 0.50 → 1.7:1; 0.60 → 1:1; 0.70 → 0.6:1; 0.80 → 0.3:1.
Watch outDeck box uses 20, Hess formula 21; low-FiO₂ results differ. Memorize the supplied Hess table; 40% and 60% deck examples agree.
SourceLssn 4 S14–16 · Hess Ch 14

2Step 2: Total Flow = O₂ Flow × Total Ratio Parts

  • Total flow: O₂ input flow × total ratio parts, adding air parts + 1 O₂ part.
  • Example: 40% at 10 L/min, ratio 3:1 → 10 × 4 = 40 L/min; 60% at 10 L/min → 20 L/min.
  • Changing input: 40% at 5 L/min → 5 × 4 = 20 L/min; FiO₂ stays the same while total flow changes.
  • Entrainment: higher FiO₂ means lower total flow; blocking ports raises FiO₂ and lowers flow.
SourceLssn 4 S12–16 · Hess Ch 14

3Matching Total Flow to the Patient

  • High-flow requirement: total flow meets/exceeds patient inspiratory demand; deck target at least 40 L/min, distress may need 60 L/min or more.
  • Too little: inspiratory mist disappears, room air dilutes gas and delivered FiO₂ falls; system becomes variable low-flow.
  • Example: 40% uses 4 parts, so 40 L/min ÷ 4 = 10 L/min O₂; 60% needs 20 L/min O₂.
  • Blender: for FiO₂ > 0.60 at > 40 L/min, use a blending system.
SourceLssn 4 S10, 13, 15, 18 · Hess Ch 14
