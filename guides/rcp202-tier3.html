1Closed-ended vs Open-ended Questions

Closed-ended questions produce a short, specific answer — usually yes/no or a single piece of data — and the slides say they "give limited insight" but deliver specific information fast. Use them when you need an answer quickly, especially with a patient in respiratory distress: "Are you short of breath right now?", "Do you use oxygen at home?", "How many liters do you use?" Open-ended questions are thought-provoking and give you broader information — they let the patient tell their story and often uncover things you never would have thought to ask: "Tell me what happens when you become short of breath," "Tell me about your breathing today." Both types are appropriate; the RT's skill is knowing when to use each.

The slides give you an actual interview strategy: start broad → then narrow down ("Tell me about your cough" → "When did it start?" → "Is it productive?" → "What color is the sputum?"). Two question types to avoid: leading questions ("You're feeling better after that treatment, aren't you?" — nudges the patient toward the answer you want and produces false or slanted information) and compound questions (asking several things at once, so a "yes" tells you nothing).

The full questioning-technique list on the slide is: closed-ended, open-ended, clarification, leading (avoid), compound, facilitation, respectfully identify inconsistencies, silence/pause, and support and reassurance — with the summary sequence Open-ended → Closed-ended → Clarify → Silence/Facilitation → Summarize/Confirm.

SourceLssn 1a — Communication & Collaboration, slides 5, 28, 33

2The 5 Levels of Communication

The levels count down from most superficial to deepest (Table 60–1). Level 5 – Cliché conversation: basic social interaction, little meaningful information ("How are you today?" "Fine.") — not useless, it is often how rapport begins. Level 4 – Reporting facts: exchanging objective information ("I've been coughing for three days," "I'm on 2 liters at home") — where most routine healthcare communication happens.

Level 3 – Personal ideas or judgments: the patient shares what they think or believe ("I don't think these treatments are helping") — explore it instead of correcting it. Level 2 – Feelings and emotions: the patient trusts you enough to say how they actually feel ("I'm scared I'll end up on the ventilator again"). Level 1 – Peak communication: the highest, intimate level, reserved for a select few — close personal relationships, not the RT–patient relationship.

The exam point: the RT must move beyond superficial communication (Levels 4–5) and build enough trust to reach meaningful therapeutic communication, usually Levels 2–3 — that is the therapeutic "sweet spot." Important boundary: the patient may communicate at Level 2, but the RT does not reciprocate at Level 2 — patient self-disclosure gets a therapeutic response, not the RT's own personal fears.

The overall goal is relationships built on honesty, trust, and respect, and the chain to remember is Rapport → Trust → Cooperation.

SourceLssn 1a — Communication & Collaboration, slides 11, 12, 13

3The 3 V's of Communication

Verbal = 7% (the words we use). Vocal = 38% (tone, volume, pitch, rate). Visual = 55% (facial expressions, gestures, posture, body language). Therefore 93% of communication is nonverbal. When verbal and nonverbal messages conflict, people rely more heavily on the vocal and visual cues, especially when interpreting feelings and attitudes — so Verbal + Vocal + Visual must be congruent. Classic RT failure example: telling an anxious patient "Take your time, I'm here with you" while looking at your watch, standing by the door, and sighing — the words say one thing, the vocal and visual say hurry up.

Know the caveat the instructor stresses: these percentages come from Albert Mehrabian's 1960s research on communicating feelings and attitudes when verbal and nonverbal signals are inconsistent; Mehrabian himself said the formula does not apply to communication generally (you obviously cannot get 93% of "Change the PEEP to 10 cmH₂O" from a face and tone). Memorize 7 / 38 / 55 for the exam, but understand it is an overgeneralization.

SourceLssn 1a — Communication & Collaboration, slide 27

4Social, Personal, and Intimate Space (Proxemics)

Proxemics is the use and interpretation of space — how physical distance affects interpersonal communication. The four zones as stated in the slides: Intimate space = 1 inch to 18 inches (0–1.5 feet), normally reserved for very close relationships. Personal space = 1.5 to 4 feet, the comfortable distance for one-on-one conversation, patient interviews, and patient education. Social space = 4 to 12 feet, appropriate for groups, coworkers, meetings, and less personal conversations.

Public space = 12 to 25 feet (12+), lectures and presentations. The RT connection: we enter intimate space constantly — suctioning, auscultating breath sounds, placing a BiPAP mask, intubating/assisting with an airway, drawing an ABG, trach care — so the communication skill is what you do before you enter it: explain and ask permission ("I'm going to listen to your lungs now, is that okay?" / "I'm going to adjust your mask — you'll feel my hands on your face for a moment").

The instructor's qualification: these are conceptual zones, not rigid measurements, and preferences vary with culture, relationship, situation, and patient condition. Note the slide deck lists intimate space two ways — as 0–1.5 feet and as 1"–18" — which are the same distance.

SourceLssn 1a — Communication & Collaboration, slides 18, 19, 22

1Body Surface Area (BSA) — Rule of Nines

Caution

The Wallace Rule of Nines is the quick bedside method for estimating the percent of total body surface area (%TBSA) burned in an adult, and everything on the body is divided into multiples of 9% (plus 1% for the perineum) so the whole body adds to 100%. A major burn is defined as >20% TBSA. The single most important reason to get %TBSA right is fluid: %TBSA + patient weight determines the initial fluid resuscitation requirement, and the deck's example resuscitation rate is 4 mL/kg/%TBSA for adults, with half given in the first 8 hours post-burn.

The deck also gives the shortcut that the patient's palm (including fingers) ≈ 1% TBSA for estimating small or scattered burns. ⚠ Hess disagrees on this one. Hess writes that the palmar surface of the hand — without the fingers — is approximately 1% of the body surface, across all age groups. Deck = palm with fingers; Hess = palm without. Both are taught as "the palm is 1%," so answer the deck's version on a 202 exam, but know that the textbook draws the boundary differently.

Hess prints a finer subdivision of the same 100%. Hess Box 42-1 states the Wallace Rule of Nines as: head and neck roughly 9%; the anterior and posterior chest 9% each; the anterior and posterior abdomen (including buttocks) 9% each; each upper extremity 9%; each thigh 9%; each leg and foot 9%; and the genitals 1%. That is the same total as the deck's version but split more finely — the deck's "anterior trunk 18%" is Hess's chest 9% + abdomen 9%, and the deck's "each leg 18%" is Hess's thigh 9% + leg-and-foot 9%.

Either grouping gives the same answer, so use whichever the question's wording fits. Hess also ranks the methods: the Lund-Browder chart is "the preferred method used to determine the extent of a burn injury" because it is age-specific and accounts for changes in body proportions — the Rule of Nines is expressly "a rough method of estimation."

The Rule of Nines is an adult estimate only — in children the head is proportionally larger and the legs smaller, so the Lund-Browder chart (age-adjusted, more precise) is used instead; the deck does not give the individual pediatric Lund-Browder percentages, only that the chart adjusts for age. Worked example from the deck: entire anterior trunk + entire right arm = 18% + 9% = 27% TBSA.

2Degrees of Burns

Burns are classified by depth: superficial → partial-thickness → full-thickness → deeper tissue injury. First degree = epidermis only (red, dry, painful, no blisters; think sunburn), heals in about 7 days. Second degree = epidermis + part of the dermis (partial-thickness) and splits into superficial partial-thickness (epidermis + superficial dermis, blisters, very painful, heals 10–21 days with no scar) and deep partial-thickness (epidermis + deep dermis, sweat glands and hair follicles; more nerve endings destroyed so sensation is decreased/variable; heals 3–8 weeks with permanent scar).

Third degree = entire epidermis and dermis (full-thickness) — charred, pale, leathery, little or no pain because sensory nerve endings are destroyed; heals over months with severe scarring and requires skin grafts. Fourth degree extends beyond the skin into fat, muscle, tendon, and/or bone — months, severe scarring, multiple grafts necessary. The classic test point: a 2nd-degree burn can hurt more than a 3rd-degree burn because the nerve endings are still intact in partial-thickness injury (though the partial-thickness skin surrounding a full-thickness burn is still very painful, so "no pain at all" is misleading).

The deck says do not memorize the exact healing times — memorize the concept: superficial heals fast, deeper heals slowly with scarring, full-thickness needs surgery.

3Static and Dynamic Lung Compliance

Compliance is how easily the lungs expand. The deck gives these numbers on its pulmonary-mechanics reference slide: Lung compliance (the deck's own wording: "how easily the lungs expand") = 50–100 mL/cm H₂O and Static compliance = 70–100 mL/cm H₂O. The deck does not give the compliance formulas — it lists ranges only, so you must supply Cstatic = VT ÷ (Plateau pressure − PEEP) and Cdynamic = VT ÷ (PIP − PEEP) from your textbook/lecture, not from these slides.

What the deck does tell you is why compliance matters in burns: inhalation injury and major burn produce inflammation → ↑ alveolar-capillary permeability → pulmonary edema → ↓ compliance and impaired oxygenation, an ARDS-like picture; decreased compliance is also listed as a cause of the patient's tachypnea; and a circumferential chest burn with rigid eschar plus underlying edema mechanically restricts chest expansion, which is why a chest escharotomy may be needed to let the thorax expand during breathing or mechanical ventilation.

Falling compliance therefore drives the ventilator plan: low VT ≈ 6 mL/kg PBW, plateau pressure ≤ 30 cm H₂O, appropriate PEEP, to avoid VILI. The same slide also gives two other memorizable numbers: NIF/MIP normal < −60 cm H₂O (< −40 for COPD patients, < −20 for myasthenia gravis patients), and P/F ratio: >380 = normal, <300 = mild ARDS/ALI, <200 = ARDS, <150 = consider proning.

What Hess — the RCP 202 textbook — actually prints. Hess never uses the words "static compliance" or "dynamic compliance"; it says respiratory system compliance (CRS). Normal lung compliance is 200 mL/cm H₂O and normal chest wall compliance is 200 mL/cm H₂O; because the two add as reciprocals (1/CRS = 1/CL + 1/CCW), normal CRS = 100 mL/cm H₂O. For ventilated patients Hess states: "Acceptable CRS is 50 to 100 mL/cm H₂O." It calculates that as CRS = VT ÷ (Pplat − PEEP) — the static measurement.

So the deck's "lung compliance 50–100" lines up with Hess's CRS-in-ventilated-patients figure, not with Hess's lung compliance of 200 — meaning that row is a static number despite its label. Hess also sets a weaning floor: CRS should be at least 50 mL/cm H₂O.

Caution

⚠ There is no published normal range for dynamic compliance — in any of your sources. Hess uses the phrase once in 4,441 pages (in a figure description) and never gives a range. Heuer defines Cdyn = VT ÷ (PIP − PEEP), flags it as NBRC-examinable, and says only that Cdyn is always less than CRS, the gap being airway resistance — no number.

No deck gives one either. The reason is real, not an oversight: Cdyn is measured while gas is flowing, so it bundles compliance with resistance and moves with inspiratory flow, tube size and secretions. It is a comparative measurement, not a reference value. If a question asks for a normal compliance range, it wants the static one.

1Access Points — Where the Cannulas Go

VV, dual-site (two vessels). A drainage cannula into a femoral vein and a return cannula in either the right internal jugular vein or the contralateral femoral vein.

VV, single-site (one vessel, dual-lumen catheter)

The dual-lumen catheter is usually placed in the right internal jugular vein, though it can also be placed in the left subclavian vein. It drains from the superior and inferior vena cavae and returns to the right atrium. Hess lists its advantages plainly: single-site cannulation reduces vessel trauma and infection risk, and potentially allows increased wakefulness and mobility — the reason awake, mobile ECMO is possible at all.

Two caveats: it should ideally be placed under direct visualisation with fluoroscopy or transoesophageal echocardiography, and it cannot achieve the higher blood flows that dual-site cannulation can.

VA

Venous drainage from the femoral vein, return to the femoral artery. Open-chest cannulation is also used, with configurations depending on the patient's pathology and surgical history. Neonates and younger children may need VA either for cardiac support or because the internal jugular vein is too small to take a double-lumen catheter.

Recirculation — the complication that comes from the access itself. Hess describes it as "a common problem": oxygenated blood going back through the circuit rather than to the patient for oxygen extraction. Causes: improper cannula positioning, hypovolaemia, excess flows, or decreased cardiac output. How you spot it: the blood in the venous drainage tubing turns brighter red — closer in colour to the oxygenated return tubing — or, on devices with continuous monitoring, SvO₂ rises. It is significant when SpO₂ decreases. The fix: decrease the ECMO flow and confirm proper cannula position.

SourceHess Ch 23 — Figure 23-3 cannulation strategies and ECMO Management (added 2026-09-20).

2VV versus VA — One Difference, Many Consequences

Hess's Key Points state it in two sentences: "VV ECMO provides support for respiratory failure. VA ECMO provides support for cardiac and cardiopulmonary failure." The circuit is otherwise the same — where the blood is returned is the entire difference.

VV is preferred for respiratory failure, Hess says, "both for simplicity and for its more favourable safety profile."

VA is a salvage technique in adult respiratory failure, and Hess names exactly why: risks of coronary, brain, and upper-body ischaemia; increased risk of stroke; and an increased incidence of vascular complications. Returning oxygenated blood to the femoral artery means it must travel against the heart's own output, so the upper body — heart and brain first — can be perfused by the patient's own poorly oxygenated blood while the lower body gets the well-oxygenated circuit blood.

The deck’s one-line version, which is the cleanest thing to memoriseVV supports ONLY respiratory failure — it supports oxygenation and CO₂ removal and “requires an adequate cardiac function.” VA supports BOTH cardiac and respiratory failure — it provides oxygenation, CO₂ removal, and adequate perfusion. That “requires adequate cardiac function” clause is the deck’s own gloss rather than Hess’s wording, and it is the fastest route through a VV-versus-VA question: if the heart cannot do its job, VV is not an option.
SourceHess Ch 23 — ECMO Basics and Key Points (added 2026-09-20).

3Inclusion Criteria, Tracking, and Outcomes

The gate

ECMO should be reserved for patients with a reversible process, or, in end-stage lung disease, for patients who can be successfully bridged to lung transplant.

The most common VV indication is severe ARDS. Hess also lists status asthmaticus, pulmonary embolism, airway compression from mediastinal masses, air-leak syndromes, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn, and congenital airway anomalies — and notes these often do better, because they may not have the multiorgan dysfunction frequently present with ARDS-associated hypoxaemic respiratory failure.

Tracking — what ELSO is

The Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) is an international organisation that maintains a registry database tracking ECMO volume and outcomes. Growth has been in adults: the number of adult ECMO cases now exceeds neonatal cases for the first time since the technology's introduction in the 1970s.

Outcomes worth quoting

Cumulative survival for all respiratory ECMO patients is 61%, and survival decreases with age. For comparison, adults with severe ARDS managed in an ICU survive at about 54%, and in the two major trials the ECMO arms survived at 56% (CESAR) and 60% (EOLIA). EOLIA (2018) was the largest, most well-designed ECMO trial to date; it failed to show a significant mortality difference on intention-to-treat, but found a significantly higher rate of death or treatment failure in the conventional arm. Centres with greater annual ECMO volume have slightly better outcomes.

Complications and monitoring

"Bleeding is the most common complication related to ECMO." Predictors of mortality: increasing age, multiorgan dysfunction, and prolonged mechanical ventilation prior to ECMO. Transfusion threshold haemoglobin 7 to 8 g/dL. Monitoring involves an arterial line and frequent sampling of haemoglobin, platelets and anticoagulation status, a daily targeted physical examination, a neurologic examination to watch for stroke, inspection of the cannulation sites for bleeding, infection or distal swelling suggesting DVT, and a chest radiograph to confirm cannula position.

Coming off

The decisive question is whether the underlying disease has improved enough for conventional management, weighed against whether the patient is suffering undue morbidity from ECMO itself. Median duration of ECMO support in ARDS is 9 to 15 days; conditions like asthma recover much more quickly. Outcomes for repeated courses of ECMO are poor.

What the deck adds to Hess’s indication list. The deck reproduces Hess’s Respiratory Recap exactly — severe ARDS, status asthmaticus, pulmonary haemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, meconium aspiration syndrome, air-leak syndromes, and end-stage lung disease as a bridge to transplant — and then marks two more with an asterisk: smoke inhalation and severe pneumonia. Neither appears in Hess’s list. An asterisk on your instructor’s slide outweighs an omission in the book.

Two more outcome numbers

ECMO for near-fatal asthma has excellent outcomes — a survival rate of 84% — because these patients tend to be younger, have single-organ dysfunction, and have a readily reversible cause. And survival has improved over time: from 57% in 1993 to 72% in 2007, which Hess attributes to refinements in technique and technological advancement.

2Training Intensity — Target Heart Rate, Workload, Perceived Exertion

Target heart rate — the single most testable number in this section:

The patient's targeted heart rate should be AT LEAST 60% but NO HIGHER THAN 75% of their predicted maximum, where predicted HRmax = 220 − age.

That 60–75% window is what achieves optimal cardiovascular benefit. Worked example: a 70-year-old has a predicted HRmax = 220 − 70 = 150 beats/min, so the training target is 90 to 113 beats/min.

Workload — the MET: the standard work unit for exercise testing is the metabolic equivalent of task (MET). 1 MET = 3.5 mL O₂ consumption/kg of body weight, roughly equal to normal resting O₂ consumption per minute. MET level is varied by changing treadmill speed and incline, and most protocols increase intensity by 1–2 METs at each step-up in workload. Exercise capacity is reduced if the patient cannot attain an exercise level of ≥ 5 METs or if VO₂max < 15 mL/kg/min.

4The 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT)

What it is: the 6MWT measures the distance a patient can walk on a flat surface in 6 minutes — the outcome measure is the 6-minute walk distance (6MWD). It evaluates how the body responds to exertion and is used to determine overall functional capacity, or changes in capacity due to therapy, in patients with moderate to severe heart or lung disease.

What it does NOT do: it does not measure O₂ uptake, and it does not identify the cause of dyspnea or the factors limiting exercise tolerance. If you need that, recommend a comprehensive cardiopulmonary exercise test.

  2. Assemble equipment (lap counter, stopwatch, colored tape, Borg Scale, worksheet).

  4. Have the patient stand and rate baseline dyspnea and exertion on the Borg Scale.
  5. Move to the starting point; set the stopwatch to zero.
  6. Position the patient at the starting line; give the ATS-mandated demonstration and instructions.

  8. Remain at the starting line, watch the patient, and tally completed laps.
  9. After each lap, make sure the patient sees you tallying it.
  10. At the end of each minute, encourage the patient and specify the remaining time.
  11. After exactly 6 minutes, firmly say "Stop," mark the stop point with tape, and have the patient sit down.
  12. Repeat the Borg assessment, reminding the patient of the prior ratings; also ask, "What, if anything, kept you from walking farther?"
  13. Record the end-of-walk SpO₂ and pulse rate.
  14. Record laps, partial-lap distance, and total distance walked (rounded to the nearest meter).
  15. Congratulate the patient on a good effort.

1Functions of Acetylcholine — All Its Sites

Acetylcholine (Ach) is the neurotransmitter of the parasympathetic ("rest-and-digest") branch and one of the two neurotransmitters of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) — the other being norepinephrine. It is inactivated by cholinesterase (acetylcholinesterase, AchE) enzymes and it acts on two receptor families: muscarinic and nicotinic.

2Causes of Increased AND Decreased ICP

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is the pressure inside the skull — brain tissue + blood + cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — measured by an intraventricular catheter or intraparenchymal probe. It is monitored in traumatic brain injury (TBI), intracranial hemorrhage, and cerebral edema.

MEMORIZE THE NUMBERS (Heuer):

ICP valueMeaning
10–15 mm HgNormal in a supine healthy adult
> 20 mm Hg for more than 5–10 minutesAbnormally high / unsafe
> 25 mm Hg for a prolonged periodCritical — associated with poor patient outcomes
> 15–20 mm Hg (TBI section)Cerebral blood flow can fall to a critical level → secondary ischemia
Management goals in TBIKeep ICP < 20 mm Hg and cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) ≥ 60 mm Hg (CPP = MAP − ICP); keep MAP > 75 mm Hg

DECREASED ICP results from:

  • Head elevation
  • Decrease in CSF volume
  • Severe arterial hypotension
  • Hyperventilation / hypocapnia (this is why routine prophylactic hyperventilation lowers ICP — but Heuer says avoid it, because it can cause cerebral ischemia; use it only before suctioning, in confirmed herniation, or as salvage therapy)
  • Therapeutically: osmotic diuresis with mannitol or hypertonic saline, ventricular drainage, sedation, head of bed up 30–40 degrees

INCREASED ICP results from:

  • Increased brain volume — edema or tumor
  • Increased blood volume inside the skull — hemorrhage / hematoma
  • Restricted venous outflow (CVP > ICP)
  • Right heart failure / cor pulmonale
  • High intrathoracic pressure — high PEEP, recruitment maneuvers
  • Severe arterial hypertension
  • Hypoventilation / hypercapnia (CO₂ causes cerebral vasodilation)
  • Hypoxia
  • Hydrocephalus — excessive accumulation of CSF widening the ventricles (deck; acetazolamide listed as an adjunct)

RT procedures that raise ICP — do carefully or avoid: moving/positioning (especially Trendelenburg), flexion or hyperextension of the neck, intubation, suctioning/coughing, ventilator asynchrony, high PEEP / high mean airway pressure, and recruitment maneuvers. Biot breathing (clusters of rapid breaths with apnea) points to medulla damage or ↑ ICP — treat the cause and lower the ICP.

Drug tie-in from the deck

mannitol, an osmotic diuretic, is the ↑ICP drug — "a brain drug, not a lung drug." It is not reabsorbed in the tubule, so it pulls water osmotically and reduces brain swelling. Caution: mannitol first pulls fluid into the bloodstream, so it can transiently worsen pulmonary edema and CHF.

3CNS, PNS, ANS and Neurotransmitters

The body has two major control systems: the nervous system and the endocrine system. The nervous system splits into:

  • Central Nervous System (CNS) = brain + spinal cord — "the command center," sends the signal.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) = all nerves outside the CNS — "the wiring system," carries the signal. It contains three classes of neurons: sensory, somatic, and autonomic.
  • Somatic motor system → skeletal muscle, voluntary (this is where NMBAs work).
  • Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) → organs, involuntary (NMBAs do not affect it). The ANS is the motor-neuron arm of the PNS and is generally considered an efferent system.

The ANS is split for study into sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions, which together control airway smooth muscle, submucosal and surface secretory cells, bronchial epithelium, and pulmonary/bronchial blood vessels. Nerve impulses are electrochemical: an electrical action potential travels the fibre, then converts to chemical transmission at the synapse, releasing a neurotransmitter onto the neuroeffector site. The two PNS neurotransmitters are acetylcholine and norepinephrine. Catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine) are stress hormones/neurotransmitters released from the adrenal medulla; norepinephrine's action is terminated by reuptake, COMT, and MAO.

Sympathetic (adrenergic) receptors

α1 excitatory (vasoconstriction), α2 inhibitory (presynaptic — shuts off further neurotransmitter release), β1 excitatory in the heart, β2 in the lungs (smooth-muscle relaxation = bronchodilation), plus dopaminergic. A third group, the nonadrenergic noncholinergic (NANC) nerves, uses neither transmitter: inhibitory NANC relaxes airway smooth muscle (bronchodilation) via VIP or nitric oxide; excitatory NANC causes bronchoconstriction via substance P on afferent sensory fibres.

4Neurons

Structure (from the NMBA deck): a neuron consists of a cell body, axons, and dendrites. The gap between the axon and the skeletal muscle fibre is the neuromuscular junction, and transmission across it is chemically mediated by acetylcholine.

How a neuron firesDepolarization is the shift of the cell's normally negative internal charge toward positive — a brief event that lets the electrical impulse travel along the cell and jump to the next cell. Repolarization is the return to the negative resting membrane potential. In the muscle: nerve signal → Ach released into the synapse → Ach binds the end-plate receptor ("key fits the lock") → muscle depolarizes → contraction.

Classification by type (PNS): sensory neurons, somatic neurons, and autonomic neurons — the ANS is the motor-neuron arm of that group.

Classification by direction of traffic:

  • Afferent = periphery → spinal cord (sensory, "Arrives")
  • Efferent = brain/spinal cord → neuroeffector site (motor, "Exits")
  • Memory hook from the deck notes: "The Action Affects the situation, leading to an End result, the Effect."
  • The autonomic system is generally considered an efferent system, and excitatory NANC signals travel on afferent sensory fibres using substance P.
Autonomic neurons come as a two-neuron chaina preganglionic fibre to the ganglion, then a postganglionic fibre to the effector — with the relative lengths flipped between the two branches (see the table in item 3).

Clinical: in Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), inflammation destroys the myelin sheaths surrounding spinal-root and peripheral nerves → severe muscle weakness and diminished reflexes.

5Medulla Oblongata and the Respiratory Centres

Hess Chapter 52 (Control of Breathing) covers this in full — here it is.

The medulla oblongata houses two respiratory groups

The dorsal respiratory group (DRG) lies along the lateral walls within the medulla, in a region called the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS). The DRG is the initial processing centre for afferent impulses from the vagus (X) and glossopharyngeal (IX) nerves, and those impulses modify the basic breathing pattern by stimulating inspiration. The DRG is also the place of origin for efferent impulses to the phrenic nerve (which drives the diaphragm) and to the external intercostal motor nerves. Hess's summary line is the one to memorise: the DRG is the primary controller of the depth and rate of inspiration.

The inspiratory ramp

Normal inspiration is a ramp signal that increases in force gradually for about 2 seconds, followed by cessation of inspiration for about 3 seconds — giving progressively stronger inspiratory-muscle contraction and a gradual rise in lung inflation. In stressed breathing or exercise, peripheral receptors and reflexes send afferents that make the ramp steeper, so inflation is more rapid than normal.

The ventral respiratory group (VRG) sits in the medulla in two separate nuclei, anterior and lateral to each DRG, and contains both inspiratory and expiratory neurons: the nucleus ambiguus, whose inspiratory neurons innervate the laryngeal and pharyngeal muscles, and the nucleus retroambigualis, which contains both inspiratory and expiratory neurons.

The pons holds the other two centres

The pneumotaxic centre is in the UPPER pons. It receives vagal afferents that fine-tune respiratory rhythm and it blocks stimulation of the DRG and VRG from the apneustic centre — in other words it controls the stopping point of the inspiratory ramp. A strong signal shortens inspiratory time and raises the rate, producing lower tidal volumes; a weak signal lengthens inspiration and lowers the rate, producing higher tidal volumes.

The apneustic centre is in the LOWER pons and stimulates the inspiratory neurons of the DRG and VRG; normally the pneumotaxic centre inhibits it. If the pneumotaxic centre is destroyed, the apneustic centre takes over and its afferents prevent the inspiratory ramp from shutting off — the result is prolonged inspiration with a shortened expiratory time (apneustic breathing).

How to hold the four straightDRG = depth and rate of inspiration (medulla, NTS) · VRG = inspiratory and expiratory neurons, including the airway muscles (medulla) · pneumotaxic = upper pons, the OFF switch that ends inspiration · apneustic = lower pons, the ON switch that the pneumotaxic centre keeps in check.

6Spinal Cord (and the Phrenic Nerve)

The spinal cord is one of the two components of the CNS (with the brain) and is the structure everything else is defined against: afferent = periphery → spinal cord; efferent = brain/spinal cord → neuroeffector site. Parasympathetic fibres arise from the cranial and sacral portions of the cord (craniosacral outflow); sympathetic fibres leave primarily from the thoracic and lumbar regions (thoracolumbar outflow). CSF flows between the cranium and spine, cushioning brain and cord, delivering nutrients, removing waste, and compensating for changes in intracranial blood volume.

The phrenic nerve

Heuer does not print the classic "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive" mnemonic, but its C3/C3–C5 injury thresholds above are exactly that fact stated clinically. What Heuer does say directly: a condition affecting the phrenic nerve, such as quadriplegia, often shows an elevated diaphragm on chest x-ray, and unilateral phrenic nerve paralysis produces a unilateral reduction in chest movement on the affected side.

Assessment/management pearls

immobilize head, neck, and spine in a neutral position; continuous pulse oximetry and end-tidal CO₂ for all severe SCI; if apnea is present, assume a complete high cervical injury and start manual ventilation immediately; ~1 in 4 SCI patients also has head trauma; measure VC and MIP/NIF once conscious and spontaneously breathing; expect severe bradycardia from vagal stimulation during intubation (preoxygenate, IV atropine or topical lidocaine).

APRV — the full deck detail (gap-fill).

Airway Pressure-Release Ventilation (APRV) is listed as one of the other modes of partial ventilatory support used when a patient has failed an SBT or done poorly on SIMV or PSV alone (alongside Volume Support, Volume-Assured Pressure Support, and Mandatory Minute Ventilation). APRV operates at two pressure levels: a higher airway pressure (e.g., 10 cm H₂O) and a lower release pressure (usually 0 cm H₂O).

The tidal volume (VT) is determined by the pressure gradient between the airway pressure and the release pressure. The breath cycle is inverted relative to conventional ventilation: exhalation occurs during the pressure release, and inhalation occurs when pressure returns to the higher airway pressure. Weaning in APRV is done by decreasing the frequency of pressure release; when the release frequency reaches zero, the patient is essentially on CPAP.

PAV — the full deck detail (gap-fill).

Proportional Assist Ventilation (PAV) is named in the vault only as an example of a closed-loop operating mode of mechanical ventilation. The distinction the decks actually draw is open-loop vs. closed-loop: an open-loop ventilator simply delivers what you set, while a closed-loop ventilator continuously monitors the patient's response (feedback) and automatically adjusts its output to hit a target. PSV is itself given as the model closed-loop example — the clinician sets the pressure plateau (input, constant) and the ventilator uses variable flow (output) that changes with the patient's airway and lung characteristics.

PAV is grouped with PSV in that same closed-loop family, along with MMV, ASV (Adaptive Support Ventilation), volume-assured pressure support, PRVC, and NAVA. The decks' bottom-line test for any of these modes: "Is the ventilator simply delivering what I set, or is it automatically making adjustments? If it's automatically adjusting, you're dealing with a closed-loop system."

The distinctions students actually get wrong:

  • VC vs PC — you can only control ONE. "If you control the Volume, the Pressures go up and down; if you control the Pressure, the Volumes go up and down. You cannot guarantee both simultaneously." Set VT 500 mL, patient develops ARDS → VT still 500 mL, PIP rises from 20 to 35 cmH₂O. Set PC 20 cmH₂O, patient develops ARDS → pressure still 20, VT falls from 500 to 300 mL. Memorize: Volume Control = Set Volume → Watch Pressure. Pressure Control = Set Pressure → Watch Volume. On waveforms: VC pressure climbs like a mountain (square flow); PC pressure jumps and stays flat like a table (decelerating flow).
  • IMV vs SIMV vs CMV. CMV = all breaths mandatory (Hess: A/C belongs here; Chang: also the old no-trigger Control mode). IMV = mandatory + spontaneous mixed, mandatory breaths land on a rigid clock → breath stacking. SIMV = IMV with synchronization — the ventilator waits for the patient's effort inside the sync window, so the mandatory breath is delivered with the effort instead of on top of a half-finished exhalation. The single highest-yield contrast: set rate 10, patient triggers 20 → A/C gives 20 mandatory breaths; SIMV gives 10 mandatory + 10 spontaneous. And in IMV/SIMV the mandatory rate can never exceed the set rate, but it can be lower; in CMV it can exceed it.
  • PRVC vs VC. Plain VC is an open-loop, set-point mode — it delivers what you set and never adapts, so a stiffening lung means a rising pressure you have to notice. PRVC is closed-loop and adaptive — it watches the delivered volume and re-adjusts the pressure every breath to hit the same VT at the lowest pressure ("cruise control": hill gets steeper, car adds gas by itself). Both guarantee volume; only PRVC also protects the pressure.
  • PSV vs Volume Support. PSV guarantees pressure and lets volume vary. Volume Support guarantees volume and lets pressure vary. Both are patient-triggered, pressure-limited, flow-cycled.
  • Patient-triggered ≠ spontaneous. An A/C breath the patient triggered is still mandatory, because the ventilator cycles it off. Classic NBRC trap.
  • Automode (Servo 300A) switches automatically: no spontaneous trigger for 12 sec (adult) → PRVC (time-triggered, preset VT, variable PIP up to the high-pressure limit); two consecutive patient-triggered breaths → Volume Support (patient-triggered, pressure-limited, flow-cycled). Sleeping patient → PRVC; awake patient → Volume Support.

Proportional assist ventilation (PAV) is a positive-feedback control mode that provides ventilatory support in proportion to the neural output of the respiratory centre. Mechanically: the ventilator monitors respiratory drive as the patient's inspiratory flow, integrates flow and volume, measures elastance and resistance, and then calculates the pressure required from the equation of motion. From that pressure and the tidal volume it computes the work of breathing. These calculations run every 5 ms during breath delivery, so the applied pressure and the inspiratory time vary both breath-to-breath and within a breath — which is precisely what pressure support does not do. The ventilator estimates resistance and elastance by applying end-inspiratory and end-expiratory pause manoeuvres of 300 ms every 4 to 10 seconds. 2Phase Variables: Trigger, Limit, Cycle, Baseline

A ventilator breath is broken into phases, and a phase variable is the thing the ventilator measures and uses to start, sustain, and end each phase. A breath = one cycle of positive flow (inspiration) plus negative flow (expiration) on the flow–time curve.

  • Trigger = what STARTS inspiration. Ask "Who started the breath?" Options: patient (pressure trigger or flow trigger) or time (the set rate/backup timer). A patient trigger shows as a small negative deflection below baseline before the breath (breaths A and B on the A/C waveform); a time trigger has no negative deflection (breath C). Sensitivity is the setting that decides how much effort triggering takes: too insensitive (e.g. −5 cmH₂O) → the patient can't trigger and fatigues; too sensitive (e.g. −0.5 cmH₂O or a 0.5 L/min flow trigger) → auto-triggering.
  • Limit = what is held constant DURING inspiration — the value the breath will not exceed while gas is flowing. In VC the limit is volume/flow; in PC and PSV the limit is pressure (that flat plateau at point B on the PSV waveform is the pressure-limited phase).
  • Cycle = what ENDS inspiration and starts exhalation. Options: volume, time, or flow. VC = volume-cycled. PCV = time-cycled. PSV = flow-cycled — inspiration ends when inspiratory flow falls to a preset percentage of peak flow (commonly ~25%), not by time, not by volume, not by pressure.
  • Baseline = the pressure the ventilator returns to at end-expiration, and the reference point everything else is measured from. Normally baseline is 0 cmH₂O; PEEP raises the baseline (5, 8, 10 cmH₂O). Formally: volume is measured above end-expiratory lung volume, pressure is measured above the pressure at the start of inspiration (i.e., PEEP), and flow is measured relative to zero flow. So with PEEP 5 and PIP 25, the pressure actually causing inspiration is 25 − 5 = 20 cmH₂O — the driving pressure / pressure above PEEP. This is also why auto-PEEP hurts triggering: it raises the baseline the patient must drag the airway pressure below.

Trigger + cycle together classify the breath: if the patient controls both the start and the end → spontaneous (CPAP, PSV). If the ventilator controls either one → mandatory (A/C, VC/AC, PC/AC, SIMV mandatory breaths). Memorize the three-part label for the two highest-yield modes: PSV = patient-triggered, pressure-limited, flow-cycled. PCV = time-triggered, pressure-limited, time-cycled.

Independent vs dependent variables: the variable the ventilator guarantees is the independent (control) variable; the one that is left to float is the dependent variable.

  • VC ventilation: volume is the independent variable → pressure is dependent. "I will deliver 500 mL no matter what" → ARDS develops → VT stays 500, PIP rises to 35.
  • PC ventilation: pressure is the independent variable → volume is dependent. "I will deliver 20 cmH₂O no matter what" → ARDS develops → pressure stays 20, VT falls to 300 mL.
  • The three control variables are volume, pressure, and time (time control = HFOV/jet, where flow, volume, and pressure are all dependent on lung mechanics). In the ICU it is essentially always VC or PC.
  • Full classification of any mode = control variable (VC/PC) + breath sequence (CMV/IMV/CSV) + targeting scheme (set-point, dual, bio-variable, servo, adaptive, optimal, intelligent), which produces the five ventilatory patterns: VC-CMV, VC-IMV, PC-CMV, PC-IMV, PC-CSV (there is no VC-CSV, because a truly spontaneous breath can't have an imposed volume and flow pattern). Targeting: A/C and SIMV = set-point; PRVC/Autoflow = adaptive; ASV = intelligent.

3PEEP — Everything About It

Definition

PEEP (Positive End-Expiratory Pressure) increases the end-expiratory / baseline airway pressure. Normally airway pressure returns to 0 cmH₂O at end-expiration; with PEEP it stays elevated (5, 8, 10 cmH₂O). It reinflates collapsed alveoli and supports/maintains alveolar inflation during exhalation. It is not a stand-alone mode — it is applied along with another mode. CPAP is the same pressure applied to a fully spontaneous patient.

What it does: ↓ the pressure threshold for alveolar inflation → ↑ FRC → improves ventilation → ↑ V/Q → improves oxygenation → ↓ WOB. It recruits collapsed alveoli and reduces shunt. PEEP is the #1 ventilator adjustment for improving oxygenation. Why FRC matters: FRC is the gas remaining after a normal exhalation (diaphragm at rest); it maintains continuous gas exchange between breaths, prevents alveolar collapse, buffers against hypoxemia, stabilizes lung volume, and supports V/Q matching.

Indications

intrapulmonary shunting and refractory hypoxemia (consider PEEP when PaO₂/FiO₂ < 200 mmHg), decreased FRC and decreased lung compliance (reduced compliance greatly increases alveolar opening pressure), and auto-PEEP not responding to ventilator setting adjustments. Clinically you raise PEEP when the patient is hypoxemic, shunting, atelectatic, in ARDS, or needs a higher FiO₂ than you want (keep FiO₂ < 60% / PaO₂ < 80 mmHg, < 60 mmHg in COPD, to prevent O₂ toxicity).

Therapeutic range / optimal PEEP

Optimal PEEP is the lowest pressure level that offers the most benefits with the least side effects, titrated using PaO₂, compliance, O₂ saturation, and ventilator waveforms. In the worked example in the deck, 10 cmH₂O is the optimal PEEP, because the next step up (12 cmH₂O) decreased both PaO₂ and compliance. Typical applied values seen throughout the decks are +5 cmH₂O as the routine/starting level.

⚠ Dangerous levels start at: PEEP > 10 cmH₂O— "PEEP > 10 cmH₂O (or P̄aw > 30 cmH₂O; or PIP > 50 cmH₂O) is associated with an increased incidence of alveolar rupture or barotrauma." A second danger threshold in the decks: PEEP > 15 cmH₂O combined with intra-abdominal pressure > 20 cmH₂O adversely affects the cardiovascular, renal, and pulmonary systems (the lungs get squeezed from both directions).

Complications of PEEP:

  • Decreased venous return — pleural pressure becomes less negative, so the pressure gradient to the right atrium falls → ↓ preload → ↓ cardiac output → hypotension. "The major downside of too much PEEP is hypotension from decreased venous return." Garden-hose analogy: positive pressure squeezes the great veins.
  • Barotrauma — lung injury from hyperinflating alveoli past the rupture point: pneumothorax, alveolar rupture (thresholds above).
  • Increased ICP — in patients with normal lungs, PEEP may raise ICP by impeding cerebral venous drainage; matters most in TBI, stroke, neurosurgical patients.
  • Hemodynamics with PEEP specifically: ↑ PAP (expanded alveoli compress pulmonary capillaries → ↑ PVR), ↑ CVP (blood backs up in the systemic veins — high CVP does not always mean good preload), ↓ aortic pressure, ↓ cardiac output.
  • Organ downstream effects: ↓ renal perfusion → ↓ urine output, Na⁺ and water retention; hepatic perfusion falls only when PEEP is added to PPV (liver tolerates plain PPV; kidneys are more sensitive than the liver); ↓ GI perfusion; overdistended alveoli create alveolar dead space.

Weaning: wean PEEP and high FiO₂ together per protocol; PEEP is not dropped abruptly in the shunting patient.

4Auto-PEEP (Intrinsic / Inadvertent / Occult PEEP)

What it is

unintentional PEEP — air trapped in the lungs at end-expiration because the patient never finished exhaling before the next breath started. It raises the baseline pressure and increases the work of breathing, because the patient must now drag airway pressure below that raised baseline to reach the sensitivity threshold and trigger a breath — so triggering becomes much harder. Distal airway pressure can be as high as 15 cmH₂O while the ventilator's proximal manometer still reads zero (or just the set PEEP).

What causes it:

  • Air trapping / significant airway obstruction (COPD, asthma, secretions) and any patient with a history of air trapping
  • High frequency (> 20/min), including excessive pressure support ventilation driving the rate up
  • Insufficient inspiratory flow rate → long I-time
  • Insufficient expiratory time — an I:E ratio that is roughly 1:1 or inverse
  • Breath stacking in IMV (a mandatory breath landing on an unfinished spontaneous exhalation)
  • Excessive VT or rate; tracheal gas insufflation with continuous flow is also listed as a cause
How you detect itthe high PEEP alarm (triggered when actual PEEP exceeds the preset PEEP limit), rising PIP, and hypotension. It can be observed on the pressure–time waveform or measured by occluding the expiratory port just before the next inspiration — for an accurate measurement the patient should be sedated or paralyzed.

How you fix it — give the lungs more time to empty:

  • ↑ flow rate (shortens I-time, lengthens E-time)
  • ↓ VT (shortens I-time)
  • ↓ frequency (f) (lengthens E-time)
  • Keep PS frequency < 20/min
  • ↓ air trapping with bronchodilators in reversible obstruction
  • If setting changes can't correct it, apply therapeutic PEEP to offset the effects of auto-PEEP from small-airway air trapping — therapeutic PEEP is used after you've made the ventilator adjustments, not instead of them.

Classic exam vignette: COPD patient, RR 20, VT 700, flow 40 L/min → rising PIP, auto-PEEP, hypotension. Answers: lower VT, lower RR, increase flow, increase expiratory time.

5High and Low Pressure Alarms and Their Causes

Alarms exist to activate the clinician to intervene, minimize false alarms/alarm fatigue, and make sure important conditions are not missed. They fall into three groups: input power alarms (loss of electricity/battery, loss of air or O₂ pneumatic source < 50 psig), control circuit alarms (settings out of range, failed Short Self Test [SST] → ventilator inoperative), and output alarms (pressure, volume, flow, time, inspired gas, exhaled gas). Never silence an alarm without finding the cause: Alarm → look at the patient → look at the ventilator → determine cause → fix it.

2pH — What It Tells You and the Acidosis/Alkalosis Cutoffs

pH is the value you check FIRST

Normal arterial pH is 7.35 – 7.45. A pH below 7.35 is acidosis (acidemia); a pH above 7.45 is alkalosis (alkalemia). pH is the referee — it tells you the direction of the disturbance, and then PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ tell you which system caused it. The deck's disorder definitions are built on those exact cutoffs: acute alveolar hyperventilation is pH above 7.45 with PaCO₂ below 35; acute ventilatory failure is pH below 7.35 with PaCO₂ above 45. One special-population caveat: for a newborn ABG, slightly acidotic is NORMAL.

SourceRCP 103 ABG Assessments, slides 4, 11, 16, 69

3PaCO₂ — The RESPIRATORY Component

PaCO₂ (normal 35 – 45 mm Hg) is the respiratory component of the ABG and it is controlled by the lungs, which regulate it moment-to-moment. It moves the pH in the OPPOSITE direction: when ventilation drops, CO₂ builds up, combines with water, and forms carbonic acid, which lowers pH (hypoventilation → ↑ PaCO₂ → ↓ pH → respiratory acidosis). When ventilation increases, excess CO₂ is blown off, carbonic acid falls, and pH rises (hyperventilation → ↓ PaCO₂ → ↑ pH → respiratory alkalosis).

PaCO₂ is also the number you use to judge the adequacy of the patient's VENTILATION when you draw the gas. Because respiratory changes happen quickly, an acute PaCO₂ shift arrives before the kidneys have had any time to compensate, so HCO₃⁻ stays near normal initially.

SourceRCP 103 ABG Assessments, slides 4, 6, 22, 50

4HCO₃⁻ — The METABOLIC Component

HCO₃⁻ (normal 22 – 28 mEq/L arterial; NBRC 22 – 26) is the metabolic component, controlled by the kidneys, which provide long-term compensation. It moves the pH in the SAME direction: ↑ HCO₃⁻ → ↑ pH (metabolic alkalosis), ↓ HCO₃⁻ → ↓ pH (metabolic acidosis). Bicarbonate is also the body's main buffer — when fixed acids release H⁺, bicarbonate combines with those H⁺ ions to form carbonic acid, which breaks down into water and CO₂, and in the process HCO₃⁻ gets used up, which is why it falls in metabolic acidosis.

A significantly elevated HCO₃⁻ is the hallmark of CHRONIC compensation and should always raise suspicion for a long-standing respiratory disorder. Renal compensation is slow — 3 to 5 days minimum.

SourceRCP 103 ABG Assessments, slides 4, 18, 22, 43

5PaO₂ — Oxygenation

PaO₂ (normal 80 – 100 mm Hg) reflects the oxygen tension in arterial blood and tells you about oxygenation, not acid-base — but you must always read it alongside the pH and PaCO₂, because severe hypoxemia can create a MIXED disorder. Two clinical thresholds to know: when PaO₂ drops to about 60 mm Hg, the peripheral chemoreceptors are stimulated and signal the brain to increase ventilation (that is why hypoxemia is the most common cause of acute alveolar hyperventilation); and when PaO₂ is severely low, tissues switch to anaerobic metabolism and produce lactic acid, adding a metabolic acidosis on top of whatever else is happening, making both the pH and the HCO₃⁻ lower than expected for that PaCO₂. In COPD, a lower PaO₂ (around 55–60 mm Hg) can be acceptable for hypoxic drive.

SourceRCP 103 ABG Assessments, slides 4, 9, 12, 70

6Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation — What It Is USED FOR

This is the exam question, and the deck answers the "used for" part in one line. A blood gas analyzer directly measures only THREE things — pH, PCO₂, and PO₂. Everything else on the printout — HCO₃⁻, base excess, and SaO₂ — is CALCULATED from those three measured values using equations like the Henderson-Hasselbalch formula. So the answer to "the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is used for…" is: to CALCULATE the bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) — and the other derived values such as base excess — from the directly measured pH and PCO₂, i.e. it mathematically links pH, the respiratory component (PCO₂/carbonic acid), and the metabolic component (HCO₃⁻).

The actual equation itself (pH = pKa + log[HCO₃⁻ ÷ (0.03 × PaCO₂)]) is NOT written out anywhere in this deck — only its purpose is stated. Get the written formula from your textbook or RCP 101 notes if the instructor wants you to reproduce it.

SourceRCP 103 ABG Assessments, slide 64

7Volatile vs. Nonvolatile (Fixed) Acids

Partially covered — read the caution at the end of this item. The deck covers NONVOLATILE (FIXED) acids thoroughly and by name. Fixed acids are acids the LUNGS CANNOT REMOVE — they must be buffered by bicarbonate and excreted by the kidneys. Examples given: lactic acid, keto acids (from fat breakdown, as in DKA), acids from salicylate/aspirin intoxication, and phosphoric and sulfuric acids from the catabolism of foodstuffs (proteins, fats, and carbohydrates broken down during metabolism).

When fixed acids accumulate they release H⁺, which consumes HCO₃⁻, lowering the bicarbonate and RAISING the anion gap. The volatile side is described mechanistically but never with the label "volatile acid": the deck states that CO₂ builds up, combines with water, and forms CARBONIC ACID, which lowers pH, and that bicarbonate buffering forms carbonic acid, which then breaks down into water and carbon dioxide — i.e. carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) is the volatile acid, the one the LUNGS can blow off as CO₂. The exact term "volatile acid" does not appear anywhere in this deck, so confirm the wording your instructor wants; the concept and the example (carbonic acid from CO₂) are both here.

8Hydrogen Ions (H⁺) and Their Relationship to pH

H⁺ is what pH actually measures

— the Sanz pH electrode is a glass electrode SENSITIVE TO HYDROGEN IONS. The relationship is INVERSE: more free H⁺ = lower pH = more acidic; fewer H⁺ = higher pH = more alkalotic. The deck states it directly — "free hydrogen ions would significantly lower the pH," which is why the body must neutralize them. The buffering chain to memorize: acids (like lactic acid) build up → they release H⁺ → H⁺ combines with HCO₃⁻ to form carbonic acid → carbonic acid breaks down into water and carbon dioxide → bicarbonate drops (used up) and the anion gap rises.

The deck's analogy: think of bicarbonate as firefighters — the bigger the fire (extra acid), the more firefighters get used up. The same H⁺ logic drives metabolic alkalosis from the other direction: losing H⁺ (vomiting, gastric suctioning, H⁺ shifting into cells in hypokalemia, renal H⁺ excretion from steroids or aldosterone) raises the pH. Hess supplies both numbers the deck omits. Hess states the relationship explicitly — pH = −log[H⁺] — and works it: a solution containing [H⁺] of 10⁻⁷ mol/L (100 nmol/L) has pH 7. It then anchors the blood value: "The normal pH of the blood is slightly alkaline (pH of 7.4), which corresponds to [H⁺] of 40 nmol/L." Hess also cautions that an ABG pH is interpreted differently from a chemical solution's — blood is normally slightly alkaline, so acidaemia and alkalaemia are judged against 7.35–7.45, not against 7.0. ⚠ Original caution (now resolved for Hess, still true of the deck): the deck never gives a numeric normal [H⁺] and never states pH = –log[H⁺] in numbers — look those up separately if your instructor wants the math.

1Epidemiology — how common is asthma?

In the United States (CDC/NCHS), about 25 million people have asthma — 18.4 million adults (7.6%) and 6.2 million children (8.4%). Asthma accounts for 6.5% of all physician office visits and roughly 1.9 million emergency department visits per year, and causes 3,651 deaths per year. It is more common in boys than girls in childhood, but the pattern flips after puberty so it is more common in women than men in adults.

Worldwide (WHO), about 235 million people have asthma, more than 80% of asthma deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, and asthma is the most common chronic disease among children worldwide.

SourceAsthma PP, slide 5

2Pathophysiology — what physically changes in the airway?

The three headline pathologic changes are reversible bronchoconstriction, airway inflammation, and increased airway hyperresponsiveness. Structurally, bronchial smooth muscle constricts and with repeated stimulation hypertrophies up to 3× normal thickness; the mucosa is infiltrated with eosinophils causing inflammation and edema; Charcot-Leyden crystals appear from eosinophil breakdown in allergic asthma; and goblet cell hyperplasia plus enlarged mucous glands produce thick, tenacious mucus that forms mucus plugs and can cause atelectasis.

Functionally this creates air trapping and alveolar hyperinflation. Between episodes, many of these changes may be absent in mild-to-moderate asthma. In severe/chronic disease, bronchial wall inflammation leads to fibrosis from remodeling.

SourceAsthma PP, slides 7-9

3Pathophysiology — the immunologic mechanism and the early vs. late phase

Extrinsic asthma runs through an IgE-mediated pathway. In the sensitization phase, first allergen exposure stimulates IgE production and those IgE antibodies attach to mast cells in the bronchial walls. On re-exposure, allergen binds the IgE, the mast cell degranulates, and it dumps histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, ECF-A, NCF, and PAF. Those mediators cause bronchoconstriction, mucus hypersecretion, and increased vascular permeability → airway edema.

The early phase starts within minutes and usually resolves within about 1 hour; the late phase starts hours later as eosinophils, basophils, neutrophils, and T-cells migrate in, and lasts longer. Having both is a biphasic response. Th2 lymphocytes release IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 to sustain the inflammation.

SourceAsthma PP, slides 14-15, 17

4Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) and airway remodeling

AHR is an exaggerated bronchoconstrictor response to stimuli — not just allergens, but exercise, cold air, irritants, and infections. It is driven by increased sensitivity to mediators (histamine, leukotrienes) and by overactive airway smooth muscle with increased contractility and growth. AHR is why symptoms vary day to day, and it is measured with bronchoprovocation testing. Airway remodeling is the long-term consequence of chronic inflammation: thickened airway walls (increased collagen and myofibroblasts), increased smooth muscle mass, epithelial injury and repair producing more mucus-producing cells, eosinophil-driven fibrosis, a thickened basement membrane, and damaged cilia. If remodeling progresses, obstruction becomes partially irreversible — fixed airflow limitation.

SourceAsthma PP, slides 8, 18

5Risk factors — host factors and environmental triggers

Host (patient) factors

genetic predisposition / family history; sex differences (more common in boys in childhood, higher prevalence in women after puberty); and obesity, which is associated with worse asthma control and reduced response to therapy. Environmental triggers: air pollution and tobacco smoke, occupational exposures and irritant gases, viral respiratory infections (RSV, rhinovirus), and diet (cow's milk, soy, processed foods) plus early environmental exposures. The take-home is that asthma usually develops from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure.

SourceAsthma PP, slide 12

6Risk factors — medications, additives, and other provokers

Aspirin and NSAIDs can trigger bronchospasm; beta-blockers worsen asthma because they block the β₂ receptors responsible for bronchodilation. Food additives — sulfites (dried fruits, shrimp, beer, wine) and the dye tartrazine — can provoke attacks. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction is worse in cold air and is thought to come from cooling and drying of the airways. GERD can trigger bronchospasm and chronic cough. Symptoms often worsen at night (nocturnal asthma), and perimenstrual asthma reflects hormonal fluctuation. 8Extrinsic vs. intrinsic asthma

Extrinsic (allergic / atopic) asthma is triggered by specific allergens — dust mites, pollen, pet dander, mold — is associated with atopy and elevated IgE, works through mast cell activation and mediator release, is more common in children and younger adults, and often travels with eczema, allergic rhinitis, and hay fever. 12SABA details and side effects you get asked about

Albuterol is the most-used SABA — nebulized typically 2.5 mg in 3 mL normal saline, or by MDI; it is a saligenin agent (catechol nucleus modified at the carbon-3 site for β₂ selectivity), available as MDI, DPI, nebulizer, syrup, and extended-release tablets. 1Definition of COPD

COPD is a preventable, treatable, progressive, irreversible airflow limitation caused by an abnormal inflammatory response to harmful particles or gases — most commonly cigarette smoke. It is preventable and treatable, but once the process starts the airflow limitation is irreversible and gets worse over time. It primarily hits the lungs but has systemic involvement throughout the body. Pathologically it means airspace enlargement distal to the terminal bronchioles, goblet cell hypertrophy, and mucus gland hyperplasia. Key terms to own: chronic ventilatory failure, chronic hypercapnia, increased lung compliance, decreased elastic recoil, and chronic CO₂ retention.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 2-3

2Symptoms of COPD

The four classic symptoms are dyspnea (especially on exertion), chronic cough, mucus/sputum production, and wheezing. Clinically patients also show hypoxemia and hypercapnia as the disease advances. On the mMRC-style questions the deck emphasizes that dyspnea slowly worsens over years; the cough may be dry or productive, and recurrent respiratory infections are common. These are key indicators that raise suspicion — they are not diagnostic by themselves.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 2-3, 16, 18

3Diagnosis — the FEV1/FVC criterion and GOLD's clinical triggers

GOLD = Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease, an international expert committee that publishes the worldwide standard of care. Consider COPD in patients over 40 with dyspnea, chronic cough or sputum production, a history of smoking or risk-factor exposure, or a family history of COPD. The diagnosis is confirmed only by spirometry: a post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC < 0.70. The word post-bronchodilator is the whole point — COPD is confirmed when the obstruction does not go away after a bronchodilator.

In COPD the FEV₁ falls more than the FVC because obstruction slows expiration, which is what drags the ratio down; a normal person exhales about 80–85% of the FVC in the first second, versus roughly 66% in the example COPD spirogram.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 16, 18-19

4Etiology and epidemiology

Between 10 and 15 million people in the United States have chronic bronchitis, emphysema, or a combination of both, and most authorities agree COPD is underdiagnosed — patients blame early dyspnea and cough on aging and don't seek care until the disease has progressed. COPD was historically more common in men because of smoking rates, but it now affects both genders about equally, and in some regions women have higher hospitalization and mortality rates because their lungs may be more susceptible to smoke and environmental exposure.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 10-11

5Pathophysiology of COPD

Chronic inflammation from inhaled particles produces two intertwined processes. In the airways, inflammation thickens the walls, submucosal glands enlarge and goblet cells increase, mucus production goes up, ciliary function drops, and mucus plugging obstructs airflow. In the parenchyma, alveolar walls are destroyed, so surface area for gas exchange falls, lung compliance increases, and elastic recoil is lost. Losing that recoil and the structural support of the small airways means the distal airways collapse during expiration → air trapping and hyperinflation → barrel chest.

In advanced COPD, work of breathing rises so much that the patient spends more oxygen breathing than they gain, so the body shifts to a mechanically easier but less effective pattern — that is the origin of chronic CO₂ retention. Collateral ventilation through the pores of Kohn and canals of Lambert partly compensates in emphysema.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 2, 4-7, 12

6Chronic bronchitis — the "Blue Bloater" (Type B COPD)

Chronic bronchitis is defined clinically: a chronic productive cough for ≥ 3 months in each of 2 consecutive years, after excluding other causes. Pathologic changes are chronic inflammation with thickened airway walls, goblet cell hyperplasia and enlarged submucosal glands, excessive mucus, reduced ciliary function with impaired clearance, mucus plugging, and in late stages air trapping and alveolar hyperinflation. Clinically these patients have a chronic productive cough, cyanosis ("blue") from hypoxemia, a stocky, overweight build, and peripheral edema from cor pulmonale. The name comes from the pairing of cyanosis plus fluid retention from right heart failure secondary to pulmonary hypertension.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 3-5

7Emphysema — the "Pink Puffer" (Type A COPD)

Emphysema is defined pathologically: permanent enlargement and destruction of the air spaces distal to the terminal bronchioles, with alveolar wall destruction and no obvious fibrosis. Breakdown of the alveolar walls costs surface area for gas exchange; loss of airway support causes collapse during expiration with air trapping and hyperinflation. Clinically: severe dyspnea on exertion, weight loss with a thin/cachectic appearance, barrel chest (increased A-P diameter), pursed-lip breathing ("puffing"), tachypnea, and a reddish/pink complexion.

"Pink" = relatively preserved oxygenation early on; "Puffer" = pursed-lip breathing that creates back-pressure to keep the airways open during exhalation. GOLD does not use the terms chronic bronchitis and emphysema in its formal definition of COPD, but both remain clinically and epidemiologically useful, and the two conditions often coexist.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 3, 6

9Nonpulmonary (systemic) consequences of COPD

Although the lungs are the primary site of damage, COPD has systemic effects that impact the entire body. The nonpulmonary consequences the decks name are: cachexia / weight loss and muscle wasting from the energy cost of breathing; cor pulmonale (right-sided heart failure from pulmonary hypertension) producing peripheral edema, JVD/distended neck veins, and cardiomegaly; polycythemia from chronic hypoxemia stimulating erythropoietin; electrolyte disturbance (↓ Cl⁻, ↑ Na⁺) from renal compensation and aldosterone/ADH activation; digital clubbing; and cyanosis.

GOLD also directs you to assess comorbidities, because COPD commonly coexists with cardiovascular disease, anxiety, GERD, and metabolic disorders, all of which complicate management.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 2, 4, 6, 12, 14-15, 17, 30-32

10What is cachexia?

Cachectic means a patient who is severely underweight and physically wasted due to chronic illness — that is the deck's own definition, given as the pronunciation note "kuh-KEK-tik." In COPD it is a hallmark of the emphysema / Pink Puffer phenotype: these patients spend so much energy on the work of breathing that the significant energy expenditure produces weight loss and a thin, cachectic appearance with visible muscle wasting.

It is not simply being thin — it is disease-driven wasting, and it is one of the clearest examples that COPD has systemic effects beyond the lungs. Practically, this is why proper nutrition and monitoring fluid intake appear on the COPD treatment list.

SourceCOPD PP, slides 6, 14, 29

11Increased risk factors because of COPD (what having COPD puts you at risk for)

Having COPD itself raises the patient's health risks. The deck names increased risk of heart disease, a higher likelihood of lung cancer, and other related complications. Layered on top of that are the disease-driven risks above — cor pulmonale and right heart failure, acute on chronic ventilatory failure, and recurrent exacerbations. Exacerbation risk is itself self-reinforcing: the best predictor of a future exacerbation is a prior one, especially ≥ 2 exacerbations in the past year or any exacerbation requiring hospitalization, and that risk rises significantly in GOLD 3 and GOLD 4 patients because their baseline lung function is already severely reduced. Respiratory infections are a major exacerbation trigger, which is why flu, pneumococcal, and Tdap vaccinations matter.

13LTOT — long-term oxygen therapy qualifying criteria (PaO₂ and SpO₂ thresholds)

The decision threshold for continuous long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) in COPD is SaO₂ ≤ 88% or PaO₂ ≤ 55 torr on room air — meeting either qualifies the patient for home O₂. 1ETT Sizes — Adult and Pediatric

Endotracheal tubes are sized by internal diameter in millimeters, and the full range stocked runs size 2 to 10 mm ID. For adults the deck gives two numbers to memorize: 7 to 7.5 mm ID for women and 8 to 8.5 mm ID for men. Laryngoscope blades run size 00 to 4 — Macintosh (curved) blades sit in the vallecula and lift the epiglottis indirectly, while Miller (straight) blades lift the epiglottis directly.

Sizing matters anatomically because the narrowest point differs by age: in adults the glottis (vocal cords) is narrowest and the trachea below is cylindrical, but in children the cricoid ring is narrowest and the airway is funnel-shaped. That is why 1 mm of circumferential edema is tolerated in an adult but can cut an infant's airway diameter by more than 50%. Newer MRI/CT imaging suggests the pediatric glottis may be as narrow as the cricoid, which is one reason cuffed ETTs are now routinely used in pediatrics — but for the NBRC answer, adults = glottis, children = cricoid ring.

2Cuff Pressure — The Number to Memorize

Cuff pressure should be maintained at 20 to 30 cm H₂O. This is the single most testable number in the airway deck, and it is stated twice (the cuff slide and the Key Points slide). The reason for the ceiling: tracheal capillary perfusion pressure is normally 25 to 35 mm Hg, and because the pressure transmitted from the cuff to the tracheal wall is usually less than the pressure inside the cuff, 30 cm H₂O is the maximum acceptable intracuff pressure — above that you risk mucosal ischemia, tracheal wall necrosis, and stenosis.

The reason for the floor: below 20 cm H₂O, microaspiration past the cuff becomes likely, which is the primary route to VAP. In mm Hg terms (conversion given: 1 mmHg = 1.36 cmH₂O, or 1 cmH₂O ≈ 0.735 mmHg), that range is roughly 14.7 to 22.05 mm Hg, and the deck adds that cuff pressure should stay < 25 mm Hg, and lower than 25 mm Hg if the patient is hypotensive.

Monitoring cuff pressure is standard respiratory care practice; causes of a high cuff pressure are a tube that is too small and incorrect ETT positioning.

SourceAirway Mngmnt (Hess Ch 17), slides 86, 87, 88, 101

3Cuff Types and the Two Inflation Techniques

Airway cuffs are either high-volume, low-pressure or low-volume, high-pressure; because high tracheal wall pressures injure mucosa, most cuffs used today are high-volume, low-pressure. Two bedside inflation methods are named. Minimal occlusion volume (MOV): place the stethoscope diaphragm on the trachea and inflate the cuff slowly until no air leak is heard at end-inspiration (end-inspiration is when the trachea is at maximum diameter).

Minimal leak technique (MLT): inflate until leaks stop, then slowly remove a small amount of air until a slight leak is heard at end-inspiration. Hess presents both techniques neutrally — "The airway cuff can be inflated with a minimum occlusion pressure technique or a minimum leak technique" — and does not call MLT obsolete. If you meet the claim that "the minimal leak technique is no longer recommended," that is the Heuer / VAP-bundle position from your 203 book, not Hess's; on a 202 exam, treat MOV and MLT as two named options with MOV generally preferred.

A specialty option is the Bivona foam cuff — a large-diameter, high-residual-volume polyurethane foam cuff in a silicone sheath; air is evacuated before insertion, then the pilot port is left open to atmosphere so intracuff pressure sits at ambient level. Used properly, foam-cuff lateral tracheal wall pressure rarely exceeds 20 mm Hg (27 cm H₂O).

SourceAirway Mngmnt (Hess Ch 17), slides 72, 86, 87

4Mallampati Classification

The Mallampati Classification is a bedside airway-assessment tool that estimates how easy or difficult an airway will be to manage based on how much of the oropharynx is visible when the patient opens the mouth widely and extends the tongue. It is performed as part of the mouth portion of the three-part airway assessment (nose → mouth → neck), and the mouth and neck portions are the most clinically important for anticipating a difficult airway.

As the Mallampati class increases, the anticipated difficulty of intubation increases. The same score is also used as an OSA screening tool — a Mallampati score of 3 or 4 suggests anatomical narrowing (enlarged tongue, tonsillar hypertrophy, limited airway space) and predicts increased risk of obstructive sleep apnea; Class IV is specifically associated with difficult intubation AND increased incidence of sleep apnea.

6ETT Features That Reduce VAP

Because the ETT bypasses normal airway defenses, several design modifications exist to attack the two mechanisms of VAP: drainage of subglottic secretions past the cuff, and biofilm developing inside the tube lumen that then drains or is pushed into the lung. The main design is the subglottic secretion drainage (SSD) tube — a separate dorsal lumen with a port opening above the cuff, connected to continuous or intermittent negative pressure so pooled secretions are removed before they leak past the cuff.

Named examples: the Hi-Lo Evac tube, the SealGuard, and the TaperGuard; the tracheostomy equivalent is the Blom tube with a subglottic suction cannula that replaces the standard inner cannula. Separately, ETTs coated with antibiotics and silver have shown reduced VAP in clinical trials. The deck is honest about the controversy: a meta-analysis reported a significant VAP reduction with SSD devices, but the reduction has not translated into decreased mortality, these devices have a high mechanical failure rate, and the benefit may actually come from the ultrathin polyurethane cuffs (PUCs) some of these tubes carry rather than the suction lumen.

Related VAP-relevant items in this deck: keeping cuff pressure at 20–30 cm H₂O (below 20 invites microaspiration), the closed suction system (catheter becomes part of the circuit, minimizing circuit breaks and preserving end-expiratory lung volume), the endOclear ETT-cleaning device for lumen secretions, using saline instillation judiciously rather than with every suction pass, and avoiding routine nasal intubation, whose sinusitis carries an increased risk of a ventilator-associated event.

7OPA vs. NPA

The oropharyngeal airway (OPA) is a rigid, hard-plastic device inserted between the lips and teeth, extending from the lips back to the pharynx along the natural curvature of the tongue — without entering the larynx or esophagus. The nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) is plastic or rubber, inserted into the naris and directed along the floor of the nose parallel to the hard palate, so its tip rests behind the tongue just above the epiglottis.

The single most important distinction: an OPA requires the absence of a gag reflex and must NOT be used in semi-comatose or alert patients — the NPA is the airway for the patient who does not arouse but still coughs (this is the deck's own Stop-and-Think answer). Sizing is assessed before insertion in both cases; an OPA that is too large may press the epiglottis against the posterior pharyngeal wall or larynx, obstructing both device and airway, while one that is too small or improperly inserted pushes the tongue against the posterior pharynx, also obstructing.

8Oral vs. Nasal Intubation

Endotracheal intubation is placement of a tube through the mouth or nose, through the glottis, and into the trachea. Oral intubation is the default and the faster route, but the deck lists its downsides plainly: it is uncomfortable, causes gagging, and produces excessive secretions. A stylet (to give the ETT its curvature) is optional for oral intubation, and a bite block is placed between the teeth so the patient cannot bite the tube.

Nasotracheal intubation is the choice when access to the mouth is unavailable, as in trauma surgery, and specifically suits trismus (lockjaw), oral trauma or deformities, mandible fracture, and a short neck. Its advantages are that it is better tolerated than orotracheal intubation with potential for less tracheal injury. Its disadvantages are that it is harder to insert, requires a smaller ETT and therefore has higher airflow resistance, and carries a real incidence of sinusitis — which the deck flags as an increased risk of a ventilator-associated event. Equipment differs too: a stylet is NOT used in nasal intubation, and Magill forceps are required for it.

1The Clean Distinction — Ventilation is CO₂, Oxygenation is O₂

This is the whole section in one line: ventilation = CO₂ = PaCO₂, and oxygenation = O₂ = PaO₂. The ABG deck states it directly when listing why we draw an arterial sample — "to determine the adequacy of the patient's ventilation (PaCO₂), oxygenation (PaO₂), and acid-base status (pH)." Ventilation is about moving air in and out to clear CO₂; when it fails you get hypoventilation → ↑ PaCO₂ → respiratory acidosis, and the deck calls that ventilatory failure, defined as PaCO₂ > 45 mm Hg with pH < 7.35.

Oxygenation is about getting O₂ from the alveolus into the blood and on to the tissues; when it fails you get hypoxemia, and oxygenation failure is defined as severe hypoxemia (PaO₂ < 40 mm Hg) in spite of a high FiO₂ (50% to 100%). The two can move independently — that is the point. A patient can have a perfectly normal PaCO₂ and a terrible PaO₂ (ARDS, shunt), or a normal PaO₂ and a lethal PaCO₂ (opioid overdose).

The deck's own worked example: an opioid overdose with PaCO₂ 105 mm Hg, pH 7.14, PaO₂ 36 mm Hg is a ventilation problem first.

3Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve, P50, and Left/Right Shifts

The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve is the relationship the blood gas analyzer uses to calculate SaO₂ from a measured PaO₂ — which is exactly why a standard ABG's SaO₂ is a calculated, not measured, value, and why it assumes normal hemoglobin function and cannot distinguish normal hemoglobin from dysfunctional hemoglobins such as carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) or methemoglobin (MetHb). 4Dead Space — Anatomic, Alveolar, and Physiologic

Dead space is ventilation without perfusion — wasted air. Anatomic dead space is the conducting airways (nose, mouth, trachea, bronchi) and the rule to memorize is 1 mL per pound of body weight — so a 150 lb person has about 150 mL of anatomic dead space. Alveolar dead space is lung volume that cannot take part in gas exchange because of reduced or absent pulmonary perfusion — the alveolus is ventilated but no blood arrives, so O₂ enters but no blood picks it up and CO₂ never gets delivered.

Causes: pulmonary embolism (the most common board answer), severe hypotension, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, and overdistended alveoli from excessive PEEP. Physiologic dead space = anatomic VD + alveolar VD. The VD/VT ratio rises when VT falls, because VD stays constant while the breath gets smaller — the deck's worked example: 150 mL ÷ 500 mL = 30%, but 150 mL ÷ 300 mL = 50%.

A high physiologic VD/VT causes a persistent increase in the work of breathing, and when the patient can no longer sustain that WOB, ventilatory and oxygenation failure follow.

5Dead Space vs. Shunt — The One-Line Discriminator

Dead space is wasted AIR. Shunt is wasted BLOOD. The deck repeats this in three places because it is the most frequently confused pair on the exam. Dead space = ventilation WITHOUT perfusion — air reaches the alveoli, blood does not (PE, shock). Shunt = perfusion WITHOUT ventilation — blood reaches the alveoli, air does not (atelectasis, pneumonia, ARDS, pulmonary edema). The practical difference is how each responds to oxygen: shunt is the mechanism LEAST responsive to oxygen therapy (you cannot push oxygen into an alveolus that is collapsed or fluid-filled), which is why shunt produces refractory hypoxemia and requires recruitment — PEEP, CPAP, reopening alveoli — rather than more FiO₂.

V/Q mismatch, by contrast, usually improves with oxygen. The classic physiologic shunt equation is the gold standard measurement; normally less than 5% of blood misses gas exchange.

6Carbon Monoxide Poisoning — Why the Pulse Ox Lies

CO poisoning is the deck's headline example of hypoxia without hypoxemia: the patient's PaO₂ is normal and the SpO₂ looks normal, yet the tissues are suffocating. The mechanism given is that CO binds hemoglobin, so the hemoglobin is occupied and cannot carry oxygen — while PaO₂ stays normal because dissolved oxygen in the plasma is unaffected, and PaO₂ is what the ABG measures.

The deck's instruction is blunt: do not trust the SpO₂. A standard pulse oximeter reads falsely normal, so you must draw an ABG and check PaO₂ and SaO₂, and then confirm with a CO-oximeter. The conceptual parallel the deck draws is severe anemia: PaO₂ can be normal while CaO₂ is reduced, because an ABG does not measure oxygen-carrying capacity — a patient can be hypoxic with a normal PaO₂. This is precisely why oxygenation is assessed with CaO₂ = (SaO₂ × Hb × 1.34) + 0.003(PaO₂) rather than PaO₂ alone.

SourceABG Assessments (RCP 103), slide 70; Principles of Mech Vent (Chang/Hess), slides 25, 26 Hess supplies the treatment and the mechanism. Hess Ch 50: CO molecules bind Hb to form carboxyhaemoglobin (HbCO), preventing Hb from binding O₂, and HbCO shifts the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve to the LEFT, impairing unloading of O₂ at the peripheral tissues — so CO harms you twice, by cutting carrying capacity and by holding on to the oxygen that is carried. Treatment follows directly: "Supplemental O₂ is useful in treating CO poisoning because it displaces CO from Hb and decreases the half-life of carboxyhaemoglobin and CO in the tissues. This effect is greatly facilitated by hyperbaric O₂ therapy, which can dramatically increase the dissolved O₂ in the blood." Hess classes CO poisoning as anaemic hypoxia and makes the key point for exam purposes: in anaemic hypoxia from CO, supplemental O₂ does not really augment O₂ delivery to the tissues unless the PaO₂ is raised into the hyperbaric range, because what is limiting is dissolved O₂ in plasma, not PaO₂. ⚠ Still not in the decks: the treatment of CO poisoning (100% O₂ by non-rebreather to shorten the carboxyhemoglobin half-life, hyperbaric oxygen for severe cases), the COHb half-life numbers, and the left shift of the curve caused by CO. The decks stop at "recognize it and confirm with co-oximetry." Get the treatment protocol from your oxygen therapy chapter.
