Embryology & Lung Development

T1Know embryological development stages and further gestation until birth

  • Three prenatal stages (40 wk, 3 trimesters): ovum 12–14 days → embryo 54–56 days (ends at 3 cm crown–rump) → fetus 210–214 days.
  • After birth: neonate = birth to 1 month · infant = 1 month to 1 year · child = over 1 year.
  • Lung stages: embryonal (to wk 7; lung bud day 24) → pseudoglandular (7–16) → canalicular (17–26; vessels, gas exchange possible 24–25 wk) → saccular (26–36) → alveolar (true alveoli 32–34 wk, increasing to age 8).
  • Milestones: heart fully formed by week 8 · surfactant starts at 24 wk, mature at about 35 wk (L/S 2:1).
  • Term vs preterm: term >37 wk, premature <37 wk. Dating uses Nägele's rule (LMP − 3 months + 7 days), fundal height, quickening (16–22 wk) and heart tones (16–20 wk).
  • After birth, gestational age is scored by Dubowitz or Ballard, then classed as AGA (10th–90th percentile), SGA (<10th) or LGA (>90th).
Watch outSlides say the embryonal stage is the first 7 weeks; Whitaker says 8.
SourceSlides Ch01 S2–S21, Ch02 S11–S12, Ch05 S7–S9 · Whitaker pp. 4–20, 64, 131–140

T2Know the anatomical structures of the fetus and associated structures and how they change once born

  • Placenta: the fetal organ of respiration. At term it covers ⅓ of the uterus and weighs about 1 lb. Maternal and fetal blood never mix.
  • Umbilical cord: 3 vessels in Wharton's jelly: 2 arteries (to the placenta) and 1 vein (oxygenated blood to the fetus).
  • Amnion is the sac around the fetus. The amniotic fluid inside protects the fetus, lets it move and helps with thermoregulation.
  • Three shunts: ductus venosus (umbilical vein → IVC, bypasses liver) · foramen ovale (RA → LA) · ductus arteriosus (pulmonary artery → aorta, bypasses lungs).
  • At birth: the placenta is removed, so SVR rises. The lungs expand and O₂ dilates them, so PVR falls. LA pressure exceeds RA and the FO closes. The DA closes over about 96 h (surgery if it doesn't).
  • Remnants: DA → ligamentum arteriosum · DV → ligamentum venosum · umbilical vein → ligamentum teres · umbilical arteries → medial umbilical ligaments.
Watch outAmniotic fluid at term: slides say about 1 L; Whitaker says about 600 mL.
SourceSlides Ch01 S7–S27, Ch03 S10–S11 · Whitaker pp. 7–10, 24–27

D1Alveoli development

  • Alveoli begin forming in the canalicular stage (17–26 wk) and keep developing in the saccular stage (26–36 wk).
  • True alveoli appear at 32–34 weeks. There are 50–150 million at birth, and the number increases until about age 8.
  • Surfactant lines them and lowers surface tension so small alveoli don't collapse (Laplace).
SourceSlides Ch01 S13–S16 · Whitaker p. 14

D8Amnion

  • The sac surrounding the fetus that contains the amniotic fluid. It forms around day 7.
  • Amniotic fluid protects, allows movement, aids thermoregulation, and is sampled for lung maturity (L/S ratio).
Watch outFluid at term: slides about 1 L; book about 600 mL.
SourceSlides Ch01 S8–S9, S18 · Whitaker pp. 9–11

D9Surfactant

  • A substance on the alveolar wall that lowers surface tension, keeps alveoli open and reduces the work of breathing. It is made by type II cells.
  • Made of phospholipids (mainly DPPC/lecithin), neutral lipids and proteins.
  • Production starts at 24 wk; it is mature at about 35 wk (L/S ratio 2:1).
  • Deficiency = RDS. Replacement is instilled down the ETT, either prophylactically or as rescue therapy.
SourceSlides Ch01 S15–S18, Ch07 S2–S4 · Whitaker pp. 17–20, 666–669

D23Lung Development

  • Embryonal (to wk 7): lung bud at day 24.
  • Pseudoglandular (7–16): airways branch; the lung looks like a gland.
  • Canalicular (17–26): bronchioles multiply, vessels form, alveoli begin.
  • Saccular (26–36): lungs formed by 24–26 wk; alveoli develop.
  • Alveolar: true alveoli at 32–34 wk, increasing until about age 8.
  • Surfactant starts at 24 wk and is mature at 35 wk. Fetal lung fluid not cleared at birth = TTN.
SourceSlides Ch01 S10–S20 · Whitaker pp. 11–21

Fetal Circulation & Vessels

D2Foramen OvaleD4Umbilical VesselsD7Wharton's JellyD12ArteriesD19Fetal Circulation

D2Foramen Ovale

  • An opening between the atria that shunts blood from the right atrium to the left atrium in the fetus, bypassing the lungs.
  • It works because fetal right-side pressure is higher than left.
  • Closes at birth: systemic pressure rises, left-atrial pressure exceeds right, and the flap is pushed shut. Failure to close = ASD.
SourceSlides Ch01 S22–S25 · Whitaker pp. 24–26

D4Umbilical Vessels

  • The cord has 3 vessels: 2 umbilical arteries and 1 umbilical vein, cushioned by Wharton's jelly.
  • The vein carries oxygenated blood to the fetus; the arteries return blood to the placenta.
  • Clinical use: vein = emergency drug and fluid access · artery = blood sampling and UAC (thrombus is the most common complication).
  • After birth: vein → ligamentum teres; arteries → medial umbilical ligaments.
SourceSlides Ch01 S8, S23, Ch04 S19, S24 · Whitaker pp. 9, 27, 117–119

D7Wharton's Jelly

  • The tough, gelatinous tissue surrounding the three umbilical vessels.
  • It insulates and protects the vessels and keeps the cord from kinking and cutting off flow.
  • After delivery, the temperature change makes it collapse the vessels within about 5 minutes.
SourceNot on the slides · Whitaker p. 9

D12Arteries

  • Ductus arteriosus: pulmonary artery → aorta, so fetal blood bypasses the lungs. It closes within 96 h after birth.
  • Umbilical arteries (2): carry blood back to the placenta. Used for UAC and sampling.
  • Aorta and carotid arteries: sites of the chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies) and baroreceptors (carotid bifurcation, aortic arch).
  • Pre- vs postductal: right radial = preductal; UAC = postductal.
  • Defects: coarctation of the aorta, tetralogy (pulmonary artery obstruction), transposition, truncus arteriosus.
SourceSlides Ch01 S24–S27, Ch04 S24, Ch08 S24–S30 · Whitaker pp. 24–26, 500–502

D19Fetal Circulation

  • Fetal pressures are reversed: the right side is higher than the left, because the fluid-filled lungs resist flow while the placenta does not.
  • Pathway: placenta → umbilical vein → ductus venosus (bypasses the liver) → IVC → right atrium → foramen ovale → left heart → aorta. Some blood goes RV → pulmonary artery → ductus arteriosus → aorta. The umbilical arteries return blood to the placenta.
  • At birth: systemic pressure rises and PVR falls. The FO closes and the DA closes within about 96 h. Failure = PPHN.
SourceSlides Ch01 S21–S27 · Whitaker pp. 24–26

Fetal Assessment, Labor & Delivery

T4Know tocolytics and their function (sub-item: Beta sympathomimetics)

  • Tocolysis = stopping premature labor (12% of pregnancies). It is used before 37 weeks and also with placenta previa.
  • Beta-sympathomimetics: terbutaline and ritodrine. They stimulate β-receptors to relax uterine smooth muscle.
  • β-agonist side effects: maternal tachycardia, hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, pulmonary edema · fetal tachycardia · neonatal rebound hypoglycemia.
  • Magnesium sulfate lowers muscle contractility. The newborn may be hypotonic or apneic (hypermagnesemia).
  • Indomethacin blocks prostaglandins and can constrict the fetal ductus arteriosus. Nifedipine is a calcium-channel blocker.
SourceSlides Ch03 S5 · Whitaker pp. 70–72, 188

D17Chorioamnionitis

  • Bacterial infection and inflammation of the fetal membranes and amniotic fluid.
  • Cause: organisms ascend the birth canal, especially after membranes have been ruptured >24 h.
  • Clue: foul-smelling amniotic fluid and baby. Raises the risk of neonatal sepsis and pneumonia.
  • Treatment: broad-spectrum antibiotics for the newborn.
SourceSlides Ch08 S4, S13 · Whitaker pp. 253–254

D26Cervix Dilation

  • Dilation is the widening of the cervix, measured in cm. It is fully dilated at 10 cm. Effacement is thinning, measured in %.
  • Stage 1 = onset of contractions to full dilation · Stage 2 = full dilation to delivery · Stage 3 = delivery of the placenta.
SourceSlides Ch03 S3–S4 · Whitaker pp. 65–66

D27Placenta Previa

  • The placenta implants low in the uterus, near or over the cervical opening.
  • Types: low (marginal) · partial · total (completely covers the os).
  • Diagnosed by ultrasound. It blocks fetal passage, risks abruption, is a high-risk pregnancy factor and a cause of fetal asphyxia.
SourceSlides Ch02 S3, S21, Ch03 S8 · Whitaker pp. 77–78

D28Indications for Cesarean Section

  • Indications (Whitaker): prior cesarean, dystocia (failure to progress), breech presentation, fetal distress.
  • Also: transverse lie, abruptio placentae, maternal active herpes.
  • Neonatal risk: retained lung fluid → TTN.
SourceSlides Ch03 S9, Ch01 S20 · Whitaker pp. 75, 79, 81

T3Methods of treatment of fetal asphyxia and their presentation

  • Asphyxia = hypoxia + hypercapnia + acidosis. Causes: maternal hypoxia, poor placental flow, cord blockage, fetal anemia or drugs.
  • Presentation: HR and BP fall; primary apnea, then weak gasping, then secondary apnea. In utero it shows as fetal bradycardia (<100), loss of variability, late decelerations, meconium-stained fluid and a low scalp pH (<7.20 = severe).
  • Treatment before birth: O₂ to the mother, reposition her (for cord compression), amnioinfusion, and rapid delivery (cesarean if needed).
  • Treatment after birth ("immediate reversal of hypoxia and acidosis"): warm, dry, open airway, PPV if apneic, gasping or HR <100 · compressions if HR <60 after 30 s of PPV (3:1) · epinephrine 1:10,000 0.1–0.3 mL/kg IV · volume 10 mL/kg for blood loss.
  • Treat every apneic newborn as if in secondary apnea: ventilate.
SourceSlides Ch02 S8–S15, Ch04 S2–S19, Ch07 S17–S18 · Whitaker pp. 43–48, 91–112, 221–223

T5Know the APGAR score and associated function

  • Five signs, each scored 0/1/2 (total 0–10), at 1 and 5 minutes; repeated every 5 min up to 20 min if low.
  • Heart rate: 0 none · 1 slow/irregular · 2 >100.
  • Respiratory effort: 0 apnea · 1 irregular/gasping · 2 crying.
  • Muscle tone: 0 flaccid · 1 some flexion · 2 well flexed.
  • Reflex irritability: 0 none · 1 grimace · 2 crying.
  • Color: 0 pale/blue · 1 acrocyanosis (pink body, blue hands and feet) · 2 all pink.
  • Function: it objectively evaluates the newborn and the response to resuscitation. The 5-minute score predicts future impairment. <6 at 1 min or <7 at 5 min needs intervention. Never delay resuscitation to score it.
SourceSlides Ch04 S20–S21 · Whitaker pp. 111–113, 141

T15Etiology and treatment of Meconium aspiration syndrome

  • Etiology: fetal asphyxia increases gut movement and relaxes the anal sphincter, so meconium passes. The fetus then gasps it in. It affects term and post-term babies.
  • Signs: green-stained amniotic fluid, low Apgar, fetal hypoxia and acidosis, abnormal heart tracings.
  • Lung injury: ball-valve air trapping (leading to air leaks), chemical pneumonitis, and PPHN. CXR shows patchy infiltrates with hyperinflation.
  • Treatment: amnioinfusion (warm saline) to dilute · not vigorous (HR <100, weak effort, poor tone) → intubate and suction the trachea · vigorous → bulb-suction mouth and nose · warm humidified O₂ · ventilation (PEEP, long Te), surfactant, iNO if PPHN.
SourceSlides Ch07 S19–S20, Ch04 S17 · Whitaker pp. 98–100, 223–228

D21Indications for PPV

  • Apnea, gasping, or HR <100 despite spontaneous breathing.
  • Give 40–60 breaths/min and reassess at 30 s. If HR is 60–100, continue PPV; if HR <60, add compressions.
SourceSlides Ch04 S15–S16 · Whitaker pp. 101–104

D22Secondary Apnea

  • After asphyxia, the fetus/newborn stops breathing (primary apnea), then makes weak, gasping attempts, then stops completely. That final stage is secondary apnea.
  • HR and BP keep falling. Breathing will not return without PPV; stimulation doesn't work.
  • Treat every apneic newborn as if in secondary apnea.
Watch outThe slide labels the gasping itself as secondary apnea; the book puts gasping just before it.
SourceSlides Ch04 S5, S15 · Whitaker pp. 91–92

D25Intubation routes

  • Oral (orotracheal) intubation with a straight Miller blade (size 0 preterm, 1 term) and an uncuffed tube is the standard neonatal route.
  • Indications: thick meconium in a non-vigorous baby, ineffective bag-mask, prolonged PPV, compressions, surfactant, diaphragmatic hernia.
  • Confirm placement with bilateral breath sounds, CO₂ detector and CXR. Limit each attempt to 30 s.
  • Related airway routes: oral or nasopharyngeal airways; nasal prongs for CPAP.
Watch outNeither source compares oral vs nasal intubation; get that from lecture.
SourceSlides Ch04 S17–S18, Ch09 S3–S4 · Whitaker pp. 106–109

Neonatal & Infant Assessment

T6Causes for increased WOB (work of breathing — retractions, grunting, nasal flaring, and their significance)

  • Signs of increased work of breathing: tachypnea (>60), grunting, retractions, nasal flaring, cyanosis.
  • Grunting means closing the glottis on exhalation. It creates back-pressure (self-made PEEP) that keeps alveoli from collapsing, and signals low lung volume.
  • Retractions come from stiff (low-compliance) lungs pulling the soft infant chest wall inward. They are intercostal, substernal or suprasternal, graded mild to severe.
  • Nasal flaring widens the nares to cut resistance and bring in more air.
  • Causes: RDS (surfactant deficiency), TTN (retained lung fluid), pneumothorax and air leaks, pneumonia, MAS. The infant is predisposed by a narrow airway, cartilaginous chest wall, low reserve and high metabolic rate.
Watch outRDS slide lists "Increased FRC"; RDS actually lowers FRC, and grunting is the attempt to raise it.
SourceSlides Ch05 S2–S6, Ch07 S2–S3, S21–S22, S31 · Whitaker pp. 128–130, 142–143, 206

T8Know the aspects of physical examination of the neonatal/pediatric patient and the different presentations of distress and abnormalities and the associated treatments

  • Neonatal exam order: observe (distress signs) → history and gestational age (Dubowitz/Ballard) → AGA/SGA/LGA → quiet exam (color, respiration, activity) → hands-on (head, heart and lungs, pulses, BP, abdomen, temperature) → reflexes (rooting, suck, grasp, Moro).
  • Normals: HR 120–160 · RR 30–60 · temp 36.2–37.3 °C (axillary). Apnea = >15–20 s with cyanosis or bradycardia.
  • Distress presentations: tachypnea, grunting, retractions, flaring, cyanosis, poor temperature control, limpness. Treatment: O₂ first, then CPAP or ventilation as needed.
  • Abnormal findings: scaphoid abdomen = diaphragmatic hernia · shifted PMI = pneumothorax or atelectasis · weak femoral pulses = coarctation · bulging fontanelle = raised ICP.
  • Pediatric exam: history → measurements → head-to-toe. For the lungs: localize the disease (auscultate, percuss, CXR), check gas exchange (ABG, SpO₂), and judge rate, depth, rhythm and ease. A resting RR >40 after age 1 needs investigation.
  • Disease-specific treatments: RDS = steroids, surfactant, ventilation · MAS = suction if not vigorous, O₂ · TTN = O₂, CPAP · epiglottitis = O₂, prepare to intubate · croup = humidity, fluids, observation.
SourceSlides Ch05, Ch07, Ch09–Ch11 · Whitaker pp. 128–153, 337–341

D3CXR uses

  • Confirm a diagnosis and localize disease (with auscultation, percussion, palpation).
  • Check placement of tubes and lines (ETT tip halfway between carina and clavicles; UAC; chest tube).
  • Follow disease progression and response to therapy.
  • Specific uses: BPD, pneumomediastinum, pneumopericardium, foreign body.
SourceSlides Ch10 S13, Ch07 S7, S24–S25, Ch09 S16 · Whitaker pp. 534–535

D5Normal HR

  • Newborn and fetus: 120–160 bpm.
  • Infant (0–12 months): about 123–138 (average).
  • Children: about 80 at 5 years, about 70 at 10 years, 60s by 15 years.
  • Resuscitation lines: >100 is the goal · <100 → PPV · <60 → compressions.
SourceSlides Ch02 S8, Ch04 S13–S16 · Whitaker pp. 43, 143, 803

D6Normal RR

  • Newborn: 30–60 breaths/min. Above 60 = tachypnea (an RDS sign).
  • Infant: about 30.
  • Children: about 22 at 5 years, 18–19 at 10–15 years.
  • After age 1, a resting RR >40 needs investigation; after age 5, >35 does.
SourceSlides Ch07 S3 · Whitaker pp. 142, 340, 804

D10Bradycardia

  • Fetal: <100 bpm (often asphyxia).
  • Newborn: <100 bpm.
  • Children: <100 under 3 years · <60 at 3–9 years · <50 at 9–16 years.
  • In children it usually means hypoxia: ventilate and oxygenate first. If HR <60 with poor perfusion → CPR.
SourceSlides Ch02 S8 · Whitaker pp. 43, 143, 294

D11Tachycardia

  • Fetal: >180 bpm (most often maternal fever; also β-agonist tocolytics).
  • Newborn: >160 bpm.
  • Children: classify by QRS. Narrow = sinus or SVT; SVT gets vagal maneuvers, then adenosine, then cardioversion if unstable.
SourceSlides Ch02 S9, Ch05 S6 · Whitaker pp. 43, 143, 294

D18Larynx

  • In infants and children the larynx is higher and more anterior than in adults. The epiglottis is larger, floppy and U-shaped.
  • The narrowest point is the cricoid, so a little swelling causes major obstruction.
  • This is why a straight (Miller) blade and an uncuffed tube are used in infants.
SourceSlides Ch05 S2, Ch10 S3 · Whitaker pp. 107, 128–129, 329

D20Cyanosis

  • Bluish skin from deoxygenated hemoglobin. It appears at about 5 g/dL of desaturated Hb, so hypoxemia may already be significant.
  • Check central color on the trunk (in dark skin, use the mucous membranes). Acrocyanosis (blue hands and feet only) is normal after birth.
  • Central cyanosis → O₂. If it won't correct with 100% O₂, suspect a right-to-left shunt (PPHN or heart disease).
  • Seen in PPHN, pneumothorax, apnea and Wilson-Mikity.
SourceSlides Ch04 S14, Ch07 S22, S29, S33 · Whitaker pp. 100–101, 409, 497

T9Thermoregulation methods and importance in the aspects of respiratory care in both the neonatal and pediatric patient

  • Thermoregulation = balancing heat production and heat loss. It is one of the most important parts of neonatal care because the newborn relies on brown fat (it can't shiver well).
  • Goal: the neutral thermal environment (lowest O₂ consumption); rectal 36.5–37.5 °C.
  • Four losses and fixes: evaporative → dry immediately · radiant (largest) → cap, warm blanket, radiant warmer · conductive → warm mattress and surfaces · convective → avoid drafts, warm the gases.
  • Nursery: incubator = controlled and quiet but limits access · radiant warmer = access but harder to control (and more water loss).
  • Pediatric: hypothermia mostly follows trauma; CNS injury can stop shivering. Address it in the trauma survey and prevent it in burns.
  • Why RTs care: cold causes acidosis, pulmonary vasoconstriction and hypoxemia, and blowing cold O₂ on the face causes heat loss.
SourceSlides Ch04 S11, Ch06 S2–S9, Ch11 S10 · Whitaker pp. 96–97, 163–172

T10Know the neonate's response to cold stress and its consequences

  • Cold stress = any drop below the thermoneutral temperature.
  • Response: norepinephrine → brown fat burned (nonshivering thermogenesis) → ↑O₂ consumption and ↓glucose.
  • Chain of consequences: peripheral vasoconstriction → anaerobic metabolism → metabolic acidosis → pulmonary vasoconstriction → hypoxemia → death if uncorrected.
  • Other consequences: hypoglycemia, calories lost for growth, less surfactant, bradycardia and arrhythmias.
SourceSlides Ch06 S3–S6 · Whitaker pp. 141, 163–167

T17Causes of insensible water loss in the neonate and pediatric patient

  • IWL = water lost by evaporation from the skin and respiratory tract.
  • Increase IWL: prematurity (thin, permeable skin), small size, respiratory distress, high temperature, skin breakdown or open defects, radiant warmers and phototherapy, motor activity and crying.
  • Decrease IWL: humidity and heat shields.
  • Pediatric: a larger BSA means more loss and faster dehydration.
SourceSlides Ch06 S16–S17 · Whitaker pp. 181–185, 328

T11Etiology and treatment of ROP (Retinopathy of Prematurity)

  • Etiology: prematurity (<31 wk, ≤1250 g) plus oxygen. The retinal vessels are immature; high PaO₂ causes vasoconstriction, then vessel necrosis, then abnormal new vessels, then scar and retinal detachment. Formerly called retrolental fibroplasia.
  • Stages: 1 demarcation line · 2 ridge · 3 extraretinal fibrovascular proliferation · 4 partial detachment · 5 total detachment.
  • Treatment: cryotherapy or laser photocoagulation; vitrectomy/lensectomy for late stages.
  • Prevention: careful O₂ use with monitoring (PaO₂ 50–70).
SourceSlides Ch07 S11–S13 · Whitaker pp. 213–216, 410

T12Etiology and treatment of NEC

  • NEC = ischemia and necrosis of the intestine, mostly in premature, formula-fed babies.
  • Etiology: (1) bowel wall ischemia (asphyxia, PDA, UAC, exchange transfusion) + (2) bacterial invasion + (3) formula feeding. Breast milk protects.
  • Signs: bloody (guaiac +) stools, abdominal distention, bilious residuals and vomiting, feeding intolerance. X-ray shows pneumatosis intestinalis (gas in the bowel wall).
  • Treatment: stop feeds (NPO), NG suction, IV nutrition, antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin), serial abdominal X-rays, surgery if perforated.
SourceSlides Ch06 S28, Ch07 S18 · Whitaker pp. 192–194

T13Etiology and treatment of Pneumothorax

  • Etiology: alveolar rupture with air entering the pleural space. Most cases come from mechanical ventilation (barotrauma); more common with RDS and MAS. It can be spontaneous or tension.
  • Signs: sudden increase in distress, retractions and tachypnea, progressing to bradycardia, cyanosis, apnea and hypotension. Also asymmetric chest rise and a shifted PMI.
  • Diagnosis: transillumination; CXR shows air with no lung markings, with the mediastinum shifted away in tension.
  • Treatment by severity: observe if small · needle aspiration in an emergency · chest tube (suction −15 to −25 cm H₂O) for an ongoing leak.
SourceSlides Ch07 S21–S23 · Whitaker pp. 228–231, 555

T18ABG interpretation, with special considerations for RDS

  • Safe ranges (neonate and child): pH 7.35–7.45 (acceptable 7.30–7.50) · PaCO₂ 35–45 · HCO₃⁻ 22–26 · BE ±4 · PaO₂ neonate 50–70 / child 80–100.
  • Interpretation: pH ↓ with PaCO₂ ↑ = respiratory acidosis; pH ↓ with HCO₃⁻ ↓ = metabolic acidosis (and the reverse for alkalosis). Check whether the other value has compensated.
  • RDS picture: hypoxemia that responds poorly to O₂ + rising PaCO₂ + mixed respiratory and metabolic (lactic) acidosis.
  • RDS targets: PaO₂ 50–80, PaCO₂ <60, pH >7.25. Intubate if PaCO₂ >60 with pH <7.25.
  • Sampling: UAC preferred · draw 15–30 min after vent changes · capillary samples are OK for pH and PaCO₂ but not PaO₂.
SourceSlides Ch07 S2–S5 · Whitaker pp. 206–208, 505–516

D16PPHN and treatment

  • PPHN (persistent fetal circulation) = severe pulmonary vasoconstriction, so pulmonary pressure stays high and blood shunts right-to-left through the FO and DA.
  • Causes: asphyxia, MAS, sepsis, diaphragmatic hernia, cold, acidosis. Usually affects term babies.
  • Signs: cyanosis and tachypnea within hours of birth that don't improve with O₂; preductal PaO₂ higher than postductal.
  • Tests: hyperoxia test, pre/postductal PaO₂, hyperoxia-hyperventilation test, echocardiogram.
  • Treatment: keep temperature, fluids, glucose and BP normal · minimal handling · mechanical ventilation (HFV) · inhaled nitric oxide · ECMO as a last resort.
SourceSlides Ch07 S28–S30 · Whitaker pp. 232–235, 673–674

T7Bacterial infections of the neonate

  • Source: maternal intestinal and genital flora, or poor aseptic technique. Organisms ascend the birth canal after rupture of membranes (>24 h greatly raises risk), enter the fetal mouth, then the lungs, gut and bloodstream.
  • Main organisms: group B strep and E. coli. Also Listeria, Staph aureus, Klebsiella, H. influenzae.
  • Chorioamnionitis (infected membranes and amniotic fluid) greatly increases risk.
  • Signs: respiratory distress, tachycardia, lethargy, poor feeding, temperature instability (hypothermia in preemies), WBC <5000 or >15,000.
  • Diagnosis: specimen or blood culture. Treatment: broad-spectrum antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin). Mothers are screened for GBS at 35–37 wk.
SourceSlides Ch08 S2, S4, S13–S15 · Whitaker pp. 253–255, 393–394, 459

T14Etiology and treatment of Hyperbilirubinemia

  • Hyperbilirubinemia shows as jaundice (yellow-orange skin) once bilirubin is above about 5 mg/dL.
  • Etiology: physiologic (high RBC mass with short RBC life, immature liver) or pathologic: Rh/ABO incompatibility, hemorrhage, infection (CMV, rubella, toxoplasmosis), infant of a diabetic mother, galactosemia, hypothyroidism, breast-feeding.
  • Pathologic if: appears in the first 24 h · indirect >13 mg/dL (term) or >15 (preterm) · rises >5 mg/dL in 24 h · direct >1.5 · lasts >7 days (term) or >14 days (preterm).
  • Complications: kernicterus (unconjugated bilirubin enters the brain, causing cerebral palsy and deafness), and NEC.
  • Treatment: phototherapy (blue light makes bilirubin water-soluble; it increases water loss) · exchange transfusion for high levels · treat the cause.
SourceSlides Ch06 S26–S28 · Whitaker pp. 189–192

T19Understand the TORCH mnemonic

  • Toxoplasmosis: cat feces or raw meat; hydrocephalus, seizures, jaundice.
  • Other: syphilis, HIV, coxsackievirus, varicella, parvovirus B19.
  • Rubella: cataracts, heart defects, deafness. Prevented by vaccinating the mother.
  • Cytomegalovirus: IUGR, liver damage, microcephaly, progressive hearing loss. Supportive care.
  • Herpes simplex type 2: acquired from the birth canal; cesarean if the mother has active lesions; treated with antivirals (acyclovir).
  • Mnemonic purpose: these infections cross to the fetus or newborn and cause similar findings (IUGR, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, microcephaly).
SourceSlides Ch08 S3, S8–S11 · Whitaker pp. 253, 258–260

D14ASD

  • Atrial septal defect = the foramen-ovale flap fails to cover the opening, so blood flows between the atria (left-to-right after birth).
  • Most common type: ostium secundum. Often symptomless.

T16PALS Cardiac pulseless arrest: shockable and non-shockable rhythms

  • Shockable: ventricular fibrillation (VF) and rapid (pulseless) ventricular tachycardia → defibrillate.
  • Non-shockable: asystole and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) → CPR and epinephrine.
  • Sequence: CPR with O₂ → check rhythm (ECG or AED) → shock if shockable, resuming CPR immediately → recheck every 2 minutes → treat reversible causes.
  • CPR: ≥100 compressions/min, depth ⅓ of the chest · 30:2 if not intubated · 1 breath every 6–8 s if intubated · IV or IO access.
SourceSlides Ch09 S2–S5 · Whitaker pp. 291–293

T20Know about SIDS

  • SIDS = sudden, unexplained death during sleep of an infant under 1 year. The diagnosis is made at autopsy.
  • Pattern: ages 2–4 months, winter, at night, males more than females.
  • Risk factors: stomach sleeping, maternal smoking (the strongest), prematurity and low birth weight, soft bedding, bed sharing.
  • Prevention: "Back to Sleep" (supine), a smoke-free home, firm bedding.
Watch outThe slide says "place infant in prone position." The book says prone is the #1 risk and infants should sleep on their backs. Ask your instructor which they will accept.
SourceSlides Ch09 S26 · Whitaker pp. 317–319

T21Differences in the anatomy of the pediatric patient compared to an adult

  • Airway: larger tongue · larger, floppy, U-shaped epiglottis · larynx higher and more anterior · narrowest at the cricoid · narrow trachea · mainstem bronchi with less angle (aspiration can go either side).
  • Chest: ribs and sternum mostly cartilage, an unstable thorax and large abdominal contents → the diaphragm does the work, with low reserve. Infants are obligate nose breathers.
  • Body: larger BSA (heat and water loss) · about 80% water · higher metabolic rate and O₂ need · immature skin and blood–brain barrier · large head, less soft-tissue protection.
SourceSlides Ch05 S2–S5, Ch10 S2–S3 · Whitaker pp. 128–130, 328–330

D15Body Surface Area

  • Children have a proportionately larger body surface area, which means more heat loss, more water loss, and a larger %BSA per injury.
  • Burns: BSA is estimated with the Rule of Nines (adult) or the age-adjusted chart (a child's head is about 19% at birth vs 8% in the adult).
  • Parkland formula: 4 mL × kg × %BSA; half in the first 8 h.
SourceSlides Ch10 S2, Ch09 S7, S20 · Whitaker pp. 130, 310–312

D30Upper Airway Hazards

  • Epiglottitis (bacterial, H. influenzae b, ages 2–6): rapid onset, high fever, drooling, leaning forward, can completely block the airway. Thumb sign on lateral neck X-ray. Give O₂, keep the child calm, intubate (in the OR).
  • Croup (viral, subglottic, 6 months–3 years): cold first, then a barky cough and stridor. Steeple sign. Humidity, fluids, observation; racemic epinephrine and steroids if worse.
  • Foreign body: sudden dry, hacking cough. Diagnosed by X-ray; removed by bronchoscopy.
  • Smoke/CO and chlorine inhalation: immediate O₂; watch for airway damage. CO is diagnosed by CO-oximetry, not pulse ox.
  • Congenital: choanal atresia (oral airway or intubation) and Pierre Robin (jaw too small; tongue blocks the airway).
SourceSlides Ch09 S14–S18, Ch08 S19–S20 · Whitaker pp. 267–269, 302–310

T22Eating disorders of the pediatric patient

  • Pica: eating non-food items (dirt, paint, hair). Risks are nutrient deficiency and lead poisoning.
  • Anorexia nervosa: refusing to eat in pursuit of thinness.
  • Bulimia nervosa: binge eating, then purging (vomiting or laxatives). Look for eroded tooth enamel.
  • Anorexia and bulimia are most common in adolescent girls, with a distorted body image and perfectionism.
SourceSlides Ch11 S7 · Whitaker pp. 360–361

T23Neurobehavioral problems of the pediatric patient

  • Developmental delays: global developmental delay (2+ areas delayed) · autism spectrum disorder · ADHD (inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive or combined) · Asperger's (higher functioning, normal intelligence).
  • Behavioral problems: sleep problems · obstructive sleep apnea · oppositional defiant disorder (chronic defiance and anger) · conduct disorder (aggression, cruelty, destruction, stealing).
  • Assessment: language, fine and gross motor, personal-social and cognitive development; the neuro exam.
Watch outThe slide shows behavior problems "resulting in" OSA; the book says untreated OSA causes behavior and learning problems.
SourceSlides Ch11 S3–S6 · Whitaker pp. 356–360

D13OSA

  • Obstructive apnea = no airflow despite breathing effort (vs central apnea = no effort).
  • Cause: upper-airway obstruction during sleep, mainly enlarged tonsils and adenoids, obesity, small jaw or large tongue.
  • Diagnosis: polysomnogram (sleep study).
  • Treatment: surgery (remove the obstruction) or nasal CPAP during sleep.
SourceSlides Ch05 S18, Ch07 S36, Ch11 S6 · Whitaker pp. 237–240, 359

D24pH levels

  • Normal (safe) pH: 7.35–7.45 for neonates and children; acceptable 7.30–7.50.
  • Below 7.35 = acidemia (acidosis); above 7.45 = alkalemia.
  • Fetal scalp pH: >7.25 normal · 7.20–7.24 slight asphyxia · <7.20 severe asphyxia.
  • pH <7.25 with PaCO₂ >60 = ventilatory failure.
SourceSlides Ch02 S10, Ch04 S5 · Whitaker pp. 48, 508, 603

D29OSA in the Pediatric

  • Affects 2–4% of children. Main causes: enlarged tonsils and adenoids, and obesity.
  • Signs: loud snoring, disrupted sleep, behavior and learning problems (can look like ADHD), bedwetting, poor growth, high blood pressure.
  • Diagnosis: polysomnogram. Treatment: tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy or nasal CPAP.
SourceSlides Ch11 S6 · Whitaker pp. 239–240, 359
