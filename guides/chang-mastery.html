CH 01

Principles of Mechanical Ventilation

36 must-knows · 53 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineMechanical ventilation supports gas exchange when patients cannot eliminate enough CO2 or maintain oxygenation. The most testable distinction is that increased airway resistance raises peak inspiratory pressure while plateau pressure stays steady, whereas decreased compliance can raise both.

Airway resistance

  1. FormulaSimplified Poiseuille relationship: ΔP ∝ flow/r⁴. Halving airway radius at fixed flow requires 16-fold driving pressure.p. 3 · Q 4
  2. NumberNormal adult Raw: 0.5–2.5 cm H2O/L/sec. Intubation raises Raw because the endotracheal tube has a smaller diameter.p. 3 · Q 2, 5
  3. FormulaRaw = (peak inspiratory pressure − plateau pressure)/flow, in cm H2O/L/sec. Example: (36 − 24)/1.5 = 8.0 cm H2O/L/sec. Doubling Raw at fixed ΔP halves flow.p. 4 · Q 9, 10
  4. CompareP-V loop bowing indicates increased airflow resistance: inspiratory-limb bowing reflects excessive inspiratory flow; expiratory-limb bowing reflects increased expiratory resistance (e.g., bronchospasm).p. 5 · Q 13, 14
  5. RulePressure support ventilation (PSV) compensates for airflow resistance (ET tube, circuit) and augments spontaneous tidal volume.p. 4 · Q 8

Lung compliance

  1. RuleObtain plateau pressure with an inspiratory hold or by occluding the exhalation port at end-inspiration.p. 6 · Q 16
  2. TrapBronchospasm → ↑ peak inspiratory pressure with steady plateau pressure → ↓ dynamic compliance with steady static compliance.p. 8 · Q 20
  3. FormulaCompliance = ΔV/ΔP (mL/cm H2O): corrected VT divided by (plateau pressure − PEEP) for static, or (peak pressure − PEEP) for dynamic. Static example: 480/(21 − 5) = 30.p. 10 · Q 17, 20
  4. NumberCritically ill patients: static compliance 40–60 mL/cm H2O; dynamic compliance 30–40 mL/cm H2O.p. 10 · Q 23
  5. CompareStatic compliance reflects lung/chest wall elastic resistance (↓ in ARDS, atelectasis, tension pneumothorax, obesity, retained secretions); dynamic compliance adds airway resistance (↓ in bronchospasm, kinked tube, airway obstruction).p. 7 · Q 19
  6. CompareHigh compliance (e.g., emphysema) loses elastic recoil → incomplete exhalation, air trapping, ↑ FRC/TLC, and deeper, slower breathing; low compliance (restrictive disease) reduces FRC with shallow, rapid breathing.p. 7 · Q 11, 18
  7. RuleOn a P-V loop, slope shifting toward the pressure axis indicates decreased compliance (higher ΔP for the same VT); shifting toward the volume axis indicates increased compliance.p. 9 · Q 21

Deadspace ventilation

  1. NumberEstimate anatomic deadspace at 1 mL/lb ideal body weight. At fixed deadspace, ↓ VT raises VD/VT: 150/500 = 30% versus 150/300 = 50%.p. 11 · Q 25, 26
  2. ConceptPhysiologic deadspace = anatomic + alveolar deadspace. It approximates anatomic deadspace normally and rises when ventilated alveoli lose perfusion.p. 11 · Q 24, 27
  3. FormulaPhysiologic VD/VT = (PaCO2 − mixed expired PCO2)/PaCO2. Use simultaneous samples with pressures in mm Hg. Example: (50 − 30)/50 = 40%.p. 12 · Q 28
  4. NumberDuring mechanical ventilation, VD/VT <60% is acceptable and suggests adequate ventilatory function for weaning.p. 12 · Q 29

Ventilatory failure and hypoventilation

  1. ConceptVentilatory failure occurs when CO2 removal cannot match production → ↑ PaCO2 → respiratory acidosis.p. 12 · Q 12
  2. FormulaAlveolar volume per breath = VT − VD. Minute alveolar ventilation = (VT − VD) × breaths/min. Example: (500 − 150) mL × 12 breaths/min = 4.2 L/min.p. 13 · Q 31
  3. RuleAt constant CO2 production, ↓ minute alveolar ventilation → ↑ PaCO2. If a low spontaneous rate causes hypoventilation, increase the ventilator rate.p. 13 · Q 32
  4. NumberHypoventilation criterion: PaCO2 >45 mm Hg, or >50 mm Hg in patients with COPD.p. 13 · Q 32

V/Q mismatch and shunt

  1. NumberGravity increases perfusion at the lung base. V/Q rises from about 0.4 in the lower zone to 3.0 in the upper zone.p. 14 · Q 34
  2. NumberNormal physiologic shunt: <5% in healthy individuals and <10% in noncritically ill patients.p. 14 · Q 36
  3. FormulaEstimated shunt fraction uses arterial blood. Set D = CcO2 − CaO2. Noncritical: D/(5 + D). Critically ill: D/(3.5 + D).p. 15 · Q 37
  4. FormulaClassic shunt fraction = (CcO2 − CaO2)/(CcO2 − mixed venous O2 content). Obtain arterial blood and mixed venous blood from the pulmonary artery.p. 15 · Q 37
  5. RuleRecruit collapsed or fluid-filled lung units causing intrapulmonary shunt with PEEP during mechanical ventilation or CPAP during spontaneous breathing.p. 15 · Q 39
  6. NumberHospitalized patients: shunt <10% normal, 10–20% mild, 20–30% significant, >30% critical. A shunt of 25% is significant.p. 15 · Q 38
  7. CompareUncomplicated V/Q mismatch hypoxemia reverses readily with O2 and, given sufficient reserve, triggers hyperventilation; intrapulmonary shunting (wasted perfusion) causes refractory hypoxemia unresponsive to medium-to-high inspired O2.p. 14 · Q 15, 35

Diffusion and oxygenation failure

  1. NumberPersistent diffusion defect: gas diffusion rate <75% of predicted normal.p. 13 · Q 33
  2. NumberO2 diffuses from alveoli to pulmonary capillary blood along a 109 − 40 = 69 mm Hg pressure gradient.p. 15 · Q 40
  3. NumberCO2 diffuses from pulmonary capillary blood to alveoli across a 6 mm Hg gradient. Its diffusion coefficient is 19 times that of O2.p. 16 · Q 41
  4. FormulaCaO2 = (Hb × 1.34 × SaO2) + (PaO2 × 0.003). Anemia can lower hemoglobin-bound oxygen and CaO2 despite normal PaO2.p. 17 · Q 46, 47
  5. NumberAdult hypoxemia (PaO2 and PB in mm Hg): PB 760 normal 80–100, mild 60–79, moderate 40–59; PB 630 normal 60–79, mild 50–59, moderate 40–49; both severe <40.p. 17 · Q 44, 45, 48
  6. NumberOxygenation failure may develop when severe hypoxemia (PaO2 <40 mm Hg) persists despite 50–100% supplemental oxygen.p. 17 · Q 49
  7. CompareDiffusion slows with ↓ PIO2/O2 gradient (altitude, fire → hypoxic hypoxia in tissues), thickened membrane (pulmonary edema, retained secretions), ↓ surface area (emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis), or insufficient time (tachycardia).p. 16 · Q 42, 43

Clinical indications for ventilation

  1. CompareMechanisms leading to ventilatory support: depressed drive (drug overdose), excessive workload (airflow obstruction), and ventilatory pump failure (chest trauma).p. 18 · Q 50
  2. RuleAcute C1-C3 spinal cord injury in a tetraplegic patient can cause respiratory paralysis.p. 19 · Q 51
Notes on the review questions
  • The question generalizes 30% of tidal volume as normal. The prose uses deadspace ventilation as its denominator, while the numerical example establishes 30% of tidal volume for 150 mL of deadspace and a 500 mL tidal volume.
CH 02

Effects of Positive Pressure Ventilation

31 must-knows · 53 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom linePositive pressure ventilation moves gas by raising airway pressure, and the pressure transmitted to the chest can change blood flow and organ perfusion. The most testable chain is ↑ mean airway pressure or PEEP → ↑ intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return, stroke volume, cardiac output, and oxygen delivery.

Pulmonary mechanics

  1. CompareNegative-pressure inspiration lowers airway, alveolar, and pleural pressures. With atmospheric pressure set to 0, the spontaneous expiration example has alveolar pressure +5 cm H2O.p. 28 · Q 1, 2
  2. FormulaPositive-pressure ventilation: ΔP = airway-opening pressure − alveolar pressure, in cm H2O. Expiratory example: 0 − 20 = −20 cm H2O → outward flow.p. 29 · Q 6
  3. TrapPressure control presets PIP. Low compliance or high resistance can limit tidal volume when target pressure is reached early. A cuff or circuit leak can prevent reaching PIP.p. 29 · Q 4, 5
  4. CompareVolume control presets tidal volume and allows pressure to vary. PIP and mean airway pressure rise with ↑ tidal volume, resistance, or peak inspiratory flow and with ↓ compliance.p. 30 · Q 3, 7

Cardiovascular and hemodynamic effects

  1. CompareNormal compliance transmits about 50% of airway pressure to the thorax. Stiff lungs transmit less, so the same PIP or PEEP depresses cardiac output less.p. 30 · Q 8, 9
  2. RuleMinimize inspiratory time, respiratory rate, PIP, and PEEP to limit mean airway pressure and preserve cardiac output. PEEP treats refractory hypoxemia from intrapulmonary shunting.p. 30 · Q 10, 11
  3. Formula↑ Intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return → ↓ stroke volume and cardiac output. Oxygen delivery = blood oxygen content × cardiac output, yielding oxygen volume per unit time.p. 31 · Q 12
  4. ComparePulsus paradoxus: spontaneous inspiratory systolic drop >10 mm Hg. Positive-pressure ventilation causes reverse pulsus paradoxus (systolic rise via ↓ left ventricular afterload); >15 mm Hg indicates hypovolemia.p. 31 · Q 13, 14, 15
  5. CompareLarge tidal volumes ↓ left ventricular filling in hypotension and ↑ it in hypertension. During expiration, the thoracic pump increases blood flow from the right to the left ventricle.p. 32 · Q 16, 17
  6. NumberIn children with right ventricular dysfunction, pressures up to 40 cm H2O and tidal volumes of 20–30 mL/kg may unload the right heart via the thoracic pump.p. 34 · Q 18
  7. NumberAt 1 L tidal volume, an estimated 15–20% of pulmonary blood volume shifts systemically. Systemic venous compensation limits the change in pulmonary capillary wedge pressure.p. 34 · Q 19, 20, 23
  8. RuleAdding PEEP ↑ pulmonary artery and central venous pressures and ↓ aortic pressure and cardiac output. Intravascular volume expansion and positive inotropes can limit the output decline.p. 34 · Q 21, 25

Renal perfusion and clearance

  1. NumberRenal hypoperfusion reduces filtration and urine output. Oliguria is <400 mL in 24 hours or <160 mL in 8 hours.p. 36 · Q 24
  2. NumberNormal GFR is 90–120 mL/min/1.73 m². GFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m² indicates renal failure, which usually raises serum potassium and lowers serum sodium.p. 37 · Q 29, 30
  3. RuleRenal failure can prolong paralysis after pancuronium or vecuronium and delay weaning. Vecuronium-related paralysis may last up to 7 days after more than 2 days of use.p. 36 · Q 26
  4. Concept↓ Renal perfusion → ↓ filtration and tubular secretion → ↑ circulating drug levels. Tubular secretion clears digoxin, furosemide, procainamide, and some penicillins.p. 37 · Q 27, 28
  5. Concept↓ Cardiac output → ↓ renal blood flow and urine volume → concentrated filtrate → ↑ reabsorption of aminoglycosides, theophylline, and phenobarbital.p. 38 · Q 32
  6. NumberThe kidneys receive about 25% of circulating blood volume at any one time, making renal function highly vulnerable to ventilator-induced decreases in blood flow.p. 35 · Q 22
  7. ConceptGlomerular filtration pressure: afferent arteriole kept somewhat dilated vs the efferent arteriole, which is always somewhat constricted.p. 37 · Q 27

Hepatic perfusion and clearance

  1. NumberHepatic perfusion is about 15% of cardiac output. In one study, PEEP of 10, 15, and 20 cm H2O reduced hepatic flow by 3%, 12%, and 32%, respectively.p. 38 · Q 33
  2. RuleProthrombin time >4 seconds above control suggests liver impairment. Hepatic hypoperfusion can raise serum lidocaine, meperidine, propranolol, and verapamil.p. 38 · Q 31, 34, 35

Abdominal and gastrointestinal effects

  1. RuleMeasure intra-abdominal pressure through a bladder catheter. Use caution with PEEP >15 cm H2O when intra-abdominal pressure is >20 mm Hg because pressure on the heart and great vessels increases.p. 39 · Q 36, 37, 38
  2. ConceptHigh PEEP raises right atrial pressure, narrowing its gradient with mean systemic venous pressure and reducing cardiac output and gastrointestinal perfusion. Morphine can cause constipation and vomiting.p. 40 · Q 39, 40

Nutrition and respiratory muscles

  1. NumberWork of breathing costs 430–720 kcal/day in COPD versus 38–72 kcal/day normally. High airway resistance and low lung or chest wall compliance promote mechanical fatigue.p. 41 · Q 41, 42
  2. RuleProlonged ventilation → diaphragm proteolysis, loss of myofiber content, and time-dependent loss of force. Start weaning as soon as feasible.p. 41 · Q 43
  3. FormulaTotal energy needs = resting energy expenditure × activity or stress factor. Use 1.2–1.6 for severe infection and 1.5–2.1 for burns.p. 42 · Q 45
  4. RuleGlucose supplies 40–60% of total parenteral nutrition calories. Consider fat-based nutrition for CO2 retention or fluid restriction: fat emulsion 9.1 kcal/g versus hydrous dextrose 3.4 kcal/g.p. 42 · Q 46, 47
  5. ConceptFrequent interruptions in enteral feedings often cause undernutrition in ventilated patients, leading to protein catabolism and depleted diaphragm glycogen and protein stores.p. 42 · Q 44

Neurologic effects

  1. RuleHyperventilation <24 hours → respiratory alkalosis → ↓ cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure. After 24 hours, buffering restores pH toward normal and reverses the vasoconstrictor effect.p. 43 · Q 49, 50
  2. ConceptProlonged hyperventilation shifts the oxyhemoglobin curve left, reducing tissue oxygen release. Intracellular phosphate shift → hypophosphatemia → ↓ ATP and 2,3-BPG → further left shift.p. 43 · Q 48, 51
  3. CompareHypercapnia with normal pH ↑ cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure. With low pH, cerebral metabolism is impaired. Cerebral vasodilation and elevated intracranial pressure can cause papilledema.p. 44 · Q 52, 53
CH 03

Classification of Mechanical Ventilators

37 must-knows · 62 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineClassify ventilators by how they turn input power into ventilatory work, what they control, and how they deliver each breath. The most testable distinction is that the trigger starts inspiration, a limit caps a variable during inspiration, and the cycle variable ends inspiration.

Ventilatory work and hardware

  1. FormulaMuscle pressure + ventilator pressure (each cm H2O) = volume (L) ÷ compliance (L/cm H2O) [elastic load] + resistance (cm H2O/L/sec) × flow (L/sec) [resistance load].p. 53 · Q 3, 5
  2. FormulaCompliance (L/cm H2O) = change in volume (L) ÷ change in pressure (cm H2O).p. 53
  3. NumberPneumatic power uses anhydrous, oil-free compressed medical gas at 50 psi.p. 53 · Q 7
  4. NumberElectrical input power can be 120 V AC at 60 Hz or 12 V DC.p. 53 · Q 8
  5. ConceptPrimary drive mechanisms are pistons, bellows, and pneumatic circuits.p. 54 · Q 10
  6. ConceptClosed-loop control uses continuous feedback to adjust input and maintain the target output. This is servo control.p. 56 · Q 14
  7. CompareFull ventilatory support means the ventilator performs all work (patient muscles contribute none); zero support means patient muscles sustain all requirements; partial support lies between.p. 53 · Q 4, 6
  8. ConceptThe four control circuits are mechanical (open-loop levers, pulleys, cams), pneumatic, fluidic (Coanda effect attaches a high-velocity gas jet to an adjacent wall), and electronic.p. 56 · Q 15, 17
  9. ConceptChatburn classifies ventilators by related features, physics, and engineering, not manufacturer terminology.p. 52 · Q 2
  10. ConceptMicroprocessor-controlled pneumatic drive: proportional solenoid valves open variably to create selectable flow and pressure waveforms.p. 55 · Q 13
  11. ConceptPneumatic control circuits (IPPB, Percussionaire IPV/VDR) use valves, nozzles, ducted ejectors, and diaphragms.p. 56 · Q 16
  12. TrapPositive or negative pressure ventilator, the lungs expand because of positive transrespiratory system pressure.p. 58 · Q 21

Control variables

  1. FormulaA pressure controller regulates transrespiratory pressure (cm H2O) = airway pressure − body surface pressure.p. 57 · Q 19, 21
  2. TrapA true volume controller must measure volume directly (e.g., piston or bellows displacement) for feedback; ventilators that derive volume from flow are flow controllers.p. 59 · Q 22
  3. CompareTime controllers regulate inspiratory and expiratory time while pressure and volume vary. Operating a flow-measuring ventilator in pressure support or pressure control makes it a pressure controller.p. 59 · Q 23, 24

Breath phase variables

  1. FormulaTime trigger interval (sec/breath) = 60 sec/min ÷ set frequency (breaths/min). At 12 breaths/min, 60 ÷ 12 = 5 sec/breath.p. 60 · Q 25, 26
  2. NumberPressure-trigger change from baseline is −1 to −5 cm H2O. A more negative setting decreases sensitivity and requires more patient effort.p. 60 · Q 27, 28
  3. RuleWith auto-PEEP, the patient must overcome both auto-PEEP and the set sensitivity to pressure-trigger a breath.p. 60 · Q 29
  4. ConceptFlow triggering combines continuous flow and demand flow to reduce inspiratory effort: patient inspiration diverts circuit flow, dropping return flow below delivered flow to trigger a breath.p. 61 · Q 30, 31
  5. TrapReaching an inspiratory limit holds that variable at its preset value while inspiration continues. Reaching the cycling criterion ends inspiration.p. 62 · Q 32
  6. FormulaVolume (L) = flow (L/sec) × inspiratory time (sec). Flow controllers that derive volume from flow and time without measuring volume directly are time-cycled, not volume-cycled.p. 62 · Q 20, 33
  7. RuleWhen titrating PEEP or CPAP, monitor hemodynamics, blood gases or oximetry, and compliance.p. 63 · Q 35
  8. ConceptThe baseline variable is controlled during expiration—most commonly pressure. PEEP and CPAP apply positive pressure above ambient baseline during exhalation to increase FRC and prevent alveolar collapse.p. 63 · Q 34

Ventilation modes

  1. FormulaVolume control fixes tidal volume. Minute ventilation (L/min) = tidal volume (L/breath) × frequency (breaths/min).p. 66 · Q 41
  2. ComparePressure control fixes inspiratory pressure. ↓ Compliance or ↑ airway resistance → ↓ tidal volume and minute ventilation.p. 66 · Q 42
  3. NumberPressure support holds preset inspiratory pressure and cycles to expiration when flow falls to 10%–40% of peak inspiratory flow.p. 67 · Q 44
  4. TrapDual control within a breath starts in pressure control and switches to volume control during that same inspiration.p. 68 · Q 45
  5. NumberVC+ and PRVC adjust inspiratory pressure between breaths in 1–3 cm H2O increments toward the tidal-volume target, within the upper pressure limit.p. 68 · Q 46
  6. NumberAutomode switches from volume support back to PRVC after apnea lasts 12 seconds in adults or 8 seconds in children.p. 69 · Q 48
  7. RuleIn APRV, initially set the higher pressure above the lower inflection point of the lung’s pressure–volume curve.p. 70 · Q 51
  8. ComparePAV amplifies pressure proportionally to inspiratory flow and volume. ATC offsets endotracheal or tracheostomy tube resistance during both inspiration and expiration, reducing air trapping and intrinsic PEEP.p. 69 · Q 49, 50

Waveforms and alarms

  1. ConceptWith volume control, an ascending-ramp volume waveform produces constant rectangular inspiratory flow. Flow is the change in volume per unit time.p. 74 · Q 58
  2. RuleControl circuit alarms flag incompatible settings or failed self-diagnostics. A self-diagnostic failure can leave the ventilator inoperative.p. 75 · Q 60
  3. TrapOutput flow alarms are limited to exhaled minute volume; some ventilators allow both high and low limits.p. 76 · Q 61
  4. ConceptPressure-controlled waveforms are rectangular, exponential, sinusoidal, or oscillating. Ventilators with a rotary-driven piston drive mechanism produce sinusoidal pressure, volume, and flow waveforms.p. 72 · Q 55, 57
  5. NumberAC power loss alarm: most battery backup alarms run on rechargeable nickel cadmium batteries, recharged while AC power is on.p. 75 · Q 59
  6. RuleOutput gas alarms (exhaled O2, end-tidal CO2) help determine VD/VT, gas exchange, and respiratory exchange ratio (R).p. 76 · Q 62
CH 04

Operating Modes of Mechanical Ventilation

61 must-knows · 113 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineIdentify each breath by what starts inspiration, what the ventilator controls during inspiration, and what ends it. The most testable distinction is that patient triggering can start a mandatory breath in assist/control or SIMV, while pressure support assists a spontaneous breath and cycles off at a flow threshold.

Pressure gradients and mode classification

  1. FormulaTransairway pressure PTA = PAO − PALV, in cm H₂O. Equal airway-opening and alveolar pressures at end-exhalation produce zero flow.p. 82 · Q 1, 2, 3
  2. CompareNegative-pressure ventilation lowers alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure and generally needs no artificial airway unless obstruction is present. Positive-pressure ventilation raises airway-opening pressure above atmospheric pressure.p. 83 · Q 4, 5, 10
  3. ConceptIron-lung tank shock occurs when negative pressure around peripheral veins ↓ venous return → ↓ cardiac output. A cuirass improves access but requires an airtight chest-wall seal.p. 83 · Q 6, 7
  4. ConceptTrigger starts inspiration, control delivers the breath, and cycle ends inspiration. Continuous spontaneous breaths may receive assistance with PSV or remain unassisted with CPAP.p. 85 · Q 13, 14, 15
  5. CompareServo adjusts output to patient demand (PAV). Adaptive changes one setpoint to reach another (PRVC). Optimal uses a mathematical model to select settings (ASV).p. 85 · Q 16, 17, 18
  6. CompareSimple volume control delivers set VT with constant flow. Closed-loop PSV varies flow to maintain set pressure as airway and lung characteristics change.p. 86 · Q 19, 20
  7. RuleIn spontaneous breathing, the patient determines frequency and VT. Verify apnea backup, which delivers preset VT, frequency, and FiO₂ during apnea or an extremely low rate.p. 87 · Q 21, 22, 23
  8. ConceptCustom-fitted chest cuirass ventilators minimize air leaks in chest-wall diseases such as scoliosis and suit home care because they are easy to maintain without an artificial airway.p. 84 · Q 8, 9
  9. RuleEvery ventilator operating mode should achieve four main goals: adequate ventilation and oxygenation, avoidance of ventilator-induced lung injury, patient-ventilator synchrony, and successful weaning.p. 84 · Q 11

PEEP, CPAP, and BiPAP

  1. RuleApply PEEP for shunt with refractory hypoxemia, reduced FRC with reduced compliance, or auto-PEEP persisting despite ventilator adjustments.p. 87 · Q 24
  2. FormulaRefractory hypoxemia guideline: PaO₂ ≤60 mm Hg on FiO₂ ≥50%. P/F = PaO₂ ÷ fractional FiO₂: 60 ÷ 0.50 = 120 mm Hg. ARDS oxygenation cutoff: <200 mm Hg.p. 88 · Q 25, 26
  3. RuleTo lengthen expiration in air trapping, ↑ peak inspiratory flow or ↓ frequency or VT. For persistent auto-PEEP, extrinsic PEEP slightly below auto-PEEP reduces triggering effort.p. 88 · Q 24, 27, 29
  4. FormulaRequired negative trigger-pressure magnitude = auto-PEEP + pressure sensitivity: 6 + 2 = 8 cm H₂O. A pressure-triggered assisted breath begins with a negative deflection.p. 88 · Q 28, 30
  5. FormulaNormal end-expiratory alveolar distending pressure = alveolar − pleural pressure = 0 − (−5) = 5 cm H₂O. PEEP recruits alveoli → ↑ FRC, ↓ shunt, ↑ oxygenation.p. 89 · Q 31, 32
  6. RulePEEP makes pleural pressure less negative → ↓ venous return → ↓ cardiac output. If BP falls, assess hypovolemia first. If volume is adequate, reduce PEEP.p. 90 · Q 33, 34, 35
  7. NumberBarotrauma is more frequent with PEEP >10 cm H₂O, mean airway pressure >30 cm H₂O, or PIP >50 cm H₂O. Unexplained crepitus signals barotrauma.p. 90 · Q 36, 37
  8. NumberNormal/high compliance transmits PEEP more readily: venous drainage can fall and ICP can rise above 8–12 cm H₂O. Kidneys receive approximately 25% of circulating blood volume.p. 90 · Q 35, 38, 39
  9. RuleCPAP is PEEP during spontaneous breathing. Use PEEP indications plus the ability to sustain normal PaCO₂ spontaneously. Nasal CPAP is preferred in neonates.p. 91 · Q 12, 40, 41
  10. CompareBiPAP: IPAP aids ventilation; EPAP raises FRC and reduces shunt. A wider IPAP−EPAP gradient increases VT. Equal pressures produce CPAP; EPAP cannot exceed IPAP.p. 92 · Q 42, 48, 49
  11. NumberFor spontaneous BiPAP, start IPAP 8 and EPAP 4 cm H₂O; target VT 5–7 mL/kg. Raise IPAP for ventilation or EPAP for shunt oxygenation in 2 cm H₂O increments.p. 92 · Q 44, 47
  12. NumberBiPAP spontaneous/timed backup is 2–5/min below spontaneous rate; timed rate is slightly above spontaneous rate. Timed %IPAP of 33% or 50% gives I:E 1:2 or 1:1, respectively.p. 92 · Q 45, 46
  13. RuleBiPAP helps prevent intubation in end-stage COPD and supports chronic ventilatory failure, restrictive chest-wall disease, neuromuscular disease, and nocturnal hypoventilation.p. 91 · Q 43

CMV, assist/control, IMV, and SIMV

  1. FormulaVolume CMV time-triggers preset breaths: minute ventilation = rate × VT; 10/min × 800 mL = 8,000 mL/min. Use medication to suppress effort; disabling trigger sensitivity leaves inspiratory efforts unsupported.p. 93 · Q 50, 56
  2. RuleCMV may be indicated for crushed chest with paradoxical movement or seizures interrupting ventilation. Active exhalation can trigger early high-pressure cycling and reduce delivered VT.p. 93 · Q 51, 52, 62
  3. RuleCMV disconnection or failure risks apnea and hypoxia. Prioritize low exhaled-volume and low inspiratory-pressure alarms. Marked diaphragm atrophy was reported after 18–69 hours of complete inactivity.p. 94 · Q 53, 54, 55
  4. TrapVolume assist/control delivers preset VT on patient- or time-triggered breaths. At 12 assisted/min (5 sec) versus 10 backup/min (6 sec), all breaths are patient-triggered.p. 95 · Q 57, 58, 62
  5. NumberAssist/control: stable spontaneous drive is typically ≥10–12/min. Set backup 2–4/min below assist frequency, or use a minimum of 8–10/min. Match or exceed inspiratory flow demand.p. 95 · Q 59, 60
  6. RuleAssist/control at >20–25 assisted breaths/min with VT 10–15 mL/kg can cause hypocapnia and respiratory alkalosis. Switch to a mode such as SIMV to limit excessive minute ventilation.p. 96 · Q 61
  7. CompareIMV mandatory breaths can stack with spontaneous breaths; set appropriate high-pressure limits. SIMV synchronizes mandatory delivery with patient effort when it occurs inside the synchronization window.p. 97 · Q 63, 64
  8. FormulaAt 10 SIMV breaths/min with a 0.5-sec synchronization window, it opens at 60 ÷ 10 − 0.5 = 5.5 sec; without effort, time-trigger at 6 sec.p. 98 · Q 64
  9. CompareSIMV allows patient-controlled spontaneous rate and VT between mandatory breaths. Spontaneous breaths distribute ventilation more evenly; lower PIP and shorter inspiratory time tend to lower mean airway pressure.p. 99 · Q 65, 66, 67, 68, 70
  10. RuleDuring SIMV weaning without PSV, decrease mandatory frequency slowly and monitor for fatigue. Reducing the rate too quickly increases spontaneous work and risks weaning failure.p. 100 · Q 69
  11. ConceptSIMV spontaneous breaths distribute VT more evenly than positive pressure breaths (which hyperinflate upper zones) → ↓ alveolar deadspace ventilation.p. 99 · Q 67

MMV and pressure support

  1. FormulaSIMV total minute ventilation = mandatory rate × mandatory VT + spontaneous minute ventilation. Example: 4/min × 0.8 L + 6 L/min = 9.2 L/min.p. 101 · Q 71
  2. CompareBelow the MMV target, most ventilators increase SIMV mandatory rate. Hamilton Veolar uses pure PSV and automatically raises pressure support to reach the minimum.p. 102 · Q 72, 75, 76
  3. TrapRapid shallow breathing can satisfy MMV while alveolar ventilation falls. Set the high-frequency alarm approximately 10 breaths/min above baseline spontaneous frequency.p. 101 · Q 73, 74
  4. FormulaIn PSV, PIP = pressure support above baseline + PEEP. Example: 30 + 5 = 35 cm H₂O.p. 102 · Q 78
  5. TrapPSV is patient-triggered, pressure-limited, and flow-cycled. Decelerating flow ends inspiration at the set threshold, e.g., 5 L/min or 25% of peak flow.p. 103 · Q 79, 80, 85
  6. NumberWith SIMV, PSV supports spontaneous breaths only. Titration endpoints include VT 10–15 mL/kg, spontaneous rate ≤25/min, or reduced work from endotracheal tube resistance.p. 103 · Q 77, 81, 82
  7. FormulaEstimate airway resistance as (PIP − plateau pressure) ÷ mean inspiratory flow. Pressures in cm H₂O and flow in L/sec yield cm H₂O/(L/sec).p. 103 · Q 83
  8. ConceptPressure support breath length is set by the patient: it flow-cycles, so inspiratory time depends on spontaneous inspiratory flow demand.p. 104 · Q 85

Adaptive, proportional, and volume-targeted modes

  1. FormulaASV target minute ventilation = selected fraction × baseline × body weight. Adults 100, children 200 mL/min/kg; select 20–200%. Adult 160%: 1.60 × 100 = 160 mL/min/kg.p. 104 · Q 84, 87
  2. FormulaASV test breaths measure compliance, airway resistance, and intrinsic PEEP. The Otis Equation selects minimum-work frequency; optimal VT (L) = minute ventilation (L/min) ÷ frequency (/min).p. 104 · Q 18, 86, 88
  3. FormulaAs ASV patient triggering increases, mandatory breaths decrease and pressure support increases. Target VT = alveolar volume + estimated deadspace, calculated as 2.2 mL/kg × body weight.p. 105 · Q 89
  4. FormulaPAV flow assist offsets resistance; volume assist offsets elastance. Elastic assist pressure = assist fraction × elastance × volume: 80% × 40 cm H₂O/L × 1 L = 32 cm H₂O.p. 105 · Q 16, 90, 91
  5. RulePAV delivers assisted breaths until volume or flow demand is met. Sudden improvement in elastance or resistance can make positive-feedback pressure excessive → overdistension, air trapping, barotrauma.p. 106 · Q 92, 93
  6. TrapVAPS presets minimum VT and pressure support. A volume shortfall switches the same breath to constant flow until target VT is reached, prolonging inspiration and risking air trapping.p. 107 · Q 94, 95

Initiation of Mechanical Ventilation

41 must-knows · 65 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineRecognize acute or impending ventilatory failure, severe hypoxemia, and the need for prophylactic support. The most testable distinction is that acute failure pairs a sudden PaCO2 rise with respiratory acidosis, whereas impending failure can begin with normal or low PaCO2 despite excessive work of breathing. Choose initial settings for the patient's physiology, then reassess gas exchange, airway pressures, and hemodynamics.

Indications and contraindications

  1. RuleAcute failure: sudden PaCO2 >50 mm Hg with pH <7.30. In COPD, look for an acute rise above the patient's baseline with decompensating respiratory acidosis.p. 214 · Q 2, 3, 6
  2. TrapPaO2 50–60 mm Hg or SaO2 85–90% alone does not establish acute ventilatory failure. With adequate ventilation, consider oxygen, CPAP, or BiPAP.p. 214 · Q 4
  3. ConceptImpending failure: early ↑ minute ventilation can keep PaCO2 normal or low. Sustained excessive work → muscle fatigue → rising PaCO2 >50 mm Hg and falling pH.p. 216 · Q 7, 9
  4. NumberImpending failure clues: spontaneous VT <3–5 mL/kg, f >30/min, minute volume >10 L/min, VC <15 mL/kg. Rapid shallow breathing increases the deadspace fraction.p. 217 · Q 9, 10, 11, 12

Bottom lineTrend bedside findings, blood gases, oximetry, and capnography together before changing ventilator settings. Vital Signs

Pressure-Controlled Ventilation and PSV

  1. CompareIn PCV, pressure, rate, and TI or I:E are preset. Breaths may be time- or patient-triggered. Flow and VT vary with mechanics and effort; descending flow may be concave.p. 338 · Q 4, 75, 77, 78, 95
  2. FormulaIRPCV uses TI > TE for severe hypoxemia or ARDS. Sedation and paralysis are often used. With passive zero end-inspiratory flow and the expiratory valve closed, CLT = expired VT/(PIP − total PEEP).p. 339 · Q 79, 80, 81, 82, 84

  1. ConceptBoth respiratory muscle disuse and excessive WOB delay weaning; dyssynchrony → ↑ WOB, ↑ O2 consumption, ↑ minute ventilation, and ↑ myocardial work.p. 345 · Q 99, 100
  2. RuleTrigger response time spans threshold detection to flow onset; ease weak drive or high demand with higher sensitivity (−1 vs −3 cm H2O), flow triggering, or higher peak flow.p. 346 · Q 101, 102

  4. TrapEarliest signs of ↑ WOB are tachypnea (f > 20/min) and agitation, progressing to accessory muscles, active expiration, and paradoxical breathing; ABG respiratory failure is the LAST sign.p. 347 · Q 106, 107, 108

Expiratory Diagnostics and Circuit Troubleshooting

  1. ConceptDuring constant-flow VCV, severe obstruction raises PTA and PIP, lowers expiratory flow and circuit pressure, and prolongs emptying. Expiratory flow exceeded five seconds in Chang’s example; decompressed circuit gas spiked initially.p. 352 · Q 122, 123, 124
  2. RuleSevere obstruction may require temporary sedation and paralysis for controlled ventilation. Chang gives 6–8 breaths/min and VT 4–7 mL/kg ideal body weight as examples to prevent severe auto-PEEP.p. 354 · Q 125

  4. CompareDuring constant-flow VCV at unchanged VT and resistance, decreased CLT raises plateau and PIP while initial PTA stays unchanged. Higher elastic recoil increases peak expiratory flow and shortens emptying time.p. 355 · Q 128, 129
  5. RuleTrapped volume may appear above baseline and empty below baseline later. Abruptly reduced expiratory flow magnitude and pressure suggest inspiration or obstruction; a sudden increase suggests active expiration or relieved obstruction.p. 357 · Q 130, 131, 133

Ventilation Strategies and Permissive Hypercapnia

  1. Compare↑ f, VT, PSV, or ΔP primarily ↑ ventilation (↓ PaCO2); ↑ FIO2 or PEEP primarily ↑ oxygenation while ventilation stays unchanged or ↓.p. 375 · Q 1
  2. RuleNormal PaCO₂ is 35–45 mm Hg. In COPD, use the patient’s last discharge PaCO₂ (often ~50). Prioritize increasing ventilator frequency (maximum 20/min), increasing spontaneous then ventilator VT, and reducing mechanical deadspace.p. 376 · Q 2, 3, 4
  3. FormulaNew f = (f × PaCO2) / desired PaCO2 (VT, deadspace constant; 12×60/45 = 16/min). Prefer ↑ spontaneous VT over ↑ f (rapid shallow breaths ↑ VD/VT).p. 377 · Q 5, 6
  4. NumberPSV supports spontaneous breaths, including during SIMV. Below 10 cm H₂O offsets tube/circuit resistance. Muscle support usually starts at 10–15. Above 20 resembles PCV. Inadequate VT may cause hypoventilation/atelectasis.p. 378 · Q 7, 8, 9
  5. FormulaPermissive hypercapnia accepts elevated PaCO₂ to lower pulmonary pressures: VT 4–7 mL/kg, Pplat ≤35 cm H₂O. Acidosis raises PVR. THAM lowers CO₂. For metabolic acidosis, 0.3 M THAM dose = kg × base deficit (mEq/L).p. 379 · Q 10, 11, 12, 13

  2. RuleO₂ corrects hypoxemia from uncomplicated V/Q mismatch. Hypoventilation with hypoxemia calls for ventilation plus O₂. Target PaO₂ ~80 mm Hg (lower in COPD), FIO₂ <60%, high-FIO₂ exposure <24 hours.p. 380 · Q 14, 18, 19
  3. RuleImprove perfusion for high-V/Q deadspace. Replace volume losses. In shock with loss of venous tone, use vasopressors and cautious fluids because tone recovery risks overload. Report Hb <10 g/100 mL.p. 382 · Q 15, 20, 21, 22
  4. CompareCPAP/PEEP increase FRC in shunting. CPAP requires sustained spontaneous breathing. Rising PaCO₂ suggests fatigue. Consider APRV after CPAP. Optimal PEEP maximizes oxygenation or compliance at lowest pressure without significant cardiopulmonary complications.p. 383 · Q 16, 23, 24
  5. RuleIf hemodynamically stable with minimal PEEP risk, lower FIO₂ to 40%, then reduce PEEP by 2–3 cm H₂O toward 3, maintaining SpO₂ ≥90%. IRV raises mPaw. ECMO helps infants with severe refractory hypoxemia.p. 384 · Q 25, 26, 27

  9. RuleInverse ratio ventilation prolongs inspiration by ↓ inspiratory flow or ↑ inspiratory pause time.p. 384 · Q 26
  10. NumberAdult HFOV, severe hypoxia: mPaw of 40 cm H2O may be applied for 40–60 seconds.p. 385 · Q 29

Arterial Blood Gases and Acid-Base Traps

  1. CompareCompensated ABG rule (normal pH 7.35–7.45): primary acidosis leans acidotic (e.g., 7.37–7.38), primary alkalosis leans alkalotic (e.g., 7.42–7.43); always correlate with clinical signs.p. 388 · Q 33, 36
  2. TrapTreat hypoxia or metabolic acidosis driving hyperventilation. Hyperventilating COPD patients can mimic partially compensated metabolic alkalosis. Consider ET-to-Y deadspace for persistent respiratory alkalosis when VT and frequency cannot be reduced.p. 388 · Q 34, 35, 37
  3. TrapDelay weaning for fatigue-induced respiratory acidosis rather than ↑ f. Never use ventilator adjustments for primary metabolic acidosis (renal failure, DKA, lactic acidosis) or metabolic alkalosis (hypokalemia).p. 389 · Q 38, 39

Troubleshooting Ventilator Alarms and Auto-PEEP

  1. CompareCircuit/cuff leaks can lower pressure, expired volume, and PEEP. A low-pressure limit above PIP triggers low pressure.   2. RuleHigh frequency alarm tracks total f: caused by tachypnea (↓ E-time, ↑ mPaw) or autotriggering from excessive sensitivity. Most frequent apnea alarm trigger: circuit disconnected from ET tube.p. 392 · Q 43, 44, 45
  3. RuleMeasure auto-PEEP by end-expiratory occlusion under sedation/paralysis. Lower VT/frequency, increase inspiratory flow, treat reversible obstruction. Ventilator Circuit, Artificial Airway, and VAP

  1. FormulaCompressible volume = PIP × circuit compliance (40×3 = 120 mL; 40×5 = 200 mL). Prevent condensation obstruction using heated-wire circuit and inline water trap.p. 395 · Q 50, 51
  2. RuleHME delivers 70–90% RH at 30–31°C; contraindicated with thick/copious secretions, VE >10 L/min, body temp <32°C, or aerosolized meds (place MDI between HME and patient).p. 396 · Q 52, 53, 54
  3. NumberTarget 37°C near the patient in the inspiratory limb. Change humidifier/HME circuits when visibly soiled. Studies favored weekly over more frequent changes. Halving airway radius requires 16-fold pressure for unchanged flow.p. 397 · Q 55, 56, 57
  4. RulePreoxygenate and limit total suction to ≤10 seconds. Closed inline suction helps maintain FIO₂/PEEP. Catheters may be changed weekly. Avoid direct saline instillation to thin secretions or stimulate cough.p. 398 · Q 58, 59
  5. RuleVAP incidence 10–65%, fatality 13–55%. Prevention: handwashing, closed suction, head elevation 30–45°. Pseudomonas stains red on Gram stain, TB acid-fast, Pneumocystis silver. Clinical evidence suggesting a pathogen can justify empiric antibiotics.p. 400 · Q 60, 61, 62, 63, 65

Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

  1. NumberPercentages of body weight: water 60%, ICF 40%, ECF 20% (plasma 5%, interstitial 15%). Oliguria <20 mL/h or <400 mL/day suggests deficit. Ringer’s lactate or 0.9% saline improves HR, BP, urine output.p. 400 · Q 66, 67, 68, 70, 74
  2. RuleECF excess (bounding pulse, ↑ pulmonic P2, pulmonary edema, ↑ UO): restrict fluid or give furosemide (never mannitol—↑ plasma volume first); improvement = clearing pulmonary edema, ↓ pulse intensity.p. 402 · Q 69, 71, 72
  3. FormulaAnion gap = Na+K−Cl−HCO₃: 15–20 mEq/L with K, 10–14 without. Serum means (mEq/L): Na 140, Cl 103, K 4, HCO₃ 25, Ca 5, Mg 2, protein 16, phosphate 2, sulfate 1, organic acids 4.p. 402 · Q 73, 75, 76, 77, 78
  4. CompareFor hypovolemia-associated hyponatremia, Chang describes 100–300 mL of 2.5% or 3% saline. Avoid sodium-free fluid. Hypernatremia after prolonged sodium-containing, dextrose-free IV fluids suggests water deficit. Replace water with dextrose solution.p. 404 · Q 79, 80, 81
  5. RuleHypokalemia (flattened T, ↓ ST; coexists with hypochloremia): give KCl (oral safer). IV KCl precautions: UO ≥40–50 mL/h, never undiluted, ≤40 mEq/h, ≤200 mEq/24 h, drip ≤40 mEq/L.p. 405 · Q 82, 83, 84

Nutrition and Caloric Requirements

  1. CompareUndernutrition weakens respiratory muscles and immunity. Severe hypoalbuminemia lowers osmotic pressure, risking edema. Overfeeding increases CO₂ production and breathing work, risks fatty liver, and may provoke weaning distress with limited pulmonary reserve.p. 406 · Q 87, 88, 89
  2. NumberFat emulsion provides 9.1 kcal/g, hydrous dextrose 3.4. Fat-based diets (≥40% fat calories) reduce CO₂ production. Phosphate <1 mg/dL lowers tissue ATP. Severe deficiency may cause confusion, weakness, CHF, or respiratory failure.p. 407 · Q 90, 91, 93
  3. FormulaREE (kcal/day; kg, cm, years): men 66+13.7W+5H−6.8A, women 655+9.6W+1.85H−4.7A. TEE=REE×activity×stress. Activity: bedbound 1.2, ambulatory/out-of-bed 1.3. Stress: minor operation 1.20, skeletal trauma 1.35, major sepsis 1.60, severe thermal burn 2.10.p. 408 · Q 92, 94, 95

  1. RuleIn volume-targeted ALI/ARDS ventilation, use the lowest VT meeting minimum oxygenation and ventilation needs with Pplat <35 cm H₂O. Low VT risks hypercapnia, increased breathing work, dyspnea, severe acidosis, and atelectasis.p. 409 · Q 96, 97, 98

  3. TrapProne ↓ shunting and rapidly ↑ oxygenation, but does not increase ARDS survival. Contraindications: unstable spine, ↑ ICP, instability, flail chest, recent thoracic/abdominal surgery; complications: brachial plexopathy, accidental extubation.p. 410 · Q 99, 101, 104, 106

Bottom linePharmacotherapy during mechanical ventilation serves two primary goals: ensuring patient comfort and facilitating airway management and ventilation. Adrenergic and Anticholinergic Bronchodilators

  1. CompareRising PIP, wheezing, hypoxemia and agitation suggest airway narrowing. Albuterol/formoterol stimulate β₂ receptors. Ipratropium/atropine block muscarinic responses to acetylcholine, which increases bronchial tone and mucosal secretion.p. 426 · Q 2, 3, 4, 5, 17
  2. ConceptAdrenergic receptors: α-1 causes vasoconstriction and pupil constriction; α-2 ↓ GI activity; β-1 produces ↑ contractility (inotropic) and ↑ heart rate (chronotropic); β-2 produces bronchodilation and peripheral vasodilation.p. 424 · Q 6, 7
  3. CompareCOMT/MAO rapidly degrade enterally ineffective catecholamines. Structural modifications give derivatives such as albuterol greater β₂ selectivity and slower degradation, prolonging bronchodilation with fewer cardiac effects.p. 425 · Q 8, 9
  4. NumberNebulized doses: racemic epinephrine 2.25% 0.25–0.5 mL QID, metaproterenol 5% 0.3 mL TID/QID, levalbuterol 0.63–1.25 mg TID/QID, arformoterol 15 mcg BID. Terbutaline QID: one DPI puff or two MDI puffs.p. 425 · Q 10, 12, 13, 14, 15
  5. TrapRegular beta-agonist use causes receptor desensitization (surface receptor loss and cell failure); combining adrenergic agonists with corticosteroids can rarely provoke sudden paradoxical bronchospasm.p. 426 · Q 16
  6. CompareAtropine crosses the blood-brain barrier (hallucinations); poorly absorbed ipratropium (0.025% 1–2 mL; add-on, not acute rescue) and glycopyrrolate (0.02% 5 mL) limit systemic effects; tiotropium 18 mcg DPI daily.p. 427 · Q 18, 19, 20, 21

Xanthines, Corticosteroids, and Ventilator MDI Delivery

  1. ConceptProposed theophylline mechanisms include phosphodiesterase inhibition (↑ cAMP), adenosine antagonism and increased catecholamine release. In CO₂ retainers, xanthines increase central CO₂ sensitivity and diaphragmatic contractility.p. 428 · Q 22, 23
  2. NumberTheophylline therapeutic range is 5–15 mcg/mL; 20–30 mcg/mL causes mild–moderate toxicity; >40 mcg/mL causes severe toxicity (gastric bleeding, arrhythmias, seizures); IV aminophylline is 78–86% theophylline.p. 428 · Q 24, 26
  3. RuleHeart failure or liver disease slows theophylline clearance → toxicity; smoking ↑ clearance → higher doses; closely monitor paralyzed patients' levels because early GI signs are hidden.p. 428 · Q 25
  4. TrapCorticosteroids lack direct bronchodilator action. Onset takes 2–24 hours, precluding sole acute asthma therapy. Rinse and gargle after each inhaled dose. Abrupt withdrawal after prolonged systemic therapy risks adrenal insufficiency.p. 430 · Q 27, 28, 29
  5. RuleVentilator MDI delivery improves with a spacer, flow-onset actuation (1–1.5 sec delay ↓ delivery), Vt ≥500 mL, longer Ti, and slower flow; end-inspiratory pause does not help.p. 430 · Q 31, 32

Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

  1. TrapParalysis can improve synchrony and chest wall compliance. Provide sedation and opioid analgesia. Apnea is immediately life-threatening. During ventilation activate apnea, low-pressure/disconnect, low exhaled Vt/VE, high/low HR and low SpO₂ alarms.p. 436 · Q 33, 34, 52
  2. CompareDepolarizing agents (succinylcholine) sustain depolarization with fasciculations, have no antidote, and clear via plasma pseudocholinesterase; nondepolarizing agents compete with acetylcholine and reverse with anticholinesterases (neostigmine, pyridostigmine).p. 433 · Q 35, 36, 37, 39, 40
  3. NumberEyes/digits weaken before diaphragm. Initial doses (mg/kg) and onset (minutes): succinylcholine 0.3–1.1/1, rocuronium 0.3/3–4, vecuronium 0.05/4–6, pancuronium 0.07/5–7. Succinylcholine’s rapid onset suits emergency intubation.p. 434 · Q 38, 43, 44, 45
  4. RulePrefer atracurium in organ failure. High aminoglycoside concentrations reduce acetylcholine release, potentiating competitive blockade. Avoid corticosteroids during prolonged blockade with any agent, especially steroidal blockers such as vecuronium and pancuronium.p. 434 · Q 41, 42, 46
  5. Rule↓ Ca++ or ↑ Mg++ enhances nondepolarizing block (↑ Mg++ enhances depolarizing too); hypokalemia ↑ nondepolarizing but ↓ depolarizing; acidemia intensifies blockade, whereas alkalemia diminishes it.p. 435 · Q 47, 48, 49, 50, 51
  6. TrapSuccinylcholine and atracurium can release histamine, causing bronchospasm and hypotension. Vecuronium is unlikely to do so. With succinylcholine or volatile anesthetics, rapidly rising exhaled CO₂ suggests malignant hyperthermia. Give dantrolene.p. 436 · Q 53, 54, 56
  7. NumberTrain-of-Four delivers four stimuli in 2 sec at 2 Hz (0.5-sec intervals) on ulnar, facial, or posterior tibial nerves; target one–two twitches (three ≈80% block).p. 437 · Q 55, 57
  8. NumberReversal: sustained head lift/tongue protrusion (>5 seconds), handgrip, PaO₂ >80 and PaCO₂ <45 mm Hg (slightly higher acceptable with chronic CO₂ retention), MIP ≤−25 cm H₂O, VC >900 mL.p. 438 · Q 58, 59

  1. ConceptBenzodiazepines enhance GABA-regulated chloride channels to hyperpolarize neurons; prefer IV in unstable patients (midazolam is most lipophilic); hepatic metabolites excrete mainly in urine.p. 440 · Q 62, 63, 64, 65

  3. NumberBenzodiazepine IV sedation: intermittent bolus for short sedation/procedures, bolus plus continuous infusion for prolonged use; initial doses: diazepam 2.5–10 mg, lorazepam 0.5–2 mg, midazolam 1–5 mg.p. 442 · Q 68, 72

Opioid Analgesics and Pain Control

  1. ConceptPain causes muscle splinting (↓ Vt, ↓ RR, ↓ VE); nonverbal signs of inadequate analgesia include tachycardia without hypoxemia, BP changes, dilated pupils, diaphoresis, grimacing, restlessness, and guarding.p. 446 · Q 74, 84
  2. CompareMu activation sedates and kappa activation induces hypnosis. Both reduce pain and respiratory rate. Agonist-antagonists activate one receptor while blocking another. Sigma activation can cause delirium. Naloxone blocks all three.p. 443 · Q 73, 75, 76
  3. TrapBrainstem mu activation depresses breathing. Ventilator sighs may prevent atelectasis. Any opioid can cause chest wall rigidity, most often reported with fentanyl. Naloxone does not reverse normeperidine-associated convulsions.p. 445 · Q 77, 78, 79

Barbiturates, Propofol, Haloperidol, and Dexmedetomidine

  2. TrapBarbiturate coma ↓ cerebral oxygen consumption → ↓ cerebral blood flow → ↓ ICP; causes venodilation hypotension, ↑ hepatic clearance of other drugs, and no analgesia (may heighten pain).p. 448 · Q 88, 89, 90

  1. ConceptEndogenous NO (from L-arginine) ↑ cGMP → vasodilation; inhaled NO selectively dilates vessels of ventilated alveoli (↓ PVR, restores V/Q, ↑ oxygenation) without systemic hypotension because hemoglobin inactivates it.p. 453 · Q 104, 105, 110
  2. NumberNO reacts with O₂ to form NO₂. Above 10 ppm, NO₂ can cause hemorrhage, pulmonary edema and death. Nitric/nitrous acids risk pneumonitis. Cylinders: 800/2,200 ppm NO in nitrogen. INOvent monitors NO/NO₂/O₂.p. 452 · Q 106, 108, 109, 111
  3. NumberNO oxidizes hemoglobin to methemoglobin (worse in methemoglobin reductase deficiency): 10–20% asymptomatic central cyanosis, 20–45% CNS depression, 45–55% coma/arrhythmia/shock/convulsion, >70% high risk of death.p. 454 · Q 107, 112, 113

Critical Care Issues in Mechanical Ventilation

31 must-knows · 82 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineThis chapter covers four major ICU conditions closely tied to mechanical ventilation: ALI/ARDS, ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), and traumatic brain injury (TBI). Across all four, management centers on supporting gas exchange while preventing secondary injury—barotrauma and volutrauma in ARDS, nosocomial infection in VAP, and cerebral ischemia in HIE and TBI. The most testable focus is mastering exact numerical thresholds and protocols: ARDS lung-protective limits and decremental PEEP titration, VAP CPIS and prevention rules, CPP = MAP − ICP (70–80 mm Hg), and TBI GCS and PaCO2 ≥26 mm Hg limits.

ALI and ARDS: Classification and Pathophysiology

  1. ConceptRepeated reopening injures noncompliant units, while compliant units overdistend. In one study, 30 cm H2O peak transalveolar pressure produced 140 cm H2O stress at the normal–collapsed boundary.p. 491 · Q 1, 2
  2. CompareBoth require acute onset, bilateral frontal-film infiltrates, and PCWP ≤18 mm Hg if measured or no clinical evidence of left atrial hypertension. PaO2/FIO2 thresholds at any PEEP: ALI ≤300, ARDS ≤200 mm Hg.p. 491 · Q 3, 4, 5
  3. CompareDirect epithelial insults such as pneumonia, aspiration, toxin inhalation, near drowning, contusion, and fat embolism can fill alveoli. Indirect insults such as sepsis, severe trauma, and acute pancreatitis cause interstitial edema.p. 492 · Q 6, 7, 8
  4. ConceptARDS lowers PaO2/FIO2 and compliance while deadspace ventilation rises. Most develop diffuse infiltrates and respiratory failure within 48 hours of symptoms. Normal PCWP without cardiomegaly or effusions favors ARDS.p. 493 · Q 9, 10, 11, 12

Lung-Protective Ventilation and ARDSNet

  1. NumberGeneral lung-protection ceilings to reduce barotrauma: PIP <50 cm H2O, PPLAT <35 cm H2O, mPaw <30 cm H2O, and PEEP <10 cm H2O (adjust for coexisting conditions).p. 494 · Q 13
  2. RuleLow VT reduces airway pressure, increases deadspace ventilation, and decreases alveolar ventilation, risking hypercapnia, acidosis, and atelectasis. For COPD with air trapping, decrease VT and increase peak inspiratory flow.p. 494 · Q 14, 15
  3. RuleDuring volume-controlled ventilation, permissive hypercapnia uses VT 4–7 mL/kg and allows PaCO2 >50 mm Hg. Titrate VT until PIP approaches baseline plateau pressure. Chang prefers tromethamine over bicarbonate.p. 494 · Q 16, 17, 18
  4. Concept↑ PEEP → ↑ pleural pressure and PVR → ↓ LV compliance, venous return, cardiac output, and O2 delivery → renal insufficiency, ↓ urine output, and Na+/water retention.p. 495 · Q 19
  5. NumberInitial ARDSNet VC-A/C: PPLAT <30 cm H2O, VT down to 4 mL/kg PBW, SpO2 88–95%. FIO2 30/40/50/60/70/80/90/100% pairs respectively with PEEP 5/5–8/8–10/10/10–14/14/16–18/20–24 cm H2O. Individualize PEEP after stabilization.p. 495 · Q 20, 21, 22

  1. RuleConsider recruitment for ALI/ARDS with severe pulmonary edema and high risk of death from refractory hypoxemia. Contraindications include existing barotrauma, hemodynamic compromise, radiographic blebs or bullae, and elevated ICP.p. 496 · Q 23
  2. RuleSedate until spontaneous breathing ceases. Give 100% O2 for 20 minutes, then CPAP 40 cm H2O ≤40 seconds. Stop for SpO2 <88%, HR >140/<60, MAP <60 mmHg/>20% deviation, or arrhythmia.p. 497 · Q 26, 27, 28
  3. RulePCV 15 above PEEP 20 cmH2O. Repeat recruitment ≤3× if PaO2 rises <20%. Reduce FIO2 by 5–20% to SpO2 90–94%, then PEEP 2 cmH2O/15–20 minutes. After final recruitment, use pre-desaturation (<90%) PEEP.p. 497 · Q 29, 30, 31
  4. RuleProne positioning (15–30° Trendelenburg) puts lower lobes uppermost, ↓ opening pressure and transpulmonary gradient; indicated when supine SpO2 ≥90% needs PEEP >10 cm H2O and FIO2 ≥60%.p. 496 · Q 24, 25

Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia: Diagnosis and Microbes

  1. NumberVAP affects 10–25% of ventilated patients, with 33–50% mortality. Incidence: 3% first five days, 2%/day next five, then 1%/day. Susceptibility increases with malnutrition, immunosuppression, and depressed consciousness.p. 498 · Q 32, 33, 34
  2. CompareNew/progressive infiltrates plus two findings among fever, leukocytosis/leukopenia, and purulent secretions: 69% sensitivity, 75% specificity. Predominant microbes after intubation: upper-airway flora ≤48 hours, gram-negative bacilli days 3–7, S. aureus/MRSA thereafter.p. 498 · Q 35, 36
  3. NumberBaseline CPIS >6 suggests VAP. Scoring findings include abundant purulent secretions, localized infiltrate, temperature <36/>39°C (38.5–38.9°C scores 1), WBC outside 4–11 thousand/mm³ with >500 bands, and PaO2/FIO2 <240 without ARDS.p. 499 · Q 37, 38, 39, 40, 41

  1. RuleVAP prevention priorities: frequent hand washing (simplest, most cost-effective), HOB 30–45° (avoid in severe hypotension or postneurosurgical), NIV mask when possible, and 6–8 hr daily sedation vacation for early weaning.p. 499 · Q 45, 46, 48
  2. TrapChange soiled or malfunctioning circuits. One bundle changed circuits weekly and HME filters/inline suction devices daily. Oral feeding tubes avoid nasal-tube-associated sinusitis risk. A dorsal lumen drains secretions above the cuff.p. 500 · Q 43, 44, 47, 49

  1. ConceptAbsent cerebral circulation depletes neuronal O2 within 20 seconds. Complete anoxia depletes glucose/ATP within five minutes. Na+/K+ pump failure triggers depolarization, glutamate release, and Ca²⁺ influx. Na+/Cl− influx causes osmotic edema.p. 502 · Q 52, 53, 54, 55
  2. FormulaCPP = MAP − ICP. Chang gives 70–80 mm Hg as normal. Mortality rises ~20% per 10-mmHg decrease. Severe head-injury studies reported 35% lower mortality with CPP maintained >70 mm Hg.p. 503 · Q 56, 58, 67
  3. RuleShock ↓ MAP → ↓ CPP (sepsis causes vasodilation/relative hypovolemia); without hemorrhage, restore fluid balance first, then vasopressor (norepinephrine or dopamine); avoid systemic hypotension (SBP <90 mm Hg).p. 504 · Q 60, 61

  3. CompareCloudy consciousness impairs short-term memory. Lethargic patients remain aware. Obtunded patients briefly rouse but lack awareness. Stupor permits arousal by pain. Coma resists vigorous stimulation. Brain death entails irreversible whole-brain cessation.p. 507 · Q 73, 74

  6. NumberGCS eye opening scores 4 if spontaneous, 3 to verbal command (to a shout if under one year), 2 to pain, and 1 for no response.p. 508 · Q 76
  7. CompareFour verbal GCS points correspond to disoriented conversation in an adult, inappropriate words in a three-year-old, and crying in a one-year-old. Appropriate crying in a one-year-old scores five.p. 508 · Q 79

Weaning from Mechanical Ventilation

31 must-knows · 87 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineWeaning transfers the work of breathing from the ventilator back to the patient, guided by objective ventilatory, oxygenation, pulmonary reserve, and mechanics criteria rather than any single guarantee. The single most testable concept is screening spontaneous breathing with the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI <100 breaths/min/L) and confirming extubation readiness via a 30-minute spontaneous breathing trial while avoiding SIMV as a stand-alone weaning mode. If weaning fails, return the patient to full support to rest and correct the underlying increased airflow resistance, decreased compliance, or respiratory muscle fatigue.

Weaning Definitions & Readiness

  1. RuleWeaning transfers breathing work to the patient. After brief ventilation, usually 1–2 days or less, recovered cardiopulmonary function may permit abrupt withdrawal. Longer ventilation generally favors individualized, gradual withdrawal.p. 517 · Q 1, 2
  2. CompareWeaning success: no ventilatory support 48 hours post-extubation; weaning in progress: extubated on NIV; weaning failure: failed SBT or reintubation ≤48 hours. Medical patients take longer than surgical patients.p. 518 · Q 3, 4, 5, 7
  3. RuleAssess significant recovery from the acute phase, ability to sustain spontaneous breathing, cough, secretions, and hemodynamic stability. Correct conditions that impair breathing according to their severity.p. 519 · Q 8, 9, 10

Ventilatory Weaning Criteria

  1. NumberPaCO2 weaning criteria: 35–45 mm Hg and pH 7.35–7.45 for normal lungs (<50 mm Hg with compensated pH non-COPD); COPD may wean at baseline (~50 mm Hg, pH ~7.35).p. 521 · Q 11, 13, 16
  2. NumberAfter full support, observe 3 minutes of spontaneous breathing before measuring VC, VT, and frequency. Favorable values are VC >10 mL/kg, VT >5 mL/kg, f <35/min. Poor effort lowers VC.p. 521 · Q 11, 14, 15
  3. RuleVE <10 L/min with satisfactory gases favors weaning. Needing >10 L/min to normalize PaCO2 suggests excessive breathing work. Increased CO2 production, alveolar deadspace, or metabolic acidosis can raise VE requirements.p. 522 · Q 17, 18, 19, 23

Oxygenation Weaning Criteria

  1. NumberOn FIO2 ≤0.40, PaO2 >60 mm Hg or SaO2 >90% supports readiness. A PaO2/FIO2 ratio ≥150 mm Hg suggests acceptable physiologic shunt.p. 522 · Q 12, 22, 24, 25
  2. TrapIn anemia or carboxyhemoglobinemia, PaO2 and SpO2 miss true oxygenation—use CaO2 and SaO2; keep pulse oximetry SpO2 in the mid-90s because critical-care SpO2 is accurate within 2%–4% of SaO2.p. 522 · Q 20, 21
  3. FormulaQS/QT fraction = (CcO2−CaO2)/(CcO2−CvO2). Multiply by 100 for percent. ≤10% is normal, 10–20% mild, 20–30% significant, and >30% severe. Correct shunt >20% before weaning.p. 523 · Q 26, 27
  4. FormulaP(A−a)O2 = PAO2−PaO2. On room air, expect <4 mm Hg per decade of age. On 100% O2, each 50 mm Hg approximates 2% shunt. <350 mm Hg favors weaning.p. 523 · Q 28, 29, 30, 31, 32
  5. NumberWeaning PaO2 at FIO2 ≤0.4: >60 mm Hg without PEEP, >100 mm Hg with PEEP <8 cm H2O; SaO2 >90%.p. 520 · Q 12

Pulmonary Reserve, Mechanics & RSBI

  1. NumberVC and MIP require effort. Favorable values are VC >10 mL/kg and MIP −30 cm H2O or more negative during 20-second occlusion. Mean MIP was −37 weaned versus −30 nonweaned.p. 524 · Q 33, 34, 35, 36
  2. FormulaStatic compliance = corrected VT/(PPLAT−PEEP), in mL/cm H2O. Measurement is effort-independent. Chang gives ≥30 mL/cm H2O as consistent with successful weaning. Lower compliance increases breathing work.p. 525 · Q 37, 40
  3. FormulaAirway resistance (effort-independent) = (PIP − PPLAT) / constant inspiratory flow (cm H2O/L/sec); normal 0.6–2.4 cm H2O/L/sec (higher intubated). Reduce via unkinking ET tube, suctioning, and bronchodilators.p. 525 · Q 38
  4. FormulaVD/VT fraction = (PaCO2−PECO2)/PaCO2, using mixed expired PECO2. Multiply by 100 for percent. Higher VD/VT increases minute ventilation demand. A value ≤60% favors weaning.p. 526 · Q 41, 44
  5. FormulaAfter 3 minutes of unassisted breathing or once breathing stabilizes, RSBI = f/VT, with VT = VE/f in liters. <100 breaths/min/L favors success, >100 favors failure. Rapid shallow breathing increases VD/VT.p. 522 · Q 42, 43

Weaning Procedures & Protocol

  1. RuleSBT is the main extubation-readiness test: ≤30 min via T-tube, CPAP, ATC, or PS ≤8 cm H2O (≤10 pediatric). Pass: normal breathing, adequate gas exchange, stable hemodynamics (13% reintubated).p. 527 · Q 45, 46, 48, 50, 6
  2. NumberSBT failure indicators (changes from baseline): FIO2≥0.50 with PaO2≤60 or SaO2<90%; PaCO2>50 or ↑>8; pH<7.32 or ↓≥0.07; f>35 or ↑≥50%; RSBI>100; HR>140 or ↑≥20%; SBP>180, <90, or ↑≥20%; arrhythmias.p. 529 · Q 57, 58, 59, 60
  3. RulePSV starts 5–15 cm H2O (max 40) for VT 10–15 mL/kg or f ≤25/min, decreasing 3–6 toward 5 cm H2O; give full nighttime support; ATC offsets artificial airway resistance.p. 528 · Q 39, 52, 53, 54, 55
  4. TrapDo not use SIMV as a stand-alone weaning mode (partial support synchronized to inspiratory effort); reduce rate 1–3/min to 2–4/min by tolerance, checking ABG/SpO2 after ≥30 min (pH >7.30–7.35).p. 527 · Q 47, 51, 56
  5. CompareVS adjusts PS for target VT. VAPS combines PSV and volume-assisted cycles. MMV adjusts frequency for target VE. APRV alternates pressures (e.g., 10 cm H2O, usually releasing to zero). Zero releases/min yields CPAP.p. 530 · Q 61, 62, 63, 65
  6. RuleExample protocol requires improving cause, inspiratory effort, stable hemodynamics, light sedation or better, P/F>150, PEEP<8, pH≥7.25. If RSBI<100, try SBT up to 30 minutes. Stop for intolerance, including f>35/min for 5 minutes.p. 531 · Q 64, 66, 67, 68
  7. RuleReassess throughout weaning. If the patient tolerates reduced support and criteria remain acceptable, reduce support again. Use trends to adjust ventilator settings and treatment.p. 536 · Q 87

Signs & Causes of Weaning Failure

  1. TrapEarly failure signs: tachypnea, accessory muscle use, paradoxical abdominal movement. Never reduce support solely for hyperventilation PaCO2 (hypoxia, pain, anxiety); on failure, return to full support, rest, and reassess.p. 532 · Q 69, 70
  2. NumberWarning trends include rising PaCO2 (>50 mm Hg), falling pH (<7.30), PaO2 (<60 mm Hg), SpO2 (<90%), or P/F (<150). BP changes, rising heart rate, and arrhythmias also warn.p. 532 · Q 71, 72
  3. NumberWarning trends include falling VT (<250 mL), rising f (>30/min), rising RSBI (>100 breaths/min/L), weakening inspiratory effort, falling static compliance (<30 mL/cm H2O), and rising VD/VT (>60%).p. 524 · Q 73, 74
  4. CompareFailure causes include increased resistance, decreased compliance, and muscle fatigue. Use an ≥8-mm ET tube when patient size permits. ARDS lowers static compliance. Bronchospasm and tube kinking lower dynamic compliance.p. 533 · Q 75, 76, 80
  5. FormulaWork per breath = transpulmonary pressure × VT. In ventilator-dependent patients, work rates ≤1.6 kg·m/min favor weaning; ≥1.7 kg·m/min correlates with failure. Prolonged full support and disuse can cause diaphragmatic atrophy.p. 534 · Q 77, 78, 79
  6. Rule↑ CO2 production (↑ minute ventilation need): extensive burns, fever, and overfeeding, especially excess carbohydrate.p. 522 · Q 18

Terminal Weaning

  1. CompareTerminal weaning withdraws ventilation and results in death. Withholding means never starting ventilation. Discuss the informed request over time, medical futility, relief of suffering, and fear or distress.p. 535 · Q 81, 82, 83
  2. RuleBefore terminal withdrawal, let uncomfortable caregivers withdraw, notify chaplain, remove invasive monitors/alarms/extra lines, keeping basic vitals, O2, and IV access. Invite family to stay; titrate analgesics/sedatives for comfort.p. 536 · Q 84, 85, 86

Neonatal Mechanical Ventilation

31 must-knows · 87 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineNeonatal mechanical ventilation requires specialized airway sizing, surfactant replacement, pressure-targeted ventilation, and low-compliance circuits to support immature lungs without causing barotrauma or retinopathy of prematurity. When conventional ventilation fails, management escalates to high frequency ventilation (HFPPV, HFJV, or HFOV) and, for reversible lung disease with ≥80% predicted mortality, ECMO. The single most testable principle is how each mode controls gas exchange and avoids lung injury—especially lowering PIP after surfactant compliance surges and remembering that HFOV lowers PaCO2 by increasing amplitude (∆P) or decreasing frequency (Hz).

  2. NumberMiller blades: 1 term, 0 preemie, 00 micropreemie. ETT ID (mm): 2.5 (<1,000 g/<28 wk), 3.0 (1,000–2,000 g/28–34 wk), 3.5 (2,000–3,000 g/34–38 wk), 3.5–4.0 (>3,000 g/>38 wk).p. 547 · Q 4, 5

  1. ConceptSurfactant deficiency raises alveolar surface tension and breathing effort in RDS. DPPC (~60% of phosphatidylcholine) lowers surface tension. Replacement reduces RDS mortality and may lessen PIE.p. 548 · Q 10, 11, 21
  2. RuleProphylactic tracheal surfactant within 15 minutes: PaO2/PAO2 <0.22 with ≤26 weeks/<1,250 g or <30 weeks/500–1,000 g. Rescue: clinical RDS, rising oxygen/ventilatory needs, bilateral ground-glass CXR, usually <34 weeks.p. 549 · Q 13, 14, 15
  3. NumberDoses (mL/kg): Surfaxin 5.8 and Survanta 4, ≥6h apart, maximum four/48h. Infasurf 3 every 6h, maximum four. Curosurf 2.5 initially, then 1.25 every 12h up to twice; halves 30s apart.p. 550 · Q 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

  4. NumberAcceptable UAC ABG: PaO2 >50 mm Hg, PaCO2 35–45 mm Hg, pH 7.3–7.45 (higher PaCO2 in CLD/PIE); capillary PO2 is unreliable for oxygenation (use SpO2 or transcutaneous PO2).p. 558 · Q 41, 42
  5. NumberInitial neonatal ventilation (either compliance group): FIO2 to keep infant pink with SpO2 90%–95%, with appropriate PEEP if needed.p. 557 · Q 39

High Frequency Ventilation: HFPPV and HFJV

  1. CompareHFV can ventilate below dead-space VT at low pressures. HFPPV uses VT above dead space and convection. Frequencies: HFPPV 1–2.5, HFJV 4–11, HFOV 8–30 Hz. One Hz equals 60 cycles/min.p. 559 · Q 43, 44
  2. FormulamPaw = [(f×I time/60) × (PIP−PEEP)] + PEEP (40×0.5/60 × [22−4] + 4 = 10 cm H2O); HFPPV uses convection; ↑ f → ↑ mPaw (barotrauma, cardiac compromise, ICH).p. 560 · Q 45, 46, 48
  3. RuleHFJV pulses gas inside the ETT (special adaptor/tube) for air leaks (PIE), hypoplasia, restrictive disease, or PPHN; tandem conventional ventilator supplies sighs (surfactant/atelectasis), PEEP, and entrainment flow.p. 561 · Q 47, 49
  4. TrapHFJV principal hazard: necrotizing tracheobronchitis (use internal jet catheter/triple-lumen ETT). ↓ chest vibration + ↑ PaCO2 with stable PaO2 = ETT obstruction/malposition; with ↓ PaO2 = ↓ compliance/pneumothorax (transilluminate).p. 561 · Q 50, 51

High Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation (HFOV)

  1. ConceptHFOV piston assists both inspiration and expiration (no tandem ventilator; pendelluft enhances diffusion). High PEEP + hyperinflation → ↓ CO, ↑ barotrauma; deterioration shows pallor, cyanosis, bradycardia, hypotension.p. 561 · Q 52, 53, 60, 61
  2. RuleConsider HFOV: <1,000 g with FiO2 >50%, rate >30/min, PIP >20 cm H2O; rapidly rising FiO2 without pneumothorax and OI >10; pulmonary hypertension with nitric-oxide candidacy and OI ≥15.p. 563 · Q 54, 55, 56
  3. NumberInitial HFOV mPaw: diffuse disease 3–4 cm H2O above conventional mPaw; nonhomogeneous disease/air leak at or below conventional. Bias flow 10–15 L/min below 2,000 g, 20 above. Frequency 8–15 Hz.p. 564 · Q 57, 58, 59
  4. NumberHFOV profiles: preterm diffuse +1–2 mPaw (10–15 Hz), term +2–4 (8–10); MAS air trapping = CV (6–10); preterm PIE minimal wiggle, −1 (10–15); uniform hypoplasia = CV (10–15).p. 565 · Q 62, 63, 64, 65
  5. TrapHFOV: increasing amplitude or decreasing Hz raises VT and lowers PaCO2. Readjust mPaw after power changes. Wean mPaw every 6h, more often for persistent expansion beyond nine posterior ribs. Ti 33%=1:2.p. 566 · Q 66, 67, 68

Dual Control Ventilation

  1. CompareDual control merges VCV/PCV: Machine Volume sets PIP within-breath (proximal flow sensor); Volume Guarantee adjusts next-breath PIP from preceding expired VT (lung mechanics limit I time in PSV).p. 567 · Q 69, 70, 71, 72
  2. ConceptLiquid ventilation uses perfluorocarbons to inflate the lungs. These liquids carry more oxygen and remove carbon dioxide more easily than gas. Chang describes neonatal applications as investigational.p. 568 · Q 73

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)

  1. RuleECMO: ≥80% predicted mortality with conventional care, reversible lung disease, no significant cardiac disease. Exclude gestation <34 weeks, weight <2,000 g, existing ICH, or ventilation >2 weeks. Correct coagulopathy first.p. 569 · Q 74, 75, 77, 78
  2. FormulaECMO alternatives: on 100% O2, PB−47−PaCO2−PaO2 605–620 mmHg for 4–12h; (mPaw×fractional FiO2)/PaO2 0.35–0.6 for 30min–6h; PaO2 35–50 mmHg for 2–12h; or pH <7.25 for 2h with hypotension.p. 569 · Q 79, 80, 81
  3. CompareVA ECMO: right atrium (internal jugular) → aortic arch (right carotid), supports oxygenation + heart; VV ECMO: right atrium → right atrium (femoral vein), oxygenation only (roller pump circuit).p. 570 · Q 82, 83, 84
  4. NumberReported ECMO findings: ICH 14% in one study, mechanical complications ~10%, hypertension ~7%, positive blood cultures ~6%. Oxygenator consumption can cause cytopenias. One comparison averaged 25 hospital days versus 76 conventionally.p. 571 · Q 85, 86, 87
CH 18

Mechanical Ventilation in Nontraditional Settings

38 must-knows · 112 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineChapter 18 covers mechanical ventilation outside the ICU: home mechanical ventilation, mass casualty triage and allocation, hyperbaric chambers, hypobaric flight, and air travel with portable ventilators and POCs. Outside hospitals, clinicians work with limited monitors and support and must adapt settings to changing environments. The single most testable principle is Boyle's law under changing ambient pressure: hyperbaric pressure compresses gas (lowering VCV tidal volume and favoring PCV), whereas hypobaric ascent expands gas (raising tidal volume and peak flow, requiring pressure compensation or a 3% per 1,000 ft adjustment).

Home Mechanical Ventilation (HMV)

  1. RuleHMV goals: extend life, enhance quality of life and rehabilitation in a familiar home environment, strengthen physical/physiological function, and reduce cost (secondary to quality care).p. 582 · Q 3
  2. RuleHMV requires clinical stability, persistent ventilatory failure/inability to wean, screening for noninvasive alternatives (diaphragm pacing, pneumobelt), and caregivers willing and able to manage patient, airway, and ventilator.p. 583 · Q 4, 5, 7
  3. RuleHMV contraindications: unstable medical conditions needing complicated procedures or specialized personnel (e.g., progressive arrhythmias), severe desaturation unresponsive to low–moderate FIO2/PEEP, patient unwillingness, or insufficient financial/human resources.p. 583 · Q 6, 8
  4. NumberCOPD: airflow obstruction and air trapping prolong weaning; acute-on-chronic failure shows pH 7.27, PaCO2 74, PaO2 43 mm Hg, HCO3- 33 mEq/L; correct SaO2/SpO2 < 90% before transfer.p. 585 · Q 9, 10, 11, 14
  5. CompareHMV groups: restrictive disease (↓ volumes → rapid shallow breathing → ↑ deadspace; low compliance → ↑ WOB), muscle dysfunction (healthy lungs; prolonged support if C1–C2 injury), central hypoventilation (autonomic failure).p. 585 · Q 12, 13, 15, 18
  6. RuleNonmedical HMV selection: patient/family desires, cost (≤ hospital), and physical/technical/emotional resources; discuss discharge only when patient comprehends (not hypoxic/confused/distressed); family commitment and communication drive adaptation.p. 586 · Q 16, 17, 19, 20
  7. RuleHome ventilator selection prioritizes immediate/long-term needs; a backup ventilator may be necessary if totally dependent (part-time users may not need one); ventilators should have high-pressure, low-pressure, and ventilator failure alarms.p. 588 · Q 21, 22, 26, 27
  8. ComparePPV alternatives: negative-pressure iron lung, chest cuirass, and airtight wrap (larger VT than cuirass, needs help donning); pneumobelt pushes diaphragm upward; rocking bed; diaphragmatic pacing stimulates thoracic phrenic nerves.p. 588 · Q 2, 23, 24, 25

Mass Casualty Incidents and Triage

  1. ConceptMass casualty exceeds timely regional response; ventilatory failure follows primary blast lung injury, pandemics causing ALI/ARDS, or nerve agents (acetylcholinesterase inhibition → acetylcholine surge → seizures, flaccid paralysis, apnea).p. 589 · Q 28, 29, 30, 31, 32
  2. NumberU.S. pandemic projections (moderate vs severe): 90 million ill (50% outpatient), 865,000 vs 9,900,000 hospitalized, 128,750 vs 1,485,000 ICU, and 64,875 vs 742,500 needing mechanical ventilation.p. 590 · Q 35, 36, 37
  3. NumberCDC Strategic National Stockpile holds ~10,000 ventilators (replacing LP-10 and Uni-Vent Eagle 754 with LTV 1200 and Newport HT50); con is clinician unfamiliarity; DHS HSEEP guides preparedness exercises.p. 594 · Q 44, 51, 56
  4. RuleModern triage prioritizes severely ill patients most likely to survive; prehospital START (pediatric: JumpSTART) uses RPM (respiration, perfusion—radial pulse replacing capillary refill—mental status) with Immediate > Delayed > Minor.p. 591 · Q 33, 34, 38
  5. RulePrehospital SALT sorts still/life-threat 1st, purposeful movers 2nd, walkers 3rd; immediate LSI includes hemorrhage control, airway opening (consider 2 rescue breaths if child), chest decompression, and auto-injector antidotes.p. 592 · Q 39, 41, 43
  6. NumberHospital SOFA assesses 6 organ systems (sepsis/hypoperfusion) to predict ICU outcomes: ≤ 7 = intermediate critical care, 8–10 = highest critical-care priority, ≥ 11 = palliation only (95% mortality).p. 593 · Q 40, 42
  7. NumberSOFA score each organ by the worst category met. Score 3 = PaO2/FIO2 ≤200, platelets ≤50 ×10³/μL, bilirubin 6.0–11.9, GCS 6–9. Score 4 = P/F ≤100, platelets ≤20, urine <200 mL/day.p. 594 · Q 45, 47, 48, 49, 50
  8. RuleNYS DOH exclusions: cardiac arrest (unwitnessed/recurrent/trauma), metastatic cancer with poor prognosis, burn BSA >40%/severe inhalation injury, end-stage organ failure (NYHA III–IV, FEV1 <25%, MELD >20, dialysis, severe neuro).p. 595 · Q 52, 53, 54, 55

Mechanical Ventilation in Hyperbaric Conditions

  1. Formula100% O2: PAO2 = (760−47)×1.0−40 = 673 mm Hg; dissolved O2 = PAO2×0.003 ≈ 2 vol% (1 ATA) and 3×2 = 6 vol% (3 ATA).p. 597 · Q 58, 59
  2. NumberAt FIO2 0.21, normal O2 content is 20 vol% (plasma 0.3 vol%); tissues need ≥ 60 mL/L (6 vol%), met by dissolved O2 at 3 ATA.p. 596 · Q 57, 58
  3. Rule33 ft underwater adds 1 ATA (total 1,520 mm Hg); treat decompression sickness (rapid diving ascent or altitude >18,000 ft) with rapid recompression followed by gradual decompression.p. 597 · Q 60, 61
  4. RuleCO poisoning: treat mild/moderate with 100% O2 by mask until COHb < 5%; severe poisoning requires HBO; HBO also adjuncts antibiotics in clostridial myonecrosis/gas gangrene by hindering anaerobic growth.p. 597 · Q 62, 63
  5. TrapIn HBO, fill the ETT cuff with water (not air) to prevent decompression overdistension/rupture; neuromuscular blockers have a shorter half-life → monitor closely and give lower doses more frequently.p. 597 · Q 64, 65
  6. ComparePCV gives stable VT in HBO; under VCV, gas compression requires ↑ set VT (550–710 mL at 1.3–2.8 ATA delivers 500 mL); monitor expired VT via Wright respirometer.p. 598 · Q 66, 69, 73, 74
  7. NumberMultiplace HBO vents: Eagle 754 and Omni-Vent Series D (VCV only, 6 ATA); PLV-100 and Servo 900 C (VCV/PCV, 6 ATA); EVITA 4 (VCV/PCV/CPAP/PSV, 2.8 ATA); Bird Avian (2.5 ATA).p. 598 · Q 67, 68
  8. RuleTest all electrical devices before hyperbaric use (voltaic arc/fire risk); invasive pressures (BP, CVP, PAP, PCWP) are safe when the calibrated, fluid-filled transducer is exposed to chamber pressure.p. 599 · Q 70, 71
  9. TrapHBO monitoring: pulse ox for pulse rate only; manual BP cuff; thermistor (no mercury); sidestream PETCO2 outside chamber (mainstream reads falsely high); Ohmeda 5420 monitors airway pressure + exhaled VT.p. 600 · Q 75, 76, 77, 78
  10. RuleDefibrillation: not in monoplace; multiplace needs air-compressed chamber (FIO2 <21.5%), outside defibrillator, large low-resistance cable, gel plates, 3-person team (deluge); most implanted pacemakers safe <3 atm.p. 601 · Q 72, 79, 80, 81, 82

Mechanical Ventilation in Hypobaric Conditions

  1. NumberCommercial cabins pressurize to 5,000–8,000 ft (PB 564 mm Hg, PAO2 59 mm Hg at 8,000 ft); FAA requires pilot O2 >12,500 ft (>30 min) and continuously >14,500 ft.p. 602 · Q 84, 90, 91
  2. RuleAMS (headache + GI/insomnia/dizziness/fatigue) and HACE (cerebral vasodilation, overperfusion, inadequate CSF buffering): treat with descent and O2; use acetazolamide/dexamethasone if unavailable, or portable hyperbaric chamber if persistent.p. 602 · Q 85, 86, 87
  3. RuleHAPE (leading high-altitude fatality): noncardiogenic edema with pulmonary hypertension and ↑ capillary pressure (pink/bloody sputum, tachycardia, tachypnea); treat with descent, O2, portable hyperbaric chamber, or nifedipine if unavailable.p. 602 · Q 88, 89
  4. NumberTolerated SpO2: 95–100% sea level, 88–93% at 10,000 ft (text says 88–92%), 83–88% 13,000, 75–80% 16,000, 70–75% 20,000; give O2 when SpO2 is 10% below home-altitude normal.p. 603 · Q 92, 93, 94
  5. Trap↓ PB at altitude expands gas → ↑ measured VT (512–647 mL for 500 mL set, 2,500–10,000 ft) and ↑ peak flow (1,158–1,554 mL/s) → hyperinflation/volutrauma, while displayed VT reads falsely low.p. 603 · Q 95, 98, 99
  6. ComparePressure-compensated ventilators tend to deliver stable VT, flow, airway pressure, and VE (better suited for long-distance high-altitude travel); non-compensated units need recalibration (manual ventilation meanwhile) or manual VT/rate adjustment.p. 604 · Q 1, 83, 96, 97

Air Travel with Portable Ventilators and POCs

  1. RuleTravel ventilator priority: required modes/settings; supply MD approval, specs, and manufacturer contact (distinct from O2); bring extra batteries (power not guaranteed); carry on intermittent/BiPAP units, never check.p. 605 · Q 100, 101, 102, 103
  2. NumberPortable vents: LTV 800 (>5 kg, VT 50–2,000 mL), Eagle 754 (13 lb, f 1–150/min, 3 hr), V-Leonardo (20.8 lb, VT 100–1,600 mL, 10 hr), LP-10 (35 lb, 10–20 hr external).p. 606 · Q 104, 105, 106, 107
  3. FormulaNon-compensated ventilator rule: ↓ set VT 3% per 1,000 ft ascent (expansion) and ↑ set VT 3% per 1,000 ft descent (compression; 2,000 ft descent: 2 × 3% = +6%).p. 607 · Q 108
  4. RuleAltitude hypoxia causes hyperventilation, tachycardia, and tachypnea (not hypoventilation) and hits cardiopulmonary patients hardest; give intermittent ventilator users prophylactic O2 during spontaneous breathing to ↓ WOB and prevent ventilatory failure.p. 607 · Q 109
  5. FormulaAirlines need not supply O2/DC power; carry POC batteries ≥ 150% of one-way travel time (6 hr × 1.5 = 9 hr), fitting under seat/lap; EverGo (8.5 lb) runs 12 hr.p. 607 · Q 110, 111
  6. ConceptOutside the hospital, providers often work alone or in small teams, lack monitors and support, and must adapt ventilator settings to the changing environment.p. 608 · Q 112
CH 19

Case Studies

30 must-knows · 85 review Qs in the Chang drill
Bottom lineThis chapter applies mechanical ventilation assessment, mode selection, monitoring, and weaning across 11 clinical cases spanning obstructive disease, postoperative care, head injury, smoke inhalation, overdose, chest trauma, ARDS, and neuromuscular weakness. The single most testable idea is recognizing early ventilatory fatigue and tailoring settings and weaning indices (especially RSBI f/VT <100/min/L, MIP trends, lung-protective VT, and hemodynamic tradeoffs) to each patient's underlying pathophysiology.

Obstructive Disease: COPD and Status Asthmaticus

  1. RuleCOPD signs and NIV: orthopnea signals pulmonary congestion/CHF; sudden weight gain with dependent edema signals cor pulmonale; trial BiPAP if alert, cooperative, with intact airway defenses.p. 618 · Q 7, 3, 2
  2. NumberKeep serum theophylline at 5–15 mcg/mL to minimize toxicity; aminophylline may improve COPD diaphragmatic function and reverses severe acute asthma bronchospasm refractory to epinephrine.p. 619 · Q 8, 6, 10
  3. TrapAsthma fatigue trap: PaCO2 rising from a low level (hyperventilation) toward normal (35–45 mm Hg) signals muscle fatigue and impending failure. In COPD, ↑f + abdominal paradox + ↑PaCO2 signal the same.p. 621 · Q 11, 4, 56
  4. TrapNever give cromolyn sodium (Intal) in acute asthma—it prophylactically blocks mast cell degranulation; corticosteroids aid COPD only with reversible airway disease; hyperinflation widens rib spaces and depresses the diaphragm.p. 624 · Q 15, 14, 5, 13
  5. RuleA comprehensive COPD rehabilitation program teaches the disease process, self-care, diet, maintenance of an active lifestyle, and smoking cessation.p. 620 · Q 9

Postoperative Monitoring, Weaning Index, and Head Injury

  1. RuleVentilate chronic CO2 retainers to their baseline discharge PaCO2 (e.g., 50 mm Hg); low hematocrit causes tissue hypoxia, and transient leukocytosis can follow infection or hemorrhage.p. 626 · Q 18, 16, 17
  2. NumberPost-op monitoring normals: PETCO2 ~2 mm Hg below PaCO2; CVP 1–7 mm Hg; ET cuff pressure 27–40 cm H2O (use lower cuff pressure in hypotension).p. 626 · Q 21, 19, 20
  3. FormulaRSBI = f / VT (L); rapid shallow breaths ventilate anatomic dead space (↑f/VT); f/VT <100/min/L predicts weaning success (e.g., 16/min ÷ 0.476 L = 34/min/L).p. 627 · Q 22, 48, 55, 78, 79
  4. NumberNeurologic grading: GCS 3 = severe coma, GCS ≤8 = severe brain injury/neurologic deficit, GCS 15 rules out deficit; light-reactive pupils show adequate cerebral circulation and oxygenation.p. 628 · Q 23, 24, 67
  5. RuleHead injury ICP control: target ICP <10 mm Hg via short-term mild hyperventilation (PaCO2 34–35 mm Hg, since CO2 is a potent cerebral vasodilator) and CSF drainage.p. 630 · Q 28, 25, 29
  6. TrapHead injury PEEP trap: PEEP is contraindicated in head injuries (positive pressure transmits to the head) unless severe hypoxemia is present.p. 630 · Q 27
  7. FormulaRapid shallow breathing gives a high f/VT (f ÷ VT in L); f/VT ≤100/min/L suggests readiness to wean. Example: Guillain-Barré day 56, f 32 ÷ 0.115 L ≈ 278, highest of days 52–58.p. 649 · Q 80, 81

Smoke Inhalation and Drug Overdose

  1. RuleSmoke inhalation: give 100% O2 if spontaneously ventilating, watch for CO/cyanide poisoning and pulmonary edema, and electively intubate early in anticipation of airway edema and obstruction.p. 633 · Q 33, 30, 35, 32
  2. TrapCO poisoning trap: never rely on SpO2 (falsely high because pulse oximetry counts HbCO as oxyhemoglobin) or PaO2 alone; evaluate oxygenation with measured CaO2 and HbCO.p. 632 · Q 31
  3. NumberHbCO half-life: 280–320 min on room air (1 atm), 80–90 min on 100% O2 (1 atm), 15–23 min on HBO 100% O2 at 3 atm (~6 vol% dissolved O2).p. 634 · Q 34, 36
  4. RuleOverdose/aspiration: PETCO2 20 mm Hg supports tracheal ET tube placement; initial PEEP 5 cm H2O prevents atelectasis; ↑PIP/↑Pplat (↓compliance) plus ↑FIO2 need (shunting) precede ARDS.p. 636 · Q 38, 37, 39
  5. RuleCMV → PCV: start pressure 10 cm H2O below CMV PIP; PCV minute volume depends on pressure, TI, and compliance; permissive hypercapnia (PaCO2 80 mm Hg) reduces pressure/volume needs.p. 637 · Q 40, 41, 42

Thoracic Trauma and Hemopneumothorax

  1. CompareChest trauma signs: tension pneumothorax/hemopneumothorax shifts mediastinum to unaffected side, whereas atelectasis pulls it toward affected side; pleural blood appears white (air dark); pulmonary contusion leaves skin unbroken.p. 639 · Q 50, 44, 49
  2. ConceptHemorrhage: hypotension + tachycardia signal inadequate blood volume; ↓Hb (15.8 → 10.7 g%) sharply cuts CaO2 (20.9 → 15 vol%) despite high PaO2; monitor volume with CO and PCWP.p. 641 · Q 45, 43, 47
  3. ConceptMassive transfusion: stored blood has limited 2,3-DPG → oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve shifts left → ↓O2 unloading at tissues → tissue hypoxia.p. 642 · Q 46
  4. ComparePC-IRV (e.g., 2:1 ratio) prolongs TI and lung expansion to improve oxygenation, but ↑mean airway pressure, ↑CVP, and ↑PAP → hemodynamic instability; guide adjustments by hemodynamics too.p. 646 · Q 51, 52, 53, 12
  5. RuleWhen weaning patients requiring both high FIO2 and high PEEP, reduce FIO2 to about 40% before weaning PEEP.p. 648 · Q 54

ARDS and Lung-Protective Ventilation

  1. RuleARDS hypoxemia on 100% FIO2 → ↑PEEP (↓f if ↓PaCO2); prolonged CPAP risks respiratory muscle fatigue; Mucomyst and Pulmozyme mobilize thick secretions, and iron increases Hb O2-carrying capacity.p. 651 · Q 58, 59, 62, 57
  2. CompareCardiogenic vs ARDS pulmonary edema: PCWP >18 mm Hg indicates cardiogenic pulmonary edema; pulmonary edema with normal PCWP indicates lung parenchymal injury (e.g., ARDS).p. 652 · Q 60
  3. FormulaARDS low VT (chapter: 6–8 mL/kg IBW; equation uses ×6 mL) minimizes airway pressures; ~25% fewer deaths. IBW (kg): male 50 + 2.3(in − 60); female 45.5 + 2.3(in − 60).p. 652 · Q 61, 63, 64

Neuromuscular Disease: Myasthenia Gravis and Guillain-Barré

  1. ConceptMIP trending: less negative (−12 vs −29 cm H2O) means weaker inspiratory effort; progressively more negative (−23 → −37) shows recovery; stop CPAP/PS for marked accessory muscle use.p. 659 · Q 70, 82, 68, 71
  2. RuleMyasthenia gravis: neostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) improves muscle tone; elevated bicarbonate reflects chronic ventilatory failure; poor nutrition and aspiration pneumonia impede weaning; ganciclovir treats CMV.p. 660 · Q 73, 72, 65, 69
  3. RuleGuillain-Barré: restrictive weakness lowers FEV1 and FVC with preserved FEV1/FVC%; ventilate when VC <1,000 mL or twice predicted VT; adjust peak flow to match inspiratory demand.p. 661 · Q 75, 74, 76
  4. RuleIf long-term ventilation is anticipated (Guillain-Barré), perform tracheotomy; each week immobile → 4 weeks rehab; titrate PS to VT 10–15 mL/kg or f <25/min; T-piece trials 10–15 min q2–4 hr.p. 665 · Q 84, 85, 83, 77
  5. ConceptMyasthenia case CXR: barium aspirated into the left lower lobe; a flat right hemidiaphragm with poorly defined costophrenic angle may be caused by pleural effusion.p. 657 · Q 66
